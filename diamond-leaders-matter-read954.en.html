Profits, Prophets, Coaches, and Kings

(When) Do Leaders Matter? · Jared Diamond · 2026

中文 →

In one sentence

The man who spent a career arguing that geography, climate and the luck of the biological draw decide the fates of civilizations finally turns the lens on individuals — and asks not whether leaders matter but under what conditions they do. Compressed, his answer is this: how much mark a leader leaves depends on three things — how much room they have to decide on their own, when they arrive, and what they are actually good at; and once you apply his test of "if this person had never existed, would a contemporary have done roughly the same?", the list of truly irreplaceable leaders turns out to be startlingly short.

Where it sits

Diamond, a UCLA geography professor, wrote Guns, Germs, and Steel (on this site as Read 4a) and Collapse (Read 4b); the first in particular drew years of criticism for geographic determinism and for underplaying human agency. This roughly 288-page book (Mariner Books in the US; the Penguin UK edition is subtitled (When) Do Leaders Make a Difference?) takes its title from four arenas: business (profits), religion (prophets), sport (coaches) and politics (kings) — four testing grounds for one question.

The central claims

  • "Do leaders matter?" is the wrong question. The useful one is when, where and under what conditions they do. Reviewers have boiled his verdict down to a single word — "sometimes" — and the book earns its keep by breaking "sometimes" into conditions you can actually check.
  • The conditions fall into three groups: context, timing and ability. The most important piece of context is discretion — how much unilateral decision-making room the surrounding institutions leave a person. Dictators, tech CEOs and college head coaches sit at the concentrated-power end; the boss of a utility sits at the other. As one summary of his conclusion puts it, leaders matter most where institutional constraints are weak, authority is concentrated, the timing is favorable, and the individual happens to have rare organizational or persuasive gifts.
  • Truly irreplaceable leaders are rare. Run Napoleon, Roosevelt, Bezos or Gates through his "Hamlet test" and most of them fail; his cleanest pass is Seretse Khama, the founding president of Botswana. Stretch the timescale far enough and, as he put it in an interview, no leader is entirely irreplaceable at all — over the long run, things wash out.

The core concepts, one by one

1. Changing the question: between Carlyle and Tolstoy

According to Diamond's interviews, the book opens on a half-forgotten incident: in March 1930, Adolf Hitler's car was hit by a large trailer truck, and he walked away. Had he died that day, would there have been a Second World War, or a Holocaust? The question is not academic for Diamond, the son of Jewish immigrants from Eastern Europe, whose wife lost many Polish relatives in the Holocaust.

Behind it sits an old quarrel with two poles. One is the Great Man theory associated with the Victorian historian and essayist Thomas Carlyle, whose 1841 lectures On Heroes cast world history as, in essence, the biography of great men (paraphrased): no Napoleon, no Napoleonic Wars. The other is Tolstoy's view in War and Peace: history is driven by the sum of countless ordinary wills, and Napoleon, who thought he was steering events, was merely the man the tide happened to lift to the crest (paraphrased). According to the review in The American Scholar, Diamond explicitly positions his book between these two.

His move is to swap "whether" for "when." Asking "does medicine work?" is a bad question; the good one is "which drug, for which illness, at what dose?" In the same way, "do leaders matter?" has no single answer, but "in what position, at what moment, could one person change the outcome?" can be checked item by item. The Globe and Mail review quotes his bottom line: leaders can make more or less of an impact, depending on the context, the timing, and the leader's ability.

Next time you hear that one person "saved the company," ask first: what conditions gave that one person the chance to matter?

2. Two measuring sticks: biography and natural experiments

The difficulty is that we never get to see the world without the person in it. Diamond approaches that invisible world from two directions.

The first is the biographical method: study a leader's life, decisions and circumstances closely. It supplies motive, sequence and texture — but it has no control group. Would a different person in the same chair have done as well? Biography alone cannot say.

The second is the natural experiment: an event nobody designed that nevertheless behaves like random assignment. A leader's sudden death, hospitalization or family tragedy strikes at a moment unrelated to how the organization was doing, so you can compare firms that abruptly lost their chief with similar firms that did not. This is familiar ground for Diamond, who in 2010 co-edited Natural Experiments of History with the economist James Robinson. According to the publisher's excerpt and press coverage, the new book draws on Denmark's national databases to report that a CEO's death lowered company profits by about 13% on average.

(Outside context, not the book's text: the best-known work in this vein is by the economist Morten Bennedsen and colleagues, who used Danish firm registries to ask "Do CEOs matter?" They found that deaths of CEOs or their close family — especially spouses and children — go with clear declines in profitability, investment and sales growth, and in a follow-up that a five-to-seven-day CEO hospitalization cut profits by about 7% that year. Which studies the book itself cites is for the book to say.)

A classic study at the national level makes a useful companion (also outside context). The economists Benjamin Jones and Benjamin Olken, writing in 2005, examined the 57 national leaders since the Second World War who died in office of natural causes or accidents, and found that growth shifted noticeably when autocrats died, but not when democratic leaders did. That is the book's central variable in miniature: the less constrained the power, the more the individual counts.

To judge whether someone mattered, don't look at how impressive they seemed in office; look at how much the world changed the moment they suddenly vanished.

3. Discretion: how big is the steering wheel?

The book's pivotal concept is discretion: the freedom a leader has to choose among different courses of action. Diamond is careful to say he doesn't mean discretion in the sense of being polite and careful, but in the sense of having a lot of decision-making power (paraphrased).

Think of drivers. A tram driver, however gifted, follows the rails; the gap between a good and a bad one is a few minutes of punctuality. An off-road driver faces open country; the gap between good and bad may be arrival versus the bottom of a ravine. Whether a leader matters starts with which kind of vehicle they are driving.

In business, Diamond splits differences in profit into four sources: the leader, the year, the particular company and the industry. As press accounts summarize the evidence he reviews, studies attribute roughly 6% to 29% of the variation in company earnings to the individual CEO (another summary gives 15% to 30%), while the calendar year accounts for only about 2%. "Accounting for variation" simply means: line up a large set of companies from least to most profitable, and ask how much of that spread moves with who happens to be CEO. The answer: a meaningful slice, nowhere near the whole.

More striking is how sharply that slice varies by industry. By these accounts, he ranks CEO influence highest in computers, perfume and cosmetics, movies, toys and restaurants — sectors where consumer taste moves faster than capital structure, so a single call can make or break a firm — and lowest in utilities, whose bosses may be the most replaceable of all. His advice to aspiring CEOs runs roughly: if you want a shot at getting very rich and influential and can live with the risk of being fired, pick a high-discretion industry — the right perfume makes you rich, the wrong one gets you fired.

Beyond business, the same logic holds: dictators, tech moguls and college head coaches all belong to the class of leaders who can act unilaterally, as reviewers summarize his analysis. (Outside context: management scholars Donald Hambrick and Sydney Finkelstein formalized "managerial discretion" in 1987, arguing that CEOs shape outcomes only to the extent that they have latitude to make real strategic choices. Diamond's argument runs along the same line.)

The easily missed corollary: a big steering wheel amplifies everything — including mistakes. It is no accident the book opens with Hitler. The concentration of power that lets a good leader rewrite an outcome also lets a bad one drive a country off a cliff. Before judging a person, ask how big a wheel their position handed them; when designing institutions, remember that the accelerator you install is not reserved for the good drivers.

4. The Hamlet test: remove them, and would someone fill the gap?

This is the book's signature tool, and it starts from a thought experiment: suppose Shakespeare had died young of smallpox. Diamond then looks at the competition — Christopher Marlowe (author of Doctor Faustus), Ben Jonson (best known for his satirical comedies) and John Webster (author of the revenge tragedy The Duchess of Malfi). All were seasoned, admired professional playwrights, yet their best plays do not approach Hamlet. Conclusion: without Shakespeare, no Hamlet, and nobody else writing one — Shakespeare passes.

So the Hamlet test does not measure how good someone is. It measures whether they could be replaced: if this person vanished, was there a plausible contemporary who could have produced something comparable?

Apply it and the roster shrinks fast. According to The Irish Times, Diamond argues we cannot be sure that someone else would not have achieved similar results had Napoleon or Franklin Roosevelt never come along; according to The Spectator Australia, he sets aside the usual suspects — Napoleon, Churchill, Roosevelt — and is not wholly persuaded by Margaret Thatcher either. In business, Jeff Bezos, Bill Gates, Elon Musk and Mark Zuckerberg are routinely credited with changing the world, but he concludes that none of them passes the Hamlet test: successful risk-takers, yes, but replaceable. Once the internet exploded, someone was going to build the online everything store.

This rests on a distinction the Globe and Mail review singles out: a leader of distinction is not the same as a leader who made a difference. The first did the job well; the second changed what happened. If the industry's wave would have carried whoever was standing on it to the same shore, the CEO riding it was distinguished, not decisive.

It changes how you admire people: stop asking "how remarkable were they?" and ask "without them, would this have happened more or less the same way?" Only the second question sorts out the people who actually rerouted history.

Ask two separate questions: how big a steering wheel did the position provide (vertical), and would someone have filled the gap without them (horizontal)? Only in the top-right cell does an individual truly rewrite the ending.

5. Timing: Genghis Khan and the rainy years

If discretion is the size of the steering wheel, timing is whether the road happened to be open. Diamond's favorite example is Genghis Khan, founder of the largest contiguous land empire in history and indisputably gifted. But as Diamond told Fortune (paraphrased), his luck was that tree-ring researchers have shown only within the last decade or so that the wettest stretch in two thousand years on the Mongolian steppe fell in his era. More rain meant more grass; more grass meant more horses; more horses meant more cavalry. One review sums it up: persistent moisture let the steppe feed more horses and more warriors.

(Outside context: the research is a 2014 paper in PNAS by Neil Pederson and colleagues, who reconstructed 1,112 years of moisture from Siberian pine rings in central Mongolia and found a roughly 15-year run of persistent wetness, unprecedented in a millennium, during the empire's thirteenth-century rise. The paper places that wet spell in the years of expansion, which differs slightly from the interview's "decade of his birth," but the point stands: the climate opened a window.)

This is exactly where the book reconnects with Diamond's earlier work: the environment has not left the stage; it has been demoted from "deciding everything" to "building the set and opening the window." He applies the same logic to tech founders — the Fortune headline says outright that Zuckerberg got lucky too. As CNBC summarizes the book, good leaders are neither born nor made; they are confident, optimistic risk-takers who are almost always in the right place at the right time — and in business, circumstances beyond a CEO's control, from timing and location to competition and regulation, often matter more than the CEO.

For every "he saw it coming" legend, ask which window happened to be open at the time. When a window is open, there is usually more than one person standing at it.

6. Seretse Khama: a rare pass

The book's central case is Botswana, and at least one reviewer calls it the most convincing chapter. At independence in 1966, Botswana was among the poorest countries in the world; Diamond has noted in interviews that it had just 22 university graduates. Then enormous diamond deposits were found — on the lands of the Bangwato, the country's largest group, of which Khama himself was the hereditary chief.

That was the hinge. Deposits like these easily harden into tribal property and then into secessionism, graft and elite capture — the pattern known as the resource curse, in which countries rich in natural resources often grow more slowly, suffer more corruption and see more civil conflict than resource-poor ones, because whoever controls the mines controls everything and politics becomes a fight over the spoils. As reviewers relate it, Khama, as both founding president and hereditary chief, placed mineral rights under national control rather than letting them become tribal property. (This page's gloss:) a president from another group demanding the largest group's diamonds might well have provoked a crisis; an ordinary Bangwato chief might simply have kept them. In the reviewers' account, Khama's combination of legitimacy, position and judgment was rare enough to make him unusually hard to replace. That is what passing the Hamlet test looks like: no contemporary who could plausibly have stepped in.

For contrast, Diamond has discussed Papua New Guinea, which he knows intimately: not unified until the Australian colonial era, home to something like 700 languages, and governed by premiers who have tended to serve their own tribe or clan rather than the nation. In a structure that fragmented, individual leaders barely leave a trace.

Khama's story is the book in miniature: geography gave Botswana its diamonds, but whether they became a blessing or a curse was decided by one person who happened to occupy the only position from which it could be decided. Diamonds, the book stresses, do not make a country prosperous on their own; resources create possibilities. Asked whether the success will last, Diamond admits his worries: politics has become more personalized and more corrupt, and synthetic diamonds are eroding the value of the natural kind.

7. Coaches: Wooden, and the myth of the new manager

Sport gives Diamond something political history rarely does: lots of repeated, measurable outcomes, enough to catch us mistaking a plot for a cause. Reviewers describe a classic trap the book examines: a team goes through an abysmal stretch, fires its coach, and improves; the replacement looks transformative — until you notice that equally miserable teams that kept their coaches improved too.

What is at work is regression to the mean. Every result mixes skill and luck, and an unusually bad run usually contains a heavy dose of bad luck — injuries, a brutal schedule, freak errors. Bad luck doesn't persist, so the next stretch drifts back toward average whether or not anyone is fired. It is the same trap Daniel Kahneman describes in Thinking, Fast and Slow (Read 1) with the flight instructors who believed that shouting at cadets improved them, when a terrible attempt was simply likely to be followed by a better one.

None of which means coaches never matter. The book dwells on John Wooden, UCLA's legendary men's basketball coach: ten national championships and an 88-game winning streak. Reviewers mention some of his methods, such as splitting the squad in practice so that several different groups grew used to playing together, and quote the element Diamond considers indispensable: "Basketball for him was part of a broad philosophy of life." A college head coach, notably, holds a high-discretion post. One reviewer's overall impression is that the business and sport chapters are a mixed bag — sometimes CEOs and coaches clearly move the needle, sometimes the data suggest they are along for the ride — and that Diamond is willing to let the evidence land where it lands.

When things improve after a change at the top, ask about the control group first: what happened to the teams that didn't make the change?

8. Prophets: the three people a religion needs

Religion is the toughest stress test in the book: the founder dies, but the movement must go on — find organizers, write rules, survive persecution, win protectors. In conversation with the economist Tyler Cowen, Diamond offered a compact formula: a new religion needs three people — a charismatic founder, an organizer, and often a general, because new faiths tend to face opposition and need someone who can protect them and win them room to survive.

Christianity is his extended example: how a tiny Jewish sect became the official religion of the Roman Empire. By published accounts he highlights several advantages: the Apostle Paul's decision to open the faith to non-Jews (converts no longer had to adopt Jewish law or circumcision first, so the potential audience grew from one people to an entire empire); Christians' care for the sick during plagues; and early Christianity's prohibition of abortion and infanticide. (Outside context: the last two echo the sociologist Rodney Stark's 1996 The Rise of Christianity, which argued that nursing the sick let more believers survive epidemics and impressed onlookers, while banning infanticide — especially of girls — let Christian numbers grow faster.) In this scheme Jesus is the founder and Paul the organizer.

So does Jesus pass the Hamlet test? Diamond's answer is surprising: not necessarily. There were half a dozen other prophets in the Roman Empire at the time, some of them mentioned in the New Testament, and had Jesus died in childbirth, one of them might have taken over (paraphrased). His real point: whether a religion succeeds depends less on the founder's brilliance than on the organization and conditions that follow.

The same holds for the founding myths of companies, schools of thought and social movements: the person who lights the fire is not necessarily the one who keeps it burning.

9. A leader without office: Rachel Carson, and life beyond charisma

The book's most unusual case turns the problem inside out. The marine biologist and writer Rachel Carson commanded no government and ran no large organization; she had almost no formal authority at all. Yet her 1962 Silent Spring, which documented the ecological damage done by pesticides such as DDT, helped — in reviewers' summary — to accelerate environmental concern and to tie ecological harm more forcefully to human health. Diamond's question is whether American environmentalism would have developed the same way without her. Reviewers note that the author of Collapse does not hide his affection for the book.

Carson leads directly to another conclusion: charisma is only one route to effective leadership. Others, as reviewers relay the book, include persuasion, attention to detail, courage and tenacity. Carson moved a nation not by platform magnetism but by evidence, prose and persistence.

Leadership is not printed on the org chart: a person with no title, the right timing and enough persuasive force can change how an entire country argues about a problem.

The skeleton

The whole book in one line of argument:

  • The question: do leaders rewrite history, or just ride the wave? Either all-or-nothing answer is wrong.
  • The method: biography supplies the detail, natural experiments supply the control group; together they estimate "what if they hadn't been there."
  • Three conditions: discretion (how much room the institutions leave, amplifying good and bad alike), timing (whether a window happens to be open) and ability (confidence and appetite for risk, plus some mix of charisma, persuasion, attention to detail, courage and tenacity).
  • The final filter: the Hamlet test — many people meet the conditions, very few are impossible to replace; and the longer the timescale, the more any individual's mark fades.

How does it relate to Guns, Germs, and Steel and Collapse? Reviewers mostly read it as a turn — one critic says Diamond has finally been seduced by notions of human agency. This page's reading is different: it is a complement and a reconciliation, not a recantation. Guns, Germs, and Steel works on the scale of thirteen thousand years and whole continents, where individuals really do wash out — and Diamond still says so. Collapse had already brought "choice" back on stage, but the chooser was a whole society. This book goes one level down: within the gaps that structure leaves open, which individuals, at which moments, can tip a society's choice the other way. Geography handed Botswana its diamonds; Khama decided who would own them — that one sentence is the synthesis of all three books.

Misreadings, criticisms, live disputes

  • Misreading one: "Diamond has finally conceded that great men make history." The opposite. The Hamlet test eliminates nearly every famous name; even Jesus may not pass.
  • Misreading two: "So leaders don't matter." Also wrong. A CEO's death cuts profits by about 13% on average, and Khama changed a country's destiny. Leaders matter — just very unevenly.
  • Misreading three: "A good leader is a charismatic one." The book lists charisma as one path among several, and Carson is close to a counterexample.
  • Criticism one: an unkept promise and a pair of straw men. In The American Scholar, Elizabeth D. Samet's review, "Chief Misunderstanding," calls the book "a speculative, sputtering study of leadership" and says its promise to ask when rather than whether "goes largely unfulfilled." She also argues that Diamond treats Carlyle and Tolstoy as convenient straw men — simplified versions of opponents set up to be knocked down — reducing Carlyle's On Heroes to a single line about Great Men while ignoring Carlyle's serious engagement with historical forces and the moral purpose of his project.
  • Criticism two: shaky evidence, and a test that rests on taste. Reviewing for Christianity Today, Haley Byrd Wilt argues that the book's conclusions often rest on shaky evidence and that the Hamlet test is, at least in part, a subjective matter of taste — who says Hamlet must outrank Marlowe's best? (This page adds: a play has one author, while a reform has thousands, so "could anyone have filled the gap?" is far harder to judge in politics.)
  • Criticism three: the answer is too vague. The review in Dawn sums it up as "sometimes," after more than 250 pages; the independent reviewer Francis Tapon likewise finds that the book never clearly answers its own question and that the Carson argument is unconvincing — though he rates the Botswana chapter the strongest.
  • There is praise too. Kirkus calls it jaunty and entertaining, moving between beguiling biographical sketches and more technical statistical analysis, with intriguing insights into how much a leader matters (or doesn't). As quoted by a review aggregator, the Financial Times judged it "debatable, certainly, but not dismissible," and found great pleasure in considering Diamond's examples.
  • A methodological worry (this page's analysis): case selection and survivorship bias. The individuals profiled in depth are mostly famous successes. Survivorship bias is the distortion that comes from studying only the cases that made it through — the people who were just as bold and just as well-timed but failed rarely get a chapter, so "bold plus well-timed" looks more like a recipe than it is. The statistical chapters are, paradoxically, the book's firmest ground.
  • A tension with the "romance of leadership" (outside context). In 1985 the organizational scholar James Meindl and colleagues described the romance of leadership: our habit of crediting or blaming leaders for organizational outcomes while discounting everything else, because a leader is the most convenient protagonist for making sense of a complex situation. Diamond's statistics are a corrective to that bias — but chapters built around one heroic figure carry the same narrative temptation.

Ten sentences

1. "Do leaders matter?" is the wrong question; the right one is "under what conditions do they matter?"

2. To judge whether someone was pivotal, don't ask how grand they looked in office — ask how much the world changed when they suddenly vanished. That is the logic of the natural experiment.

3. Danish data show company profits falling by about 13% on average after a CEO dies: leaders matter, but unevenly.

4. Discretion is the first variable: the tram driver follows the rails, the off-road driver chooses the route — first ask how big a steering wheel the position provides.

5. The CEO of a perfume, computer or film company can make or break it; almost anyone could run a utility. The bigger the wheel, the more it amplifies good and bad alike.

6. The Hamlet test: if this person had never existed, would a contemporary have done roughly the same? Distinguished leaders are common; decisive ones are rare.

7. Bezos, Gates and Zuckerberg fail — when the wave comes, someone will ride it; Seretse Khama passes, because only he could hand his people's diamonds to the nation.

8. Timing is the second variable: Genghis Khan rode the steppe's wettest years in a millennium or more — the environment never left; it opens the window.

9. The turnaround after a coach is fired is usually regression to the mean; a religion needs a founder, an organizer and a general — the one who lights the fire is not always the one who keeps it burning.

10. Geography deals the cards, societies choose how to play them, and individuals push the door while the window is open — this book doesn't overturn Guns, Germs, and Steel; it adds the missing chapter on when a person counts.
