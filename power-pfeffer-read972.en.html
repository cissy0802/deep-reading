Power: Why Some People Have It—and Others Don't

Power: Why Some People Have It—and Others Don't · Jeffrey Pfeffer · 2010 (folding in his 2022 follow-up, 7 Rules of Power)

中文 →

In One Sentence

An organization is not a fair machine that hands out rewards for merit; it is a field of power. Doing good work only gets you in the door. What decides who rises and whose ideas win is whether the right people notice you, whether you control resources others need, whether you sit at the crossroads of the network, and whether you have the nerve to speak and act like someone with power. Pfeffer asks readers to give up the comfort of believing the world is just, and to study power the way they would study any other craft.

Where It Sits

Pfeffer is a professor of organizational behavior at Stanford's Graduate School of Business and one of the founders of resource dependence theory; he has spent decades studying power inside organizations, and his Stanford course "Paths to Power" is the raw material for this book. It stands at the hard-nosed realist end of the office-politics shelf, directly opposite Marie McIntyre's win-win Secrets to Winning at Office Politics, which also has a page on this site.

The Core Claims

  • Job performance matters far less to your career than most people believe. Promotions, raises and survival depend mostly on how the people above you see you, and that perception can be shaped. It has to be.
  • The first obstacle to power is you: your belief that the world is ultimately fair, the leadership literature's sermons about modesty, authenticity and nice guys finishing first, and the self-protective habit of not trying hard so that failure doesn't count.
  • Power can be learned. It grows from a set of personal qualities you can develop and a set of moves you can make: choosing where to start, standing out, creating resources, building networks, speaking with power, managing your reputation. And it is worth having. Early on Pfeffer points to the Whitehall studies of British civil servants, led by Michael Marmot, which found that the lower people's rank and the less control they had over their work, the higher their rates of heart disease and death. Power buys more than money and status; it buys health and years of life.

The Key Concepts, One by One

1. Performance matters less than you think: get noticed before you expect to be rewarded

What it is. Pfeffer opens bluntly. Across many studies, the link between job performance and promotion, tenure or pay is modest at best, while your relationship with your boss and the impression you make on them explain much more. There is no mystery here. Performance is almost never measured objectively; it passes through someone's judgment, and that someone is busy, notices only a few things, and naturally warms to people who make them comfortable and make them look good.

An analogy. Picture a contest with judges but no scoreboard. However well you rehearsed backstage, if the judges were watching someone else, or never knew you went on, you scored zero. Most workplaces run this kind of contest most of the time: no automatic tally, only human attention and impressions.

Mechanism and examples. This leads to advice many people find hard to hear. Make sure the people with power know what you are doing and what you have achieved. Make your boss feel smart rather than outshone. Never assume your work will speak for itself. Pfeffer also takes on flattery directly. Management scholar James Westphal and colleagues, studying directors of large American companies, found that executives who flattered and agreed with their CEOs were more likely to be recommended for seats on other boards; marketing researchers have shown that even flattery the target knows to be insincere still leaves a favorable impression below the surface (paraphrased). His conclusion is plain: people underestimate flattery because everyone likes to believe they deserve the praise.

Why it matters and how it changes your view. It turns "I'm so good, why haven't I been promoted?" from a grievance into a problem you can diagnose. The question is not "Am I good enough?" but "How much do the people who decide my fate know about me, and what do they think?" None of this means performance is useless. Pfeffer is explicit that bad performance will hurt you; good performance just isn't nearly enough. It is necessary, not sufficient.

2. The just-world belief: the biggest obstacle is in your head

What it is. The "just-world belief" is a concept from social psychologist Melvin Lerner: people have a strong need to believe the world is basically fair, that the good are rewarded and the bad punished. In Lerner's classic experiments, participants watched a woman (actually a confederate) receive electric shocks for wrong answers. When they were unable to stop the shocks, they tended to think less of the victim, as if she had brought it on herself. Admitting that an innocent person is suffering and you can do nothing is too painful, so people adjust their view of the victim to protect their belief in a just world.

How it hurts you where power is concerned. Pfeffer sees two costs. First, it convinces you that if you just work hard you will be noticed eventually, so you don't build relationships or seek visibility, and you wait for a reward that will never arrive on its own. Second, it blinds you to how successful people actually got there. You assume the people at the top rose on ability and character, so you study the polished version they tell about themselves rather than what they actually did. He is especially skeptical of leadership books and executive memoirs. Winners write them, and they tend to credit modesty, authenticity and teamwork while skipping the fights, the alliances and the self-promotion.

The second obstacle: self-handicapping. Pfeffer also names a psychological defense called self-handicapping: when the outcome is uncertain, people deliberately hold back or put obstacles in their own way, so that if they fail they can blame a lack of effort rather than a lack of ability. In a 1978 experiment by social psychologists Steven Berglas and Edward Jones, participants who had just succeeded on problems they had essentially guessed at, and so were unsure they could do it again, were more likely to choose a drug they were told would impair their performance, building themselves an excuse in advance. Applied to power, many people don't compete in order to protect their self-esteem: if you never really tried, you never really lost.

How it changes your view. You start treating fairness as a hypothesis to be tested, not a default fact, and you grow wary of success narratives, which usually tell you the version the winners want you to believe.

3. Personal qualities: will plus skill

Pfeffer sorts the qualities that help people gain power into two groups. Will is what drives you to go after it: ambition (really wanting it and being willing to pay the price), energy (sustained, intense effort, which is contagious and makes others want to work with you), and focus (concentrating on one industry, one organization, one set of skills instead of scattering yourself). Skill is what lets you get it: self-knowledge (knowing your strengths, weaknesses and blind spots), confidence and the ability to project it (seeming sure of yourself even when you are nervous inside), empathy (here meaning the ability to read other people's interests and positions so you know what to offer them, not moral kindness), and tolerance for conflict (holding your ground when others back off, rather than rushing to please).

Notably, he says intelligence is not at the top of the list. It helps, but very smart people are often too sure that being right is enough; they disdain relationship-building, or argue a point so hard they lose their allies. The list is useful as a self-diagnosis: what you lack is often not ability but a stomach for conflict, or a genuine hunger for the thing.

4. Choosing where to start: join a powerful unit, or get close to the critical problems

What it is. Much of a person's power comes from where they sit. Early in his career Pfeffer and his collaborator Gerald Salancik studied how the University of Illinois allocated its budget and found that the resources departments received tracked their power (such as seats on key committees and outside grant money), not just "objective" needs like enrollment. A unit's power, in turn, comes from whether it handles the organization's most critical uncertainties and controls its scarcest resources. In a company run by its finance people, finance is the heart; in a product-driven company, engineering is.

What to do. His advice is to start in a powerful department, or as close to one as you can, and to judge a job by how near it sits to the company's central problems, not just by its title and salary. He acknowledges the trade-off: powerful units are crowded with talent, so it is harder to stand out, while in weaker units you can shine sooner but hit a lower ceiling. The point is to choose deliberately rather than drift.

How it changes your view. You start drawing a map of the organization's power terrain: who controls the budget, whose opinion decides which projects live or die, which unit has been growing lately. The same effort is amplified in a central position and diluted at the edges.

5. Standing out: ask, and break some rules

Ask. Pfeffer keeps returning to the power of asking: for opportunities, for resources, for time with people higher up. He cites research by his Stanford colleague Frank Flynn and Vanessa Bohns showing that people systematically underestimate how willing others are to help. In their experiments, people asking strangers for favors predicted they would have to ask far more people than they actually needed to, roughly twice as many (paraphrased). We think asking will bring rejection and embarrassment; in fact people usually say yes, and those who help you often end up liking you more. He tells the story of networking writer Keith Ferrazzi, who came from a modest background and, as a new consultant at Deloitte, made a point of seeking meetings with the firm's top leaders and getting noticed, eventually rising to the executive ranks (paraphrased).

Break the rules. People who play strictly by the book are easy to forget. He urges newcomers to do something memorable: take on the tasks no one wants, put forward an unconventional idea, separate yourself from a crowd of similar people. Rules are mostly written by those who already have power, and they tend to favor them; following them to the letter means accepting a game someone else designed. In 7 Rules of Power this becomes rule number two: break the rules.

6. Making something out of nothing: creating resources

What it is. Underneath power are resources: money, jobs, information, other people's attention, the ability to get things done for others. Pfeffer's insight is that resources don't have to be inherited; they can often be built from almost nothing.

Examples. He recounts how Lyndon Johnson got started. As a young man in Washington, Johnson was merely secretary to a congressman. There was an organization of congressional staffers called the "Little Congress," which was nearly moribund. Johnson got himself elected its speaker, turned it into a platform with real debates and press attention, and used it to build a wide web of contacts. Another example is Robert Moses, New York's "master builder." The public authorities he ran, such as the Triborough Bridge Authority, collected bridge and tunnel tolls that gave them a steady income independent of the city budget. That money, beyond the reach of elected officials, let a man who never won an election shape New York's physical form for decades.

How it changes your view. Instead of waiting for someone to grant you resources, ask: what here is unowned but potentially valuable? A committee no one wants to chair, a dataset no one has organized, a cross-departmental gathering no one has convened. Any of these could become your toll booth.

7. Networks: position matters more than size

What it is. Pfeffer treats networking as real work, and research links it to pay and promotion. But more important than how many people you know is where you sit in the network. He draws on sociologist Ronald Burt's theory of "structural holes": when two groups have no direct ties, there is a gap between them, and the person who spans that gap, connected to both sides (the broker), sees the information each side lacks, controls their contact with each other, and gains influence from both. Research by organizational scholar Daniel Brass showed that people at the center of a company's communication network were seen by colleagues as more influential.

An analogy. Think of a city map. Power belongs not to the people living in mansions but to whoever controls the bridge where two main roads meet.

How it changes your view. You invest in relationships more strategically. Rather than growing ever closer to your own team, connect two departments that never talk; rather than collecting business cards, become the node people have to go through to reach someone.

8. Speaking and acting with power; reputation is reality

Speaking and acting. Pfeffer borrows from his Stanford colleague Deborah Gruenfeld, who teaches a course called "Acting with Power": power is partly performed. Posture, tone, how much space you take up, whether you hedge when you speak, all signal to others whether you have power. Another colleague, Larissa Tiedens, found that people who express anger are more likely than people who express sadness to be seen as competent and deserving of status. In one study she used clips of Bill Clinton's grand jury testimony about the Lewinsky affair; viewers who saw him angry supported him more than those who saw him sad (paraphrased). Pfeffer's advice includes cutting vague qualifiers, not over-apologizing, persuading with stories and vivid images rather than abstract arguments, and not being the first to back down in a conflict.

Reputation. The slogan of this chapter is "perception is reality." First impressions are hard to change, so shape them from the start. He cites research by Teresa Amabile, who rewrote the same book reviews in a harsh version and a kind one; readers judged the authors of the harsh reviews to be more intelligent (paraphrased), a warning that being nice does not necessarily make you look capable. His own experiments with collaborators showed that having someone else sing your praises works far better than doing it yourself, even when everyone knows the person praising you is an agent with a financial stake (paraphrased).

9. The price of power, and how people lose it

The price. Pfeffer is frank about the costs. You live in the spotlight, with every move scrutinized. You lose autonomy as meetings and obligations swallow your time. You find it hard to tell real friends from opportunists. And you may become addicted to power itself, struggling to adjust once you leave the role.

Losing it. He draws on research in the psychology of power, notably the approach/inhibition theory proposed by his colleague Gruenfeld with psychologist Dacher Keltner and others, which holds roughly that power makes people pursue what they want more impulsively while dulling their sensitivity to risk and to other people. That explains why those who hold power often become overconfident because of it, stop paying attention to others, trust the wrong people, and refuse to leave when they should. Keeping power therefore takes as much craft as gaining it: stay alert to your surroundings, don't push opponents into a corner, and leave while you can still do so gracefully.

The Skeleton of the Argument

The whole book reduces to one line: rewards in organizations are not automatically distributed by merit → because performance passes through human judgment, and judges' limited attention and preferences can be influenced → we fail to influence them because of the just-world belief, the leadership myth and self-protection → once that filter comes off, power has visible sources: personal qualities, position, resources you control, network location, how you present yourself, reputation → all of these can be managed deliberately → power carries costs and is lost through arrogance, so the work never stops → organizations are arenas of power anyway, so rather than pretend otherwise, learn to act inside them.

What is the evidence? Three kinds: research in organizational behavior and social psychology (the weak link between performance and promotion, flattery, anger and status, network position and so on); a large set of case studies, from politicians to executives, doctors and academics; and feedback from years of teaching Stanford students. Twelve years later, 7 Rules of Power compressed the same ideas into seven actionable rules, in blunter language, with the conclusions unchanged.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: Pfeffer says doing good work is pointless. No. His point is that good work isn't enough. Bad performance will sink you; good performance gets you a ticket, but people holding only a ticket tend to stay where they are.

Misreading two: this is a manual for being a bad person. Mostly he is describing how power actually works and urging readers not to surrender influence out of moral squeamishness. He stresses that people who want to do good, such as a doctor pushing reform or a principal trying to transform a school, need power too, or their good ideas never land. He tells the story of Laura Esserman, a breast surgeon at the University of California, San Francisco, who wanted to build an integrated breast-cancer care center; her ideas were ahead of their time, yet she ran into obstacle after obstacle trying to push them through, which shows that a good idea does not win on its own (paraphrased).

Misreading three: power means position. A title is only one source of power. People without titles who sit at the junctions of information and resources often have more influence than people with them.

Criticism one: ethics. This is the most common objection and the weightiest. Once "success excuses (almost) everything" becomes a rule (the seventh in 7 Rules of Power, roughly that people forgive and even forget the methods winners used), it is no longer just a description; it encourages readers to pursue results by any means. Pfeffer's reply, in essence, is that he is only describing the world as it is, and pretending not to see it only hands an advantage to the unscrupulous. Critics counter that if everyone followed the book, organizations would burn huge amounts of energy on internal fights and trust would erode. That is a collective cost, and the book only keeps the individual ledger.

Criticism two: survivorship bias and case selection. Most of the book's cases are winners: they broke rules, asked boldly, fought their bosses, and succeeded. People who did the same things and were fired or frozen out don't appear. Looking only at the winners' behavior cannot tell you whether that behavior caused their success or just happened to accompany it. Pfeffer warns readers not to trust successful people's accounts of themselves, but his own cases are also drawn from pre-selected success stories.

Criticism three: context. His advice comes mainly from large American companies, politics and business-school students. In organizations where performance can be measured fairly objectively (sales figures, trading profits, competitive sports), visibility counts for less; in cultures that prize seniority and harmony, breaking rules or showing anger may backfire. Then there is gender. A 2008 study by psychologists Victoria Brescoll and Eric Uhlmann found that anger raised the status people conferred on men but lowered it for women, and Laurie Rudman's research found that women who promote themselves are more likely to suffer "backlash," that is, being disliked for violating gendered expectations. These findings are a reminder that the same "powerful" behavior does not pay off equally for everyone.

The clash with McIntyre's win-win politics. Pfeffer and McIntyre agree that organizational politics is unavoidable, but they give nearly opposite answers to the question of how to win.

DimensionPfeffer, PowerMcIntyre, Secrets to Winning at Office Politics

Table: two answers to the same question. In McIntyre's 2×2, people who help themselves while hurting the organization are "sociopaths"; by Pfeffer's account, such people are often the ones who rise fastest.

Who is right? Probably each captures half the truth. Pfeffer describes how many organizations actually work; McIntyre describes how a person can last and still sleep at night. The first helps you read the terrain; the second reminds you the terrain is not a moral yardstick. Read Pfeffer without keeping McIntyre's floor, and it is easy to slide from seeing power clearly to respecting nothing else.

Pfeffer Beyond This Book

7 Rules of Power (2022). Twelve years after Power, Pfeffer published a compressed, sharpened version. The argument hasn't changed; two things have. First, observations scattered across the earlier book are gathered into seven rules you can actually follow. Second, the tone is blunter and he takes on the charge that teaching power tactics is immoral head-on: readers face the world as it is, not as it should be, and people who can't see the rules lose to people who can. Here are the seven, one at a time.

Rule 1: Get out of your own way. This is the starting point, and the one he considers hardest. What blocks you isn't a rival but three things in your own head: the just-world belief covered above (good work will get noticed on its own), self-handicapping (not trying fully, so failure has an excuse ready), and moral discomfort with power itself, the sense that seeking power or promoting yourself is unseemly. His diagnosis is that this discomfort is mostly not a virtue but a form of self-protection, and its price is ceding ground to people who don't feel it. A related warning: caring too much about being liked gets in the way, and the value of being liked is widely overrated (paraphrased).

Rule 2: Break the rules. This picks up the "stand out" section above. Rules are usually written by people who already have power and tend to favor them; a newcomer who follows them to the letter is competing for second place in a game someone else designed. Breaking the rules doesn't mean breaking the law. It means not accepting the default queue, not waiting to be invited, and making the requests others are too embarrassed to make. He keeps returning to an asymmetry: if you ask, the worst outcome is usually a no; if you don't ask, the outcome is a guaranteed nothing. And people consistently overestimate both the odds and the cost of rejection, which is what the Flynn and Bohns research cited above found.

Rule 3: Appear powerful. This extends concept eight above. How much power others think you have comes largely from your posture, your words and your emotional display, not from any check on your competence. In practice: speak directly, drop the self-deprecating qualifiers, apologize less, and in the right setting show anger rather than sadness or hurt (Tiedens's research, mentioned above, found that people who expressed anger were judged to have higher status). Power is to a large degree a performance, and the audience takes the performance at face value. So even when you feel unsure, act sure first; inner confidence often grows in after the outward posture.

Rule 4: Build a powerful brand. The 2010 book said reputation becomes reality; the 2022 book upgrades that to a brand: a clear, memorable image that gets repeated within your circle. A brand's job is to have people speak for you when you aren't there, because opportunities are often handed out in meetings you don't attend. He stresses that having others praise you works far better than praising yourself (the experiment with his collaborators, mentioned above, found that having someone else sing your praises beats blowing your own horn, even when that someone is a paid agent). So a key part of building a brand is cultivating the people who will tell your story.

Rule 5: Network relentlessly. This matches "position matters more than numbers" above. Networking isn't a social gift; it's a skill you can practice, and its returns compound. The key word is "relentlessly". Many people think of their network only when they're job hunting; he argues it should be part of ordinary work, done every week, with special investment in positions that connect groups who don't otherwise know each other (structural holes), because information and opportunity flow through those positions.

Rule 6: Use your power. Many people finally gain power and then don't use it, for fear of offending others or seeming heavy-handed. Pfeffer's view is that power you don't use drains away, and one of the most important uses of power is acquiring more of it: placing allies in key roles, controlling how resources are allocated, widening what you control. Robert Moses, mentioned above, took control of toll-bridge revenue and used that money, beyond the legislature's reach, to sustain decades of influence. It is the textbook case of this rule. Conversely, the ways people lose power, covered in concept nine, mostly begin with no longer using it or tending it.

Rule 7: Success excuses (almost) everything. This is the harshest rule and the most controversial. His observation is that people want to be on the side of winners, history is mostly told by winners, and once someone succeeds, the methods they used tend to be forgotten, reinterpreted or even praised as boldness (paraphrased). So he advises readers not to worry too much about how their methods are judged in the moment, and to keep their eyes on results. Note the "almost": he doesn't claim everything is forgiven, and crimes and serious scandals still come back to bite. This rule is exactly what "Criticism one: ethics" above takes aim at. When the description "the world forgives winners" becomes the rule "so just win", the line between describing and encouraging has been crossed.

Taken together, the first three rules face inward: clear away your own psychological barriers, be willing to step outside the default rules, and look like someone with power. The last four face outward: manage how others see you, manage your position in the network, trade the power you have for more, and accept that results rewrite the story of how you got them. Compared with Power, this book has less research detail and more of a to-do list. For the evidence and mechanisms, the nine concepts on this page are enough; the seven rules put them in the order you would act on them.

Resource dependence and Managing with Power. In The External Control of Organizations (1978), Pfeffer and Salancik laid out resource dependence theory: organizations survive on resources supplied from outside, whoever controls those resources holds power over the organization, and much of what organizations do (mergers, alliances, choice of board members) is aimed at reducing that dependence. Power is really the same theory brought down to the individual: your power depends on how much others depend on what you control. His 1992 Managing with Power was the more academic precursor, written for managers.

The critique of the leadership industry. Leadership BS (2015) pushes the skepticism toward leadership literature in Power all the way: the industry preaches modesty, authenticity, truthfulness and trust year after year, yet real leaders rarely behave that way and employee satisfaction with leaders has not improved; better to study what leaders actually do than believe what they say. Dying for a Paycheck (2018) turns to the other side: he and his collaborators estimated that roughly 120,000 Americans a year die from causes related to workplace stress (paraphrased). Taken together, these books show that Pfeffer is not defending the strong; he is dismantling the comforting stories that keep the weak from seeing their situation.

Crossovers

  • Evolutionary biology · Frans de Waal, Chimpanzee Politics: At Arnhem's Burgers' Zoo, de Waal watched Nikkie, a young male who was not the strongest, become alpha by allying with the older Yeroen. Alike: power comes from coalitions, not strength. Unlike: chimps harbor no illusion that good work gets noticed.
  • Complexity science · Preferential attachment: In the Barabási–Albert network model, new nodes prefer to link to nodes that are already well connected, so a small early lead snowballs into a hub. Alike: early visibility compounds. Unlike: nodes don't campaign; people do.
  • Eastern thought · Han Fei's shi (positional power): Han Fei quotes Shen Dao: a flying dragon rides the clouds, but when they scatter it is no better than an earthworm; the sage-king Yao as a commoner could not govern three people. Alike: both deny that merit naturally wins position. Unlike: Han Fei advised rulers; Pfeffer advises subordinates.
  • Buddhism · The eight worldly winds: Buddhism lists eight worldly conditions that buffet us: gain and loss, fame and disrepute, praise and blame, pleasure and pain. Pfeffer teaches you to harness fame and praise; Buddhism teaches you not to be moved. Alike: reputation is an external wind. Unlike: one says ride it, the other says don't sway.
  • Distributed systems · Leader election in Raft: In the Raft consensus protocol, election timeouts are randomized, and whichever node times out first and asks for votes usually wins, much like Pfeffer's "ask first." But Raft adds a hard constraint: a candidate whose log is not up to date cannot win votes. Engineers deliberately wrote a competence floor into the rules of power, which is exactly what human organizations so often lack.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. Good performance is a ticket, not a trophy: your fate depends on whether the people who matter know about your work and what they make of it.

2. "The world is fair" is a soothing belief, and the number one obstacle to seeking power.

3. Don't learn from the stories successful people tell; learn from what they did. Winners' memoirs tend to leave out the fights and the alliances.

4. Not competing is often a way to protect your self-esteem: if you never really tried, you never really lost.

5. Power takes will (ambition, energy, focus) and skill (self-knowledge, confidence, reading others, tolerating conflict); intelligence matters less than you'd expect.

6. Position amplifies effort: in a unit that handles the organization's core problems, the same work gets seen far more.

7. Ask. People are far more willing to help than you think. Rules are mostly written by the strong, and those who break them get remembered.

8. Resources can be built from nothing, and the value of a network lies in which structural hole you span, not how many people you know.

9. Power is partly performed, and perception is reality; but the same "powerful" behavior does not pay off equally for everyone.

10. Power has a price and is lost through arrogance; and when "success excuses almost everything" is treated as a law, it is up to the reader to hold McIntyre's ethical floor.
