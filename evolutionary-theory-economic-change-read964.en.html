An Evolutionary Theory of Economic Change

Richard R. Nelson & Sidney G. Winter · 1982

中文 →

In One Sentence

A firm is not a rational machine solving for the optimum at every moment; it is a bundle of "routines" it remembers by repeatedly performing them, and routines are to firms what genes are to organisms: inherited, occasionally altered, then sorted by the market, so that economic change is an evolution with no designer.

Where It Sits

Nelson and Winter, economists who had worked together at Yale and RAND, fused Schumpeter's innovation-driven change, Simon's bounded rationality and Darwinian variation-and-selection into a theory you can actually run as a computer simulation. The book founded modern evolutionary economics and is the source of the "organizational routines" and "capabilities" vocabulary that management scholars now take for granted.

Core Claims

  • The textbook firm is a fiction. Standard theory assumes the firm faces a well-defined menu of available techniques, picks the most profitable one, and the industry settles into equilibrium. Nelson and Winter argue that all three assumptions (a given menu, maximization, equilibrium) are false to the world, and that together they blind economics to the very thing that matters most: technical progress and economic change itself.
  • What a firm actually "knows" is stored in its routines. How it produces, prices, invests, even how it runs R&D, mostly follows stable, semi-automatic, never-fully-articulated patterns of behavior. Those routines are the firm's capabilities, and its memory.
  • Economic change is evolution at the level of routines. Firms "search" for better ways of doing things mainly when they are doing badly (variation); markets let profitable firms grow and unprofitable ones shrink (selection); successful routines therefore spread through the industry (inheritance). Nobody has to compute the optimum for the industry to change and improve, and it carries its history with it.

Put together: stop asking how a firm "should" choose; look at what it habitually does, then at which habits the market lets survive.

The Core Concepts, One by One

1. Routines: the genes of the firm

What it is. A routine is any regular, predictable pattern of behavior in a firm, from the operating sequence on a production line and the approval path of a purchase order to decision rules like "price at cost plus 20 percent," "plow a fixed share of profit into new capacity," or "spend a set percentage of sales on R&D." Nelson and Winter sort routines into three kinds: operating characteristics (what the firm does day to day with the plant and equipment it has), investment rules (how its size grows or shrinks with profit and loss), and search routines (how it evaluates and modifies its own routines; even change gets routinized). They say outright that routines play the role in their theory that genes play in biological evolutionary theory (paraphrased).

The analogy. Your genes don't dictate what you do second by second, but they shape what you can do and are inclined to do, and they get passed on. Routines work the same way. They are the source of the firm's "traits"; they are fairly stable (today's way of doing things is mostly yesterday's); and they are heritable: a firm opening a new plant copies its whole way of working across, and new hires pick up the practice by working next to old hands.

Why it matters. Make the routine your unit of analysis and three stubborn puzzles suddenly have a home. First, why firms stay different for so long: same industry, same prices, same technology on offer, yet some firms persistently outperform, because their routines differ and routines are hard to swap out or copy. Second, why firms can't change: a routine is a capability and, by the same token, an inertia. Third, why the economy has a history: today's routines are edited versions of yesterday's, so where an industry goes depends on where it started. That is path dependence: accidents of the past lock in the range of options for the future.

How it changes the way you see. Standard theory asks, "Given these conditions, what is the optimal choice?" Evolutionary theory asks, "How does this firm usually do things? Where did that practice come from? When does it get changed?" The first question is good for static puzzles. The second helps explain why cash-rich giants like Nokia or Kodak looked so clumsy when technology turned: not because they couldn't calculate, but because the routines they were best at had been selected for in the old environment.

2. Skills and tacit knowledge: you can ride a bike but can't say how

What it is. To explain routines, the authors start with individual skills, leaning heavily on the "tacit knowledge" of Michael Polanyi, the Hungarian-born chemist turned philosopher. Polanyi's famous line is that we know more than we can tell (paraphrased). His example is the cyclist. To stay upright, a rider is physically obeying a rule: when the bike starts to fall to one side, steer toward that side, with a curve whose curvature is proportional to the angle of imbalance and inversely proportional to the square of the speed. Almost no cyclist knows the rule; every cyclist executes it.

From this Nelson and Winter extract three features of a skill. It is programmatic: a chain of steps, each cueing the next. It is tacit: the performer cannot fully write it down. And it involves automatic choice: performing a skill is full of choices, but they are made without conscious deliberation; a skilled typist never "decides" which finger goes next.

From person to organization. Their key move is that an organization's routines are the counterpart of an individual's skills. A firm being able to do something doesn't mean there is a manual saying how. That ability is spread across hundreds of people's individual skills, their unspoken coordination, and the web of who passes what message to whom and when. No one holds the whole picture, just as no neuron in the cyclist "knows" the balance formula.

Why it matters. This knocks out a hidden assumption of standard theory: that a firm can pick freely from a menu of techniques. If most production knowledge is tacit, then "another technique" is not a blueprint lying on the table but a whole capability that you only get by doing. Knowing that a technique exists and being able to use it are two different things. It also explains why technology transfer is so hard: ship over the machines, the drawings and the manuals, and the plant still won't run, because the crucial part lives in people.

How it changes the way you see. When a company "obviously knows what to do but can't do it," don't rush to call it stupid or lazy. What it lacks is often not information but capability, and capability has to be practiced into existence; it cannot be ordered into existence.

3. Routines as memory, truce and target

The book devotes a chapter to what routines do inside organizations. Three functions stand out.

(1) Memory: remembering by doing. An organization has no brain to store knowledge in, so where does its knowledge live? The authors' answer: organizations remember by doing (paraphrased). Each member remembers their own part of the job and knows how to respond to "messages" from others (a work order, a word, a part arriving); as long as that flow keeps running, the organization "remembers" how to produce. Conversely, a routine left unexercised for long gets "forgotten": people leave, the tacit coordination dissolves, and a restart means groping your way back. That is why output and yields typically have to climb back up after a plant restarts from a long shutdown.

(2) Truce: a routine is a frozen conflict. This is one of the sharpest ideas in the book. People in an organization don't share interests: who does more work, who gets what resources, who calls the shots. A smoothly running routine is in effect a standing truce. Under the current division of labor everyone roughly gets along, and nobody reopens the question. So changing a routine is not just a technical problem; it tears open the conflicts the truce was holding down. As an illustration: propose scrapping an obviously inefficient reporting process and you may meet surprising resistance, because buried in the old process are arrangements about who answers to whom and who gets to block whom. Inertia is therefore not only cognitive laziness but political fear.

(3) Target for control, replication and imitation. Management uses routines as a control target: deviation from the routine signals that something is wrong. Routines can also be replicated: a firm can take a practice that works in one plant and reproduce it in a new one, far more easily than inventing it from scratch, because it can send people and a working template. A rival trying to imitate has a much harder time: it can see the product, not the mass of tacit routines behind it. Replication is easy, imitation is hard, and that asymmetry is itself a source of competitive advantage. Winter later took this further with Gabriel Szulanski in work on "replication as strategy"; Intel's "Copy Exactly!" policy for new fabs is the extreme version, copying even apparently irrelevant details because no one can be sure which details actually matter.

4. Search and satisficing: looking for "good enough," not the best

What it is. Routines change through search: deliberate efforts to find new production methods, new products, new decision rules. Here the authors inherit two ideas from Herbert Simon, the 1978 Nobel laureate in economics. One is bounded rationality: people's information, attention and computing power are limited, so they cannot survey every option and pick the best. The other is satisficing (a blend of "satisfy" and "suffice"): rather than hunting for the optimum, people set a "good enough" threshold and stop at the first option that clears it. Add the observation of Richard Cyert and James March in A Behavioral Theory of the Firm: firms typically start searching when performance falls below an aspiration level, and carry on as usual while things are going well.

The analogy. Picture a climber in thick fog who can't see distant peaks, only the slope underfoot. If the current spot is comfortable enough, they stay put; if it's too low, they probe a few steps uphill nearby. That is the "local search" in the authors' models: firms look for new techniques mostly close to what they already do, because the farther away they go, the more new capability they need and the greater the uncertainty.

Why it matters. Two big consequences follow. First, technical progress is incremental and chancy: search outcomes are random, sometimes a big find, mostly small improvements. So the models treat R&D as a lottery: spend more and your odds of a win go up, with no guarantee. Second, firms get stuck on local peaks: if every direction around their current technique slopes downward, they stay where they are, even though a higher mountain stands in the distance. That supplies a mechanism for why disruptive innovation so often comes from outsiders.

How it changes the way you see. When a company "suddenly" launches a reform, ask first whether its results just dropped below some line. Evolutionary theory predicts that the trigger for change is usually failure, not foresight.

5. The selection environment: markets as sieves, not equilibrators

What it is. If firms don't maximize, why doesn't the economy fall into chaos? Selection. In the authors' models, firms expand according to their investment rules: profitable firms pour profits into capacity and grow; loss-making ones shrink and eventually exit. The result is that good routines account for a growing share of industry capacity, not because every firm learned them but because the firms that have them got bigger. The market here is not an "equilibrator" that parks prices and quantities at a point but a "selection environment" that keeps on sifting.

A key bit of intellectual history. In 1950 Armen Alchian argued that even if firms don't know how to maximize, markets will weed out the unprofitable ones, so the survivors will look as though they maximize. In 1953 Milton Friedman pushed the point to its limit: it doesn't matter whether the theory's assumptions are realistic, only whether firms behave "as if" they maximize and the predictions hold, just as an expert pool player knows no physics yet shoots "as if" solving the equations. In a 1964 paper Winter put this "natural selection vindicates maximization" argument to the test and concluded that selection guarantees that the survivors are maximizers only under fairly demanding conditions: the environment must be stable enough, selection fast enough, and profitable firms must actually reinvest to expand. Change the environment and yesterday's winning routine may no longer fit, and selection always lags behind change. The book can be read as the constructive sequel to that critique: if selection matters so much, don't use it to defend static maximization; model the selection process itself.

Why it matters. It exposes a common fallacy: "whatever exists must be optimal." The practices that survive in an industry prove only that they were good enough in past conditions, not that they are the best possible, still less that they will be good enough in the future.

6. Simulation: Solow's curves without a production function

What it is. The book's most methodologically ambitious move is to replace mathematical solution with computer simulation. Evolutionary processes, the authors argue, are too complex and too random to be boiled down to a few equations solved for equilibrium, but you can write down the rules (each firm's routines, search behavior and investment rule), let the computer run hundreds of periods, and see what macro patterns emerge.

Example one: economic growth. In 1957 Robert Solow ran a "growth accounting" exercise on US data for 1909 to 1949 and found that most of the growth in output could not be explained by more capital and labor; he attributed it to "technical change," which mainstream theory describes with a smooth aggregate production function (a mathematical rule converting inputs into output). Nelson and Winter built a simulated economy of many firms, each with a single current technique, running on routines, occasionally improving through local search and imitation, and expanding or shrinking with profits. The model has no aggregate production function and no one maximizes anything, yet the time series it generates for output, capital and wages broadly resemble the US data Solow used. The upshot: macro data that "look as if" they came from a neoclassical model do not prove the economy works that way. The same data can come from an entirely different mechanism.

Example two: Schumpeterian competition. Joseph Schumpeter held that the essence of capitalism is "creative destruction": new technologies and new firms constantly assault and replace old ones. The book's final chapters simulate this contest: some firms invest in innovation (R&D), others mainly imitate. Among the findings: even if all firms start out identical, differences in innovative luck make industry concentration (the degree to which a few big firms dominate the market) rise on its own; the easier imitation is, the lower concentration stays, but the thinner the innovator's reward; and if large firms restrain their expansion, the industry stays more dispersed. Here a classic debate gets turned around. Economists have long argued over the "Schumpeterian hypothesis," whether big firms are better innovators. The evolutionary model suggests causation may also run the other way: not concentration causing innovation, but successful innovation causing concentration.

The Spine of the Argument

The whole argument compresses into one line: orthodox theory describes firms as "maximization plus equilibrium," and that makes technical change invisible, because change happens precisely where nobody knows what the optimum is. To study change you first have to redescribe the firm. Its knowledge is mostly tacit (via Polanyi), so it isn't choosing from a menu; it is executing routines, which serve as organizational memory, truce and capability (the organizational version of individual skill). Routines are normally stable; when performance falls below target, firms search locally and generate variation (via Simon, Cyert and March); markets let the carriers of good routines expand (a direct answer to, and correction of, the Alchian–Friedman "selection vindicates maximization" argument). That gives a mechanism of variation, selection and inheritance.

How is it established? Not by econometric testing but by a "sufficient to generate" argument: starting from a behaviorally more realistic micro setup, simulation produces macro patterns resembling reality (growth data, industry concentration, the innovation–imitation trade-off). That shows two things. First, the macro regularities orthodox theory describes do not need the maximization assumption to appear. Second, the evolutionary framework can answer questions the orthodox one struggles with: where technical progress comes from, why firms stay persistently different, how industry structure shifts with innovation.

Routines (stable execution) → performance below target? → search (local, random, generating variation) → new routines → market selection (profitable firms grow, unprofitable ones shrink) → good routines gain share (inheritance) → the environment shifts, and round again

The evolutionary loop: no step requires anyone to "compute the optimum," yet the industry keeps changing and improving.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "routine" means bureaucracy and rigidity. No. In the book a routine is first of all a capability; anything complex a firm manages to do, it does through routines. Inertia is just the flip side of that capability. Even innovation can be routinized: R&D budget rules and lab workflows are search routines.

Misreading 2: "evolution" means progress and optimality. No. Selection only ensures that survivors were good enough in the past environment; it guarantees nothing optimal. The process is random and path-dependent and can lock onto local peaks. Evolution here has no goal.

Misreading 3: the book says people are irrational. Also no. The authors' agents are boundedly rational and purposeful: firms pursue profit, they just can't compute the optimum. What the authors reject is the assumption of knowing every option and picking the best, not rationality itself.

Criticism 1: the biological analogy is loose. The authors themselves admit the theory is "unabashedly Lamarckian" (paraphrased). Lamarck held that traits acquired during an organism's lifetime can be passed to offspring, an idea biology long ago rejected; firms really can pass on what they learn, so the authors adopt it on purpose. The trouble is that once the analogy is this flexible, "inheritance" gets hard to pin down. Routines are modified often inside firms and flow between them via job-hopping and consultants, so what is the stable replicator (the unit that is faithfully copied and carries the hereditary information)? Is the unit of selection the routine, the firm, or a bundle of routines? Geoffrey Hodgson and Thorbjørn Knudsen later tried to define routines more rigorously as replicators within a "generalized Darwinism," which shows how much the original left open.

Criticism 2: "routine" is too vague. It sometimes means a behavior pattern (what people actually do repeatedly), sometimes a rule (a written procedure), sometimes a disposition (a capacity to act a certain way in a certain situation). Markus Becker's 2004 review lays out this split in detail. Martha Feldman and Brian Pentland argued in 2003 that routines have an "ostensive" side (people's abstract understanding of the routine) and a "performative" side (each concrete enactment), and that the gap between them keeps routines changing, which means routines are nowhere near as stable as genes.

Criticism 3: missing microfoundations. Teppo Felin and Nicolai Foss objected in 2005 that using a collective concept (routines) to explain a collective outcome (firm performance) skips a link: the individual. Who designs routines, who maintains them, how do individual choices and differences in ability add up to organizational behavior? Flatten individuals into cogs that execute routines and you cannot explain where routines come from or why some organizations' routines change faster.

Criticism 4: testability and the persuasiveness of simulation. Simulations can generate data resembling reality, but they have many parameters, and many different models could generate the same curves. That is exactly the argument the authors aimed at neoclassical models, and it can be turned back on evolutionary ones. Mainstream economics responded by absorbing pieces such as bounded rationality and firm heterogeneity without buying the whole framework. The result: the book has always been a minority voice inside mainstream economics, yet in management and innovation studies it became one of the most-cited works there is, the direct starting point for research on "dynamic capabilities" and organizational learning.

Crossovers

  • Neuroscience · basal ganglia "chunking": Ann Graybiel found that once a rat's maze habit forms, striatal neurons fire mainly at the start and end of the sequence, the middle packed into an automatic program, much as a routine compresses a string of choices into thoughtless execution. Unlike: a brain has one owner.
  • Biology · the Weismann barrier: August Weismann showed germ cells are walled off from body cells, so acquired traits aren't inherited, the key blow to Lamarck. Firms have no such wall: what they learn passes on at once, so they evolve faster, and so do their bad habits.
  • Cognitive science · Hutchins's "cognition in the wild": Edwin Hutchins found that a navy ship's navigation knowledge sits in no single sailor's head but across people, instruments and reporting procedures: "remembering by doing" exactly. Unlike: he doesn't ask how such systems get selected out.
  • Complexity science · NK fitness landscapes: Stuart Kauffman showed that the more interdependent the parts, the more rugged the landscape and the likelier local hill-climbing strands you on a minor peak, a mathematical version of local search locking firms in. Unlike: NK landscapes are fixed; markets shift as rivals move.
  • Buddhism · habit-seeds and "perfuming": Yogacara holds that every act "perfumes" a seed into the storehouse consciousness (alaya-vijnana), and the seed later sprouts into a like act, so we cycle in our old habits; routines too are kept alive by use, and use strengthens them. Unlike: Buddhism aims to undo the seeds; Nelson and Winter treat routines as capability.

The Book in Ten Sentences

1. Routines are to firms what genes are to organisms: they shape what a firm can do and tends to do, and they are inherited.

2. Most of a firm's knowledge is tacit; knowing a technique exists and being able to use it well are two different things.

3. Organizations remember by doing, and a routine left unexercised is forgotten.

4. Behind every smooth routine is a truce, and changing it reopens the conflicts it was holding down.

5. Replicating your own routines is easy and imitating someone else's is hard; that asymmetry is competitive advantage.

6. Firms seek "good enough," not the best, and usually start searching for new ways only when results fall below target.

7. Search is local and chancy, so firms get stuck on local peaks.

8. Markets are sieves, not equilibrators: good routines spread not because everyone learns them but because the firms that have them grow.

9. Survival proves only that a practice was good enough in the past, not that it is optimal or will be good enough tomorrow.

10. Macro data that look neoclassical don't prove the economy works that way; an evolutionary economy with no maximizers can generate the same curves.
