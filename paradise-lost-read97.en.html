Paradise Lost

John Milton · ten books in 1667, twelve in the 1674 revision

中文 →

In one sentence

A blind middle-aged man who had backed the losing side and watched the republic he gave his eyesight to collapse sat down and dictated ten thousand lines of unrhymed verse in order to try a case — the defendant being God, the charge being that if you are all-knowing and all-powerful, the evil in the world is on your account. His entire defence rests on one claim: the freedom God handed out was real, and a real freedom has to include the power to destroy yourself with it. To make that claim stick he had to write a tempter good enough to be genuinely tempting. He succeeded so thoroughly that for three centuries half his readers have concluded he was secretly on the devil's side.

Where it sits

Milton (1608–1674) spent the English Civil War and the Commonwealth as the sharpest polemical pen in the country: he argued in print that executing Charles I was lawful, served Cromwell's government as Latin Secretary, and burned out what was left of his eyesight in an international pamphlet war against European royalist scholars, going completely blind around 1652. When the monarchy returned in 1660 his books were publicly burned, he was briefly jailed, and only friends in the right places kept him off the scaffold. Paradise Lost was written after all of that: a total loser, in the dark, dictating an epic whose stated purpose is to prove God just. In English literature it occupies roughly the position Dante holds in Italian — not the most lovable monument, the least avoidable one.

The central claims

The poem states its own brief in Book I: to justifie the wayes of God to men. Three propositions carry the weight.

  • Evil is not God's work, it is freedom's. God made creatures sufficient to have stood, though free to fall. Foreknowledge is not predestination — God can see what you will do tomorrow, but seeing is not doing it for you. This is a frontal rejection of the Calvinist predestination that dominated English Puritanism, and saying it in print carried real risk.
  • The real enslavement happens on the inside; external tyranny is only the invoice. Satan talks nothing but liberty and is the most thoroughly imprisoned figure in the poem. Humanity lets appetite depose reason first; tyrants arrive second. This is theology, and it is also Milton's private verdict on why the English revolution failed.
  • The garden is lost, but what replaces it may be larger. The ending is not restoration but interiorisation: what the angel promises is a paradise within thee, happier farr. The epic shifts from holding a place to becoming a state.

The core ideas, one at a time

Theodicy: a lawsuit he brought on himself

Theodicy — the word is Leibniz's, coined in 1710 from the Greek for "god" plus "justice" — names this problem: if God is at once wholly good, all-powerful and all-knowing, who is responsible for the plainly visible evil in the world, the plagues and tyrannies and dead infants? Omnipotence means he could have stopped it, omniscience means he saw it coming, perfect goodness means he should have wanted to. One of the three has to give.

Milton refuses to give up any of the three and instead loosens the human end: the freedom on offer is genuine, and being genuine, God will not override it. God says as much in Book III (paraphrased): I made them free, and free they must remain unless I change the decree that made them so; they decided their own revolt, not I. Theologically this is Arminianism — after the Dutch theologian Arminius, holding that grace is offered to all and can be refused — and it collides head-on with the Calvinist doctrine that who is saved was settled before anyone was born.

That move fixes the shape of the whole poem and plants its central problem. To prove the fall was self-inflicted, you must first make standing look genuinely possible; and for standing to be a real achievement, the temptation has to be a real temptation. A straw-man devil, obviously stupid, would wreck the defence: if Satan were transparently fake, Eve's falling for him becomes a manufacturing fault, and the liability goes straight back to the manufacturer. So Milton had to give the devil the best counsel available. He did — arguably too good.

Satan: the most attractive figure in the poem is its largest counter-example

Satan is the first fully modern protagonist in English: self-aware, self-justifying, capable of talking himself into things in soliloquy. His best lines have long since escaped the book — Better to reign in Hell, then serve in Heav'n; The mind is its own place, and in it self / Can make a Heav'n of Hell, a Hell of Heav'n. They read like the manifesto of an unbroken man.

It is a trap, and the trap has two catches.

First: strip Satan's grievance down and it is wounded status — the poem's own phrase is sense of injur'd merit, the feeling of having earned something that was not acknowledged. He is not rebelling for anyone's liberty; he is rebelling because the Son was placed above him and the ranking felt unfair. The most ordinary office emotion there is, written at cosmic scale.

Second, and worse for him: he demolishes himself. In Book IV, alone, looking down at the world he is about to ruin, he says the nakedest line in the poem — Which way I flie is Hell; my self am Hell. Set that beside "the mind is its own place": the first is the boast, the second is the bill. He did indeed make his mind its own place. It came out hell, and he cannot leave. He then admits that if God pardoned him he would revolt again immediately, because the moment his rank was restored the resentment would return. Hence the most famous act of self-cancellation in English verse: Evil be thou my Good — not the speech of someone who cannot tell good from evil, but of someone who can no longer survive recognising it.

Physically he shrinks the whole way down: from the mountain-sized commander of Book I, to a cormorant, a lion and a tiger in the garden, to a toad squatting at Eve's ear, to the inside of a snake's mouth. C. S. Lewis, in A Preface to Paradise Lost (1942), traced the curve best: from hero to general, from general to politician, from politician to secret agent, then to a thing peering in at bedroom windows, then a toad, finally a snake. His last public appearance is a victory speech back in Pandemonium; the cheer he expects comes out as hissing, because the entire audience is turning into serpents while he talks.

Here is how it changes the way you read anything: Milton demonstrates that the rhetoric of resistance and the substance of resistance can come completely apart. Every term Satan uses — liberty, equality, refusal to serve, rejection of imposed hierarchy — is a term Milton himself had used in his own political pamphlets. A man who had just defended killing a king deliberately puts that vocabulary in the devil's mouth, and what he is saying is something he had watched happen: shouting "freedom" and being free are different things, and a revolutionary becomes a tyrant without changing a single line of his script — only his seat.

The council in Pandemonium: hell is a political system first

Pandæmonium is Milton's coinage — Greek pan, all, plus daimon, spirit: the place where all the demons assemble, the debating hall Satan's army throws up in hell. (The modern English sense, "uproar", descends from it.) Book II is one long debate in that hall, and Milton — a man who had actually sat in a government office — turns it into a dissection of deliberative politics: everyone on the floor is arguing from principle, everyone is in fact arguing from temperament, and the outcome was fixed before the session opened.

Book II's "democracy": four arguments that are really four temperaments, on an agenda the chair wrote in advance.

The sharpest stroke is the adjournment. Once the motion passes, someone has to volunteer to cross Chaos and execute it. Satan rises and accepts, the very picture of a leader taking the risk for his people — and then closes the meeting before anyone else can speak. He wants the glory and he is afraid somebody might actually compete for it. A volunteer hero who is also a chairman forbidding volunteers.

Liberty is not doing as you like: inner servitude comes first, tyrants come second

Milton's definition of freedom is not the modern one, and missing that will bend the whole poem out of shape. For him, freedom means the thing that ought to be in charge inside you actually is in charge. Reason should govern appetite as law governs a commonwealth. Once reason yields, every "but I want to" you act on is not liberty; it is a hijacking.

Obedience and freedom are therefore not in tension. Adam and Eve keep the prohibition not because they are held down but because they see clearly and consent — and the poem says plainly that had they been built incapable of disobeying, the obedience would have been worth nothing. This is exactly the argument of Areopagitica (1644): I cannot praise a fugitive and cloistered virtue, unexercised and unbreathed. Untested virtue is not virtue.

And here is the beautiful cruelty of Book IX: when Eve proposes that they work apart, that is the argument she uses — what is our constancy worth if it has never once been tested alone? She is right, and her source is Milton. Adam gives way not because he is deceived but because he cannot refute a correct principle. This is the poem's finest insight into how people fall: what pushes you over is usually not a lie, it is a truth deployed at the wrong moment.

In Book XII the doctrine of inner freedom cashes out into the most political passage in the poem. Michael explains to Adam why human beings end up with tyrants (paraphrased): since the fall, true liberty is gone, because it lives and dies with right reason; and since a man permits unworthy powers to reign inside him, God's just sentence is to subject him from without to violent lords. Written by someone who had just watched a republic collapse and the English cheerfully welcome their king back. He does not say the English were unworthy of freedom; he says something colder: a people without self-government on the inside cannot hold free institutions up on the outside.

A monist universe: angels eat, digest and blush

This is the layer of the poem modern readers most reliably miss. Most Christian tradition cuts the world in two — spirit against flesh, soul against body. Milton refuses. When the angel Raphael comes to lunch in Eden he actually eats — not a symbolic nibble but "with keen dispatch of real hunger", and he digests it. When Adam asks whether angels make love, Raphael answers candidly and glows with a Celestial rosie red: an angel blushing, probably for the first time in literature.

Underneath is a distinctly heterodox metaphysics: monism — there is one first matter all, and the distance from soil to angel is not a difference of kind but of refinement — together with creation ex Deo, the claim that the world was not made out of nothing but differentiated out of God's own substance. Scholars (Stephen Fallon among them) call it animist materialism.

Raphael illustrates the ladder with a plant: root draws from the soil, sends up a green stalk, the stalk leaves, the leaves a flower, and the flower breathes out fragrant spirits — one organism, refined stage by stage. Humans work the same way.

Schematic: one substance, increasingly refined. Raphael says outright that with continued obedience human bodies may "up to spirit work".

Change that one premise and the whole story changes meaning. If spirit and flesh are two things, the fall is a soul dropping into the mud of a body and salvation is escape — the Platonised Christianity most people carry around. In Milton there is no mud. Humanity already stands on an upward ramp: keep obeying and you gradually become the kind of being an angel is. So the sin of the fruit is not carnal craving. It is the opposite — it is a shortcut, swapping a road that leads to the same place for one bite that arrives now. Which is why sex in the garden is not merely innocent but gets a hymn of its own ("Haile wedded Love"), with a swipe at anyone who confuses purity with abstinence. There is sex in Eden, and it is guiltless.

Two different falls: Eve is persuaded, Adam is not deceived

Genesis gives this a few lines. Milton splits it into two entirely different psychologies, and it is his sharpest piece of observation.

Eve's fall is a successful sales call. The serpent's pitch escalates in stages: flattery first (beauty like yours, seen by one man only, is wasted); then a live demonstration (I ate, and not only did I not die, I can talk — a case study you can see and hear); then a redefinition of the prohibition as envy (he forbids it because he fears you becoming like him); finally, disobedience repackaged as courage. You can hear the same sequence in any high-gloss pitch today. The hinge is the third move: once "he forbids it because he is afraid of you" is in your head, obedience stops being love and becomes cowardice.

Adam's fall is nothing like it: he believes not one word. The poem is explicit — not deceav'd, / But fondly overcome with Femal charm. He watches the garland he wove for her fall from his hand and does the arithmetic in full: she is finished; then he chooses to be finished with her. His reason is love — we are one flesh, to lose you is to lose myself.

Milton's verdict is hard and easy for modern readers to invert: Adam's sin is not loving, it is placing a creature above the one who made him — in non-theological terms, making a person your ultimate meaning. Three and a half centuries on that is still a live observation. Take something entirely good — love, loyalty, family, work — and elevate it to "without this I am nothing", and it starts demanding that you sacrifice your judgement to it. In the moment, that sacrifice always looks exactly like virtue.

The aftermath is just as precisely handled. No thunderbolt: the relationship simply changes state. First lust (a scene written coarse on purpose, in deliberate contrast to the earlier hymn), then shame on waking, then a whole book of mutual blame. The first lived experience of the lost paradise is an argument.

The fortunate fall, and the paradise within

Felix culpa — "fortunate fault", from an ancient Easter liturgy: Adam's sin was lucky, since it earned us such a redeemer. That old paradox is Milton's key for the ending.

In Book XII, Michael runs the whole of subsequent human history past Adam — flood, Babel, tyrants, exile, incarnation, last judgement. Adam's response is the strangest speech in the poem (paraphrased): I stand full of doubt whether I should repent of the sin I caused, or rejoice the more that so much more good will spring from it. He does not pick a side. Neither does Milton.

Then comes the actual conclusion of the epic, from Michael: do not grieve for the garden — add deeds, virtue, faith and love to your knowledge and you will possess a paradise within thee, happier farr. The line performs a swap on the poem's whole balance sheet: what was lost is real estate, what is gained is a condition you carry with you and can only grow yourself.

The closing four lines are among the most famous in English: The World was all before them, where to choose / Thir place of rest, and Providence thir guide: / They hand in hand with wandring steps and slow, / Through Eden took thir solitarie way. Every clause contradicts its neighbour: "all before them" is openness, "solitarie way" is loss; "Providence thir guide" is comfort, "wandring steps and slow" is fear. Milton refuses to let the ending taste of only one thing — which is precisely the line between this poem and every piece of didactic verse ever written. What it hands you at the end is not a conclusion but a pair of emotions that are both true.

The argument, stripped to its frame

1. The brief, and its price. To prove God just, the only available route is "evil comes from freedom", which requires proving the freedom real — and real freedom requires real temptation, which requires a first-rate tempter. Hence the most eloquent rebel in literature: a risk Milton took on purpose, and the entire source of the charge that he sided with the devil.

2. Dismantling the tempter. Not by having the narrator denounce him, but by letting Satan give himself away: injured merit (motive), my self am Hell (condition), Evil be thou my Good (endgame). The method is not refutation; it is letting him finish talking.

3. Two falls, two exits sealed. On Eve's side, "she was foolish" is blocked — what took her was a flawless pitch whose key premise is the author's own conviction. On Adam's side, "he was tricked" is blocked — he knew everything; love crushed him.

4. Settlement, and not only in theology. Book XII wires inner disorder to outer tyranny as cause and effect: appetite deposes reason first, violent lords arrive second. That is Milton's post-mortem on the English revolution.

5. A different destination. The remedy is not the garden returned but the garden relocated inward. So the lawsuit ends on a reversal: what actually gets defended is less God's justice than the proposition that freedom is worth what it costs — and Milton's answer is yes, even though the invoice is all of human history.

Misreadings, and the serious objections

  • "Milton was of the devil's party" is a real quotation, but it is a verdict, not a fact. It is Blake's (The Marriage of Heaven and Hell, c. 1790): Milton wrote in fetters when he wrote of angels and God, and at liberty when of devils and hell, because he was a true poet and of the devil's party without knowing it. Shelley went further, calling Milton's Devil morally superior to his God. That Romantic reading ruled for a century. The rebuttal is strong too: Lewis points out that Satan's complaint boils down to not having been promoted; and Stanley Fish's Surprised by Sin (1967) offers the most ingenious answer — your being moved by Satan is the design: the poem seduces you, then in the next line makes you notice you were seduced, so that you re-enact the fall in the act of reading. (The obvious objection is that this makes the theory unfalsifiable: any reader reaction whatsoever can be called part of the plan.)
  • The God of the poem is off-putting; that is a recognised problem, not a reader's error. William Empson, in Milton's God (1961), put it least politely: the poem is great precisely because it presents a morally repellent deity, and Milton half knew it. God's self-defence in Book III does read more like courtroom argument than revelation, and plenty of readers meet "they themselves decreed their own revolt, not I" and hear special pleading rather than mercy. That is the structural cost of Milton's own strategy: once you put God on the stand, he has to argue, and a God who argues is no longer mysterious.
  • Dr Johnson's sting still lands. In the Lives of the Poets he called it one of those books "which the reader admires and lays down, and forgets to take up again. None ever wished it longer than it is." He named specifics too: the allegorical figures of Sin and Death in Book II (Sin born from Satan's head, incestuously mothering Death) mix badly with the literal action, and a poem with only two human beings in it is short on human interest. T. S. Eliot added another blow in 1936, arguing that Milton's wall of a style blocked English poetry for two centuries — though he substantially withdrew that in 1947. Book VI, the war in heaven, is also often judged a failure: Satan's side invents artillery, angels tear up hills and throw them, and the effect is close to comic — although the alternative reading is that Milton is making violence look ridiculous on purpose, since three days of it achieve nothing and the Son ends the war alone in an afternoon.
  • The gender problem is real, not an anachronistic complaint. Book IV's Hee for God only, shee for God in him cannot be explained away, and Gilbert and Gubar, in The Madwoman in the Attic, borrowed Woolf's phrase to name Milton the "bogey" sitting on women writers. But the ledger has two columns. Eve is the only character who breaks the deadlock after the fall — in Book X it is she who bends first and offers to carry the whole guilt, and only then does the recrimination stop. The last human speech in the epic is hers as well (paraphrased: to go with you is to stay here; where you are, there is my country). Scholars such as Barbara Lewalski have argued on that basis that Milton's Eve is far more complicated than the hierarchy line suggests. Both columns are in the text; neither cancels the other.
  • "Paradise Lost is just the standard Christian story" is the most badly wrong of all. The relevant Genesis text runs a few dozen verses and contains no Satan (only a snake), no war in heaven, no Pandemonium, no characters called Sin and Death, and no angel giving Adam a lecture on cosmology. Milton invented all of it. More importantly, his own theology was seriously heterodox: the Latin manuscript De Doctrina Christiana, rediscovered in 1823, shows him to be an Arian (the Son begotten and subordinate, not co-equal), a mortalist (the soul sleeps with the body until resurrection), and a believer that the world was made out of God's substance rather than out of nothing. (Its authorship was challenged in the 1990s; a 2007 Oxford study concluded it is substantially Milton's.) The dashing, insubordinate Satan in the modern imagination does not come from the Bible. He comes from this heretic.
  • "The forbidden fruit stands for sex" is the most widespread folk misreading, and the poem says the reverse. Milton writes prelapsarian married sex and praises it, and mocks those who equate chastity with abstinence. The fruit stands for disobedience itself — specifically, for skipping a road that was supposed to take time. Incidentally the poem's usual word is "fruit"; Satan says "an apple" once, in his Book X victory speech, almost certainly playing on the Latin malum, which means both apple and evil.

Milton beyond this book

Read only Paradise Lost and you get a pious blind epic poet. The real Milton was a great deal more dangerous, and without that half the passages about liberty above stay abstract.

Areopagitica (1644) — titled after the Areopagus council of ancient Athens, an oration on paper addressed to Parliament against pre-publication licensing. Its argument is not that free speech is a right but an epistemological claim: truth that never meets falsehood face to face is not yet truth, and locking bad books out locks out the people's judgement with them. The cloistered-virtue line quoted earlier comes from here and passed unchanged into the free will of the epic. (His tolerance had a limit: he did not intend to extend it to Catholics.) The divorce tracts of the same period, arguing that marriage is essentially a meeting of minds and that incompatibility alone should end it, were scandalous enough that the resulting pile-on is what prompted Areopagitica in the first place. And the regicide tracts — The Tenure of Kings and Magistrates, Eikonoklastes, the Latin Defence of the People of England — arguing that sovereignty rests with the people and that a people may try and execute a tyrant, are the actual source of everything the poem says about rule, obedience and tyranny.

Paradise Regained and Samson Agonistes (published together in 1671) are the epic's counterweights. The first is the temptation in the wilderness: offered wealth, power, force of arms and the whole learning of Athens, Christ declines all of it — heroism redefined as refusal rather than conquest. The second is a verse drama about a strongman, shaved, blinded and set to grind at his enemies' mill, recovering inner freedom inside humiliation. A blind, politically annihilated author writing that subject needs no footnote.

Ten sentences

1. The opening brief is a single line: justify the ways of God to men. To do it Milton put God in the dock — and a God in the dock has to argue.

2. The whole defence rests on one point: if freedom is real it must include the power to destroy yourself; sufficient to have stood, though free to fall.

3. Which is why he had to write the devil well. Satan's magnetism is not the author slipping; it is the necessary cost of the argument.

4. Strip the rebellion down and you find injured merit — credit claimed and not granted. A cosmic revolt that starts with a promotion that went to someone else.

5. "The mind is its own place" is the boast; "my self am Hell" is the bill. He did make his mind its own place — it came out hell, and there is no exit.

6. In Pandemonium four speakers argue four principles and are really arguing four temperaments; and the chair, once his motion carries, closes the meeting before anyone can volunteer against him.

7. Angels eat, digest and blush, because Milton does not split spirit from flesh: everything is one substance at different degrees of refinement, and obedient bodies may "up to spirit work". The sin of the fruit is therefore taking a shortcut.

8. The two falls run on different machinery: Eve is taken by a flawless pitch whose crucial premise is the author's own truth; Adam believes nothing and is crushed by love — by putting a creature where the creator belongs.

9. Book XII is his post-mortem on the English revolution: appetite deposes reason inside a man first, and violent lords arrive afterwards. Liberty is not won before it is deserved.

10. The ending is a swap, not a restoration: the garden goes, "a paradise within thee, happier farr" arrives. And the last four lines refuse a single flavour — the world all before them, the way solitary; Providence as guide, the steps still wandering and slow.
