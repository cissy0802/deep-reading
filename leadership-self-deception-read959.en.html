Leadership and Self-Deception

Getting Out of the Box · The Arbinger Institute · 2000 (revised 2010, 2018)

中文 →

In One Sentence

Underneath most of the people problems in companies and families sits a single problem: the moment we go against our own sense that we ought to do something for someone, we start building a case for ourselves and begin to see that person as an obstacle or an object rather than a person — and from then on, the problem is us, which is exactly what we can't see. The book calls that blind state being "in the box," and argues that leadership starts with getting yourself out of it.

Where It Sits

The Arbinger Institute is a Utah-based consulting and training firm whose ideas grew out of BYU philosopher C. Terry Warner's work on self-deception. This is its flagship book, written as a business parable: Tom Callum, a newly hired manager at Zagrum Company, is pulled into a day-long conversation with executive vice president Bud Jefferson, later joined by the company's founder, Lou Herbert — and that conversation is the whole book.

The Core Argument

  • What really jams an organization isn't a skills gap or a bad process; it's self-deception. Self-deception means having a problem while sincerely believing you don't. Because you can't see it, every "fix" you come up with routes around the real cause — and often makes it worse.
  • The doorway into self-deception is self-betrayal. Whenever I sense I should do something for another person and don't, I start seeing the world in a way that excuses me: I magnify their faults and polish my own virtues. Over time, that self-excusing lens hardens into something I carry around as character.
  • People respond less to what we do than to how we're seeing them. The same behavior can come from seeing someone as a person or as an object, and it's the latter they pick up on. So changing behavior without changing how you see people barely works — while changing how you see them tends to change the behavior on its own.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Self-deception: when the doctors are the disease

What it is. Bud opens by telling Tom that Zagrum's work centers on "the one problem": self-deception. It isn't ordinary ignorance. Ordinary ignorance yields as soon as someone points out what you missed. Self-deception is an active, sincere refusal to see, one that keeps generating fresh reasons for not seeing. As the book puts it (paraphrased), the people who have the problem are the ones most resistant to the idea that they have it.

An example. The book borrows a chapter of medical history. In the 1840s, Vienna General Hospital had two maternity wards. In the one staffed by doctors and medical students, women died of childbed fever — a postpartum infection — at far higher rates than in the one staffed by midwives. The Hungarian physician Ignaz Semmelweis eventually worked out why: doctors were walking straight from the autopsy room into the delivery room, carrying material from cadavers on their hands. When he made them wash with a chlorinated solution, deaths fell sharply. Yet many of his colleagues refused to accept it, because it meant the ones killing the patients were the very people who believed they were saving them.

Why it matters. That's the anatomy of self-deception. As long as the doctors blamed "bad air," the patients' constitutions, or luck, the harder they worked, the more women died. Organizations run the same way. A manager who puts his team's troubles down to "people not taking ownership" will manage harder and harder while the team gets worse, because he's part of the cause, and every solution he devises steps around that fact.

How it changes the way you see. When a problem keeps coming back no matter what you throw at it, you start to wonder: am I the unwashed hands here? It's a much harder question than "why is he like this?" — and often the only useful one.

2. Self-betrayal: it starts with ignoring a small sense of what you ought to do

What it is. The book's definition, paraphrased: an act contrary to what I feel I should do for another person is an act of self-betrayal. This isn't some grave moral failing; it lives in the smallest moments. You see a colleague hauling boxes and something in you says "give him a hand," and you don't. You know a piece of information would help another team, you think "I should tell them," and you don't. The "should" here isn't an external rule — it's your own felt response, in the moment, to another human being.

An example. Bud tells a story on himself. One night the baby starts crying. He's awake, and his first thought is: I should get up and see to the baby so my wife, Nancy, can sleep. He doesn't. What happens next is the point. Lying there, he starts seeing Nancy in a whole new light: Why isn't she getting up? She's probably pretending to be asleep. How inconsiderate. And I've got important work tomorrow — I work hard, I'm the one who needs the rest. A few minutes earlier, Nancy was an exhausted person who deserved some care. A few minutes later, she's a lazy, thoughtless obstacle. Nancy didn't do anything. Bud did.

Why it matters. This is the engine of the whole book, and it reverses the order we assume: it's not that I don't help because she's bad; it's that I need her to be bad because I didn't help. "She's lazy" isn't the reason I stayed in bed; it's the reason I manufactured afterward. Psychologists call the underlying pressure cognitive dissonance — Leon Festinger's term for the discomfort we feel when our actions clash with our self-image, which we relieve by changing our beliefs. Self-betrayal is dissonance played out in a relationship: I can't hold "I'm a good person" and "I just let her down" at the same time, so I revise my view of her.

How it changes the way you see. The next time you catch yourself mentally itemizing someone's flaws, and enjoying it a little too much, try asking: did I just fail to do something I felt I should do for this person? That list of flaws is often the invoice for an excuse.

3. The box: seeing people as objects

What it is. "The box" is the book's central metaphor. After self-betrayal, I slip into self-deception, as if I've climbed into a box where all I can see are my own justifications. Inside it, other people stop being people with needs, fears, and hopes like mine and become objects — obstacles in my way, tools to be used, or irrelevant scenery. Outside the box, I see others as people, whose needs are as real and as legitimate as my own.

An example. One scene in the book sticks. On a flight with open seating, Bud has an empty seat beside him. He spreads his briefcase and newspaper across it, keeps his eyes on his reading, and quietly hopes nobody sits there. Every passenger coming down the aisle becomes, to him, someone who might take "his" seat — a threat. He isn't rude to anyone, but his way of seeing has already turned these people into objects. Contrast that with sitting in the same seat, noticing a flustered passenger dragging a bag down the aisle, and thinking: she needs a seat.

Why it matters. The trap is that people in the box almost never feel they're in a box — they just feel that other people are annoying. So the box isn't a mistake you can avoid by willpower; it's a way of seeing, and the "facts" you perceive have already been bent by it. That's why the book is about self-deception: the one deceiving you isn't someone else, it's your own point of view.

How it changes the way you see. You gain a quick self-check: right now, is this a person to me, or a thing in my way? If someone has shrunk to "the one who always drags the team down" or "that impossible client," you're probably already in the box.

4. Distortion in the box: inflating their faults, inflating my virtue, needing them to be bad

What it is. Bud sums up how the box warps perception (paraphrased): I inflate others' faults, inflate my own virtue, inflate the importance of whatever justifies me, and blame. Back to that night: Nancy's "laziness" swells, Bud's hard work gets polished, tomorrow's meeting suddenly becomes enormously important, and the whole thing is Nancy's fault.

Needing them to be bad. This is the book's sharpest cut. In the box, I need the other person to have a problem. If Nancy were actually considerate, my staying in bed wouldn't hold up — so the better she behaves, the more uncomfortable I get. Work is no different. A manager who has decided a report "isn't any good" often reacts to that report's success not with pleasure but with reasons to discount it — luck, somebody else's help — because the success threatens his justification.

Boxes we carry. The book adds that repeated self-betrayal hardens certain boxes into character, so that we carry them into new situations and can be in the box before any fresh self-betrayal has happened. These portable boxes usually form around self-justifying images: I'm the hard worker, I'm a devoted parent, I'm the one who really knows the business. Whoever threatens the image becomes an obstacle. Someone who prides himself on being the most dedicated person on the team may instinctively resent a colleague who leaves earlier and still gets everything done — not because the colleague has done anything wrong, but because her existence challenges his image.

How it changes the way you see. You start noticing that odd irritation at someone else's good performance. It's a sensitive alarm: I'm defending an image, and this person is a threat to it.

5. Collusion: in the box, we provoke the very behavior we complain about

What it is. The box isn't a solo affair. When I'm in the box toward you, I treat you in a way that carries an implicit accusation. You feel it, and you'll likely climb into your own box and respond defensively or in kind — which confirms exactly what I accused you of. In the book's words (paraphrased): in the box, we invite mutual mistreatment and obtain mutual justification; we collude in giving each other reason to stay in the box.

An example. Picture a manager who has decided a team member is unreliable (my illustration, not the book's). So he hovers, chases updates, and hands the important work to someone else. The team member feels untrusted, decides nothing will ever satisfy this boss, and starts coasting — doing the minimum, a little late. The manager looks at that and thinks: unreliable, just as I said. So he hovers more. Each complains about the other, and each is producing in the other precisely the behavior he can't stand. The real irony is that both need the loop: the manager needs an unreliable report to vindicate his tight control, and the report needs a controlling boss to excuse his coasting.

Why it matters. Collusion explains why some conflicts last for years. It isn't one person's fault; it's a self-sustaining system in which both sides collect the same prize: being right. As long as you're waiting for the other person to change first, the system keeps turning, because they're waiting for you.

How it changes the way you see. When you hear yourself say "he always does this," add a second question: does the way I treat him practically invite it? That isn't letting him off the hook; it's noticing that you're holding half the steering wheel.

6. Why behavioral fixes fail

What it is. A point the book drives home again and again: almost any behavior can be done either in the box or out of it. Praise can be genuine appreciation or a tactic. Listening can be an effort to understand or a pause while you load your rebuttal. Communication skills can help you understand people — or become a more refined way of proving you're right. What others sense is mainly how you're seeing them underneath.

An example. A manager stuck in the box goes on an active-listening course, comes back, and starts nodding and paraphrasing in meetings. His team figures it out within a week: he's found a new way of saying "I'm tolerating you." New behavior, old way of seeing, old results. The book accordingly lists the things that don't work while you're in the box (paraphrased): trying to change others, doing your best to cope with them, leaving, communicating more, adopting new skills or techniques, and changing your own behavior. None of these is bad in itself, but done from inside the box, they're just a new posture in the same box.

Why it matters. This cuts against most management training, which assumes the problem is a skills deficit. Arbinger's answer: most people aren't short on skills; they're short on seeing other people as people. Change that, and a great deal of behavior adjusts without being drilled.

How it changes the way you see. You grow wary of learning a script to "handle" someone. If the starting point is handling him, you've already made him an object — and he'll probably feel it.

7. Getting out of the box

What it is. If the box is a way of seeing, you don't get out by trying harder; you get out through a shift in the seeing itself. The book's key insight is that we can be in the box toward one person while out of the box toward another, and that gives us somewhere to stand. In the moment I stop resisting someone and feel their weight as a person again, I'm already out. The remaining question is whether I'll act on what I now feel.

How it happens. It often begins when we start doubting our own virtue: Am I really as blameless as I've been telling myself? How would this look from where he's standing? It also happens when someone treats us as a person — which is exactly how Bud treats Tom. He doesn't accuse or lecture; he just tells stories about his own boxes. Over the course of the story, Tom comes to see that his view of his own family, including his son Todd, is boxed in too, and has been for a long time. The first step out is rarely a grand decision; usually it's a small "maybe I'm wrong."

The book's practical list (paraphrased). Don't look for others' boxes; look for your own. Don't accuse others of being in the box; try to stay out yourself. Don't give up on yourself when you find you've been in the box; keep trying. Don't deny it when you have been; apologize, then keep moving forward. Don't focus on what others are doing wrong; focus on what you can do right to help. Don't worry about whether others are helping you; worry about whether you're helping them. And a very practical one: don't use the vocabulary — "the box" and the rest — on people who haven't learned it themselves, or it becomes a brand-new weapon.

Why it matters. Getting out of the box doesn't mean becoming a pushover. Out of the box, you can still criticize, refuse, hold people accountable, even fire someone. The difference is whether you're dealing with a person who deserves fair treatment or an obstacle to be beaten.

How it changes the way you see. You realize the lever on the relationship has been in your hand all along. You can't get someone else out of the box, but you can get out first — and once you do, the collusion loop is missing a gear.

The Skeleton

The argument compresses into a chain: (1) the deep cause of people problems is self-deception — those with the problem can't see it; (2) self-deception comes from self-betrayal — having gone against my sense of what I owe someone, I need a world that excuses me; (3) that excusing world is the box — others become objects, their faults swell, my virtues shine, and I even need them to be bad; (4) boxes are contagious — mine invites you into yours, and we collude, supplying each other with reasons to stay put; (5) so patching behavior doesn't work — the same behavior can be done in the box; (6) the only way out is to change how I see people first, turning from "they are the problem" to "where have I become the problem?"

What does it rest on? Not experiments or data, but a series of scenes from the parable that readers can't help recognizing — the crying baby, the empty seat on the plane, the colleagues trading blame. The book persuades by landing a hit, not by proving a case. Few people read the middle-of-the-night scene and honestly say they've never done it.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "The book says everything is my fault." It doesn't deny that other people have problems too. Its point is that while I'm in the box, I can't judge how big their problem really is or how much I've contributed. Out of the box, you may still conclude the other person was wrong — but the judgment is more accurate, and no longer driven by a need for them to be the villain.

Misreading 2: "Getting out of the box means being nice." As noted, the book is explicit that firm, even tough, action can be taken out of the box. What it objects to is the way of seeing behind the action, not firmness itself.

Criticism 1: A parable with almost no evidence. The whole case rests on a fictional story — no experiments, no data. Arbinger mostly cites client success stories rather than peer-reviewed research. Related findings in psychology — cognitive dissonance, mentioned above; the self-serving bias, our habit of crediting ourselves for successes and blaming circumstances or others for failures; and the self-justification that social psychologists Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson document in Mistakes Were Made (but Not by Me) — point the same way, confirming that people invent reasons for their own behavior. But none of them directly tests the full self-betrayal-to-box mechanism.

Criticism 2: It can feel unfalsifiable. The concept has a built-in trap: if you say "I don't think I'm in the box," that can itself be read as evidence that you are. A theory that can absorb every objection is hard to test. For readers, that means it works better as a mirror for self-examination than as an instrument for diagnosing others — which is, to be fair, what the book itself advises.

Criticism 3: Intellectual roots and religious background. Arbinger's framework comes from Terry Warner's philosophy, set out at greater length in his book Bonds That Make Us Free. Warner draws on the French philosopher Emmanuel Levinas, who argued that the face of another person makes an ethical claim on us that we can't escape — which maps neatly onto the book's "sense that I should do something for someone." Warner taught at Brigham Young University, which is run by The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, and Arbinger is based in Utah. Some commentators note that ideas like the call of conscience and self-justification after betraying it echo that tradition's moral outlook. The book itself is written in secular terms, but readers deserve to know the background.

Criticism 4: It can be turned against victims. For someone facing real bullying or injustice, "maybe the problem is you" can be a second injury — or a tool a boss uses to shut down legitimate complaints. The framework's greatest strength and its greatest danger are the same thing: it turns all your attention back on yourself. It's well suited to conflicts where both sides are trading accusations, and poorly suited to counseling someone who is being mistreated.

Criticism 5: It's also a training product. Arbinger is a commercial consultancy, and the book doubles, in a sense, as an advertisement for its programs; the image of Zagrum as a company that runs on box language mirrors what Arbinger sells to clients. That doesn't make the insights wrong, but it's fair to discount the success stories a little.

Beyond This Book: The Anatomy of Peace

Arbinger's 2006 follow-up, The Anatomy of Peace, carries the same theory out of the office and into families and larger conflicts. It's set at Camp Moriah, a wilderness program in Arizona for troubled teenagers. While the kids head into the backcountry, their parents stay for a two-day workshop led by Yusuf al-Falah, a Palestinian Muslim, and Avi Rozen, an Israeli Jew — two men whose histories should have made them enemies. Lou Herbert from the first book reappears as one of the parents.

A heart at peace vs. a heart at war

Here "out of the box / in the box" becomes a plainer pair: a heart at peace and a heart at war. With a heart at peace, I see the other as a person; with a heart at war, I see them as an object — obstacle, tool, or irrelevance. The key distinction (paraphrased): outward behavior can be warlike while the heart is at peace, and outward behavior can be peaceful while the heart is at war. A parent can firmly send a child to a treatment program while seeing that child as a person throughout — or can be outwardly gentle and accommodating while privately regarding the child as the thing that ruined their life. The child feels the second.

Four box styles

The book sorts common self-justifications into four "box styles" — the self-images we most often carry around:

The four box styles (summarized from the book; wording paraphrased). They look very different, but they share one feature: the attention is all on me, and other people are props in my drama.

The one worth lingering on is worse-than. Self-deprecation looks humble, but it's still a box: it lets me off the hook for responding to others ("I'd only mess it up anyway"), and it casts other people as the advantaged ones responsible for my situation. The difference between humility and self-deprecation is whether your attention is on others or has circled back to yourself.

Collusion at home

This book puts collusion in its most painful setting. Parents decide their child is defiant and ungrateful, so they control, lecture, and punish. The child decides the parents care only about appearances and don't understand him, so he rebels harder — and the rebellion confirms the parents' verdict. Each side feels like the victim; each is gathering evidence against the other. Yusuf and Avi use the conflict between their own peoples to show that the same mechanism scales up to nations: each side needs the other to be the villain so that its own hostility looks justified.

The Peace Pyramid

The book's most practical tool is a pyramid (presented as a peacemaking or influence pyramid). From bottom to top, roughly (level names paraphrased): (1) get out of the box — have a heart at peace; (2) build relationships with people who have influence with the other person; (3) build a relationship with the other person; (4) listen and learn; (5) teach and communicate; (6) correct. Most of us meet conflict by starting at the top — criticizing, lecturing, punishing. The pyramid's point is that far less of your time should go to the top than to the base, and a problem at one level is usually solved at the level below it. Correction fails because the other person didn't understand; they didn't understand because I didn't listen first; I didn't listen because there was no relationship; and underneath all of it is whether my own heart is at peace.

Taken together, the two books are the complete Arbinger: Leadership and Self-Deception explains how the box forms (self-betrayal and self-deception), and The Anatomy of Peace explains how the box operates between people and how to dismantle it, layer by layer (the four styles, collusion, and the pyramid).

The Book in Ten Sentences

1. The biggest problem in organizations is self-deception: the people with the problem are the ones who can't see it.

2. Acting against your own sense of what you should do for another person is self-betrayal (paraphrased).

3. I don't refuse to help because they're bad; I need them to be bad because I refused to help.

4. In the box, others are objects — obstacles, tools, or scenery; out of the box, they're people as real as I am.

5. In the box, I inflate others' faults, inflate my own virtue, and blame (paraphrased).

6. Hardened boxes travel with us, built around a self-image we feel we have to defend.

7. In the box, we invite mutual mistreatment and obtain mutual justification (paraphrased).

8. Almost any behavior can be done in the box or out of it — people respond to how you're seeing them.

9. The outward can be at war while the heart is at peace; before you correct, listen; before you listen, build the relationship; before anything, get out of your own box.

10. Don't look for others' boxes; look for your own: you can't get anyone else out of the box, but you can get out first.
