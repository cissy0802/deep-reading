The Art of Worldly Wisdom & Maxims

The Art of Worldly Wisdom (Oráculo manual y arte de prudencia) · Baltasar Gracián · 1647 & Maxims (Réflexions ou sentences et maximes morales) · François de La Rochefoucauld · 1665

中文 →

In One Sentence

Two seventeenth-century aphorists looked at the same world — a vast theater run on appearances, interest and self-love; the Spanish Jesuit Gracián teaches you how to play your part in that theater with prudence, while the French duke La Rochefoucauld walks you backstage and tells you that even the virtues you think are sincere are mostly costume. One is a manual for wearing masks, the other a manual for removing them, and only together do they add up to a complete clear-sightedness about human nature.

Where It Sits

Gracián (1601–1658) was a Jesuit of the Spanish Baroque; his Oráculo manual ("pocket oracle and art of prudence") is three hundred maxims on how to live among people, which Schopenhauer translated into German himself. La Rochefoucauld (1613–1680) was a great French nobleman on the losing side of the Fronde, the civil wars of the 1640s and 1650s; his Maxims went through five editions in his lifetime, finally numbering about five hundred, and are the sharpest book in the French tradition of the moralistes — writers such as Montaigne, Pascal and La Bruyère who dissected the human heart in aphorisms and essays. Nietzsche admired them both enormously.

The Core Argument

  • Gracián: the world is a court, and living in it is a craft that can be learned. Human dealings are full of malice and calculation; talent and virtue that don't know how to present themselves, or when to hold back, simply get swallowed. So you must cultivate prudence (prudencia) — the practical wisdom of judging, in a concrete situation, when to speak, how much, and to whom. But the craft still ends in virtue: the last of the three hundred maxims reads, "In a word, be a saint."
  • La Rochefoucauld: our virtues are, most often, only vices in disguise. (The epigraph of the Maxims, paraphrased.) Behind almost every noble-looking act — generosity, modesty, gratitude, mercy, friendship — you can find self-love (amour-propre) at work, and the first person self-love deceives is ourselves.
  • The two books mirror each other. Gracián assumes you can see the situation clearly and manage your own appearance; La Rochefoucauld asks whether the "you" doing the managing isn't itself blinded by self-love. One gives you strategy; the other gives you suspicion of the strategist.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Prudence: living well is a craft, not a set of principles

Gracián's prudence is not "caution." It is close to what Aristotle called practical wisdom (phronesis): the ability to judge what the fitting action is with these particular people, at this particular moment. You can't get it by memorizing rules, only through observation, experience and a feel for timing. So the form of the book is itself an argument. Three hundred maxims, each a short heading plus a few lines of commentary, sometimes contradicting one another — one advises acting sometimes on your first intention and sometimes on your second, so that rivals can never read you. The contradictions aren't carelessness. They say: no rule is right in every situation; judgment is the real rule.

His worldview is put most bluntly at the opening of maxim 13: "Man's life is a warfare against the malice of man" (paraphrased). That was the backdrop of Baroque Spain — an empire in decline, a court where everyone studied the moods of those above, the Inquisition everywhere. The key word of the age was desengaño, disillusionment: seeing through the world's pretenses, not in order to despair, but in order to act with clearer eyes.

An analogy: prudence is like the feel of a seasoned chess player who doesn't win by memorizing opening theory but by sensing the position — when to press, when to wait, when to look weaker than you are. Gracián's maxims are fragments of that feel: each is a good move in some position, and you have to learn to recognize the position yourself.

The form is also the method. Gracián prized agudeza, wit or conceptual sharpness (he wrote a whole treatise on the art of wit), packing the most meaning into the fewest words. One of his lines became a Spanish proverb: "Good things, when short, are twice as good" (Lo bueno, si breve, dos veces bueno). It is a theory of style and a piece of social advice at once: say too much, and you hand over too much of yourself.

How it changes the way you see things: you realize that much conventional wisdom fails because it is treated as universal principle, whereas real social skill is knowing which piece of wisdom applies here and now. Gracián doesn't hand you answers; he trains the way you ask questions.

2. Appearance and reserve: things pass for what they seem, not for what they are

The most quoted line in the book is "Things do not pass for what they are, but for what they seem" (Las cosas no pasan por lo que son, sino por lo que parecen, paraphrased). The maxim's heading is "Do, and be seen doing," and in it Gracián says that to have worth and know how to show it is to be worth twice over; what isn't seen is as if it didn't exist (paraphrased). This is not a license to fake. It is a cool observation: other people can't see your inner qualities, only what you present; if you don't tend to the presentation, you leave your reputation entirely to their prejudices.

The counterpart of showing is holding back. Gracián urges reserve again and again: don't finish everything at once or say everything at once; "keep matters in suspense," so people go on expecting more of you; "put your intentions in cipher," so no one can see at a glance what you want — because a person whose aims are visible is like a card player whose hand is face up, at everyone's mercy. He has a line of perfect calibration: "Without lying, do not tell the whole truth" (paraphrased). And another: "Think with the few and speak with the many" (paraphrased) — you may hold independent judgments, but there's no need to display them in every room and make enemies of everyone.

An analogy: a good negotiator never names a number first, and a good novelist never solves the mystery in chapter one. Reserve isn't deception; it is controlling the pace at which information is released — once you've given everything away, you've given away all your leverage.

Why it matters: Gracián exposes an asymmetry in social life. The less you know about others and the more they know about you, the more you are at their disposal. Reserve buys you room inside that asymmetry.

How it changes the way you see things: you start to separate honesty from transparency. Honesty is not saying what's false; transparency is laying everything out. The first is a virtue. The second, in a world where not everyone means you well, is often just naivety.

3. The micro-mechanics of power: dependence, outshining, and everyone's lever

Gracián's observations of court power are unsettlingly precise. Three are typical:

  • Make people depend on you. Dependence, he says, gets you far more than courtesy; the man who has quenched his thirst turns his back on the well (paraphrased). So the wise keep others needing them, rather than satisfying every need at once — satisfy it, and you're no longer of use.
  • Don't outshine your superior. Appearing more brilliant than those above you is foolish, even fatal; people will accept being surpassed in luck or temperament, but hardly in intelligence — least of all those in power. The stars have light of their own, yet never dare to shine brighter than the sun (paraphrased). The first of Robert Greene's 48 Laws of Power is practically a modern rewrite of this maxim.
  • Find each person's lever. Gracián's word is torcedor — originally the stick used to twist a rope tight and apply pressure. Everyone has something that moves them: vanity, interest, fear, some fixation. Find it, and you've found how to influence that person.

The advice sounds cold, but it rests on a premise: you are inside a power structure you can neither leave nor reform. At court, not knowing these things doesn't make you purer; it just gets you thrown out sooner.

How it changes the way you see things: you notice the same mechanics running in offices, families, any group with a hierarchy, and many puzzles of the "I did the best work, so why wasn't I recognized?" kind find their answer here. Talent is necessary, but how it is perceived by those above you is a separate matter.

4. Human means as if there were no divine ones: prudence in the service of virtue

Read only the first three ideas and Gracián looks like a Spanish Machiavelli. But he was a Jesuit, and the Jesuit spirit was to act within the world rather than flee it. One maxim captures the double vision perfectly: "Use human means as though there were no divine ones, and divine means as though there were no human ones" (paraphrased). Work as hard as if everything depended on you; pray and live virtuously as if everything depended on God.

So Gracián's prudence has a boundary: it is the art of means, not a substitute for ends. He insists that the person of integrity (hombre de entereza) would rather lose everything than abandon what is right, and in the last of the three hundred maxims he gathers up the whole book: "In a word, be a saint" (En una palabra, santo). Virtue, he says, is the chain that links all perfections; it makes a person lovable and admirable in life and remembered after death.

An analogy: prudence is a sharp knife, and virtue decides whether you use it to cook or to wound. Gracián teaches you to sharpen the knife; he never says you may use it to hurt people.

How it changes the way you see things: you don't have to choose between being a naive good person and a shrewd scoundrel. Shrewdness is a capacity; goodness is a direction. Gracián's ideal is the person who has both — el discreto, the discerning one — who sees human nature's dark corners and still chooses to stand in the light.

5. Self-love: La Rochefoucauld's master key

Turn to La Rochefoucauld and every analysis converges on a single concept: self-love (amour-propre) — love of oneself, and the resulting habit of filing everything under "what's in it for me?" and "how does this make me look?" It isn't healthy self-respect; it is closer to an all-pervading, shape-shifting egocentrism. His second maxim states it: "Self-love is the greatest of all flatterers" (paraphrased).

In the earliest edition he included a long portrait of self-love (cut from later editions). Self-love is like a creature living in the depths of the soul, changing shape in the dark; no one can see the bottom of its abyss, and it is opaque even to itself; it will give itself up to reach its ends, and even join forces with its own enemies (paraphrased). The point: what makes self-love frightening is not its strength but its concealment — it can dress up as its opposite.

There is an intellectual background here. The salon La Rochefoucauld frequented, that of Madame de Sablé, was close to Jansenism — a Catholic movement, rooted in Augustine and centered on the abbey of Port-Royal near Paris, which held that human nature is deeply corrupted by original sin and can be saved only by God's grace. In the Augustinian tradition, human "virtues" without grace are really forms of pride and self-love. La Rochefoucauld turned that theological verdict into cool psychological observation.

How it changes the way you see things: you start catching self-love red-handed in yourself — why do I care so much about this? Because it's right, or because my pride is at stake? The question is uncomfortable, and it's where clear sight begins.

6. An archaeology of motives: virtues taken apart

La Rochefoucauld's method is to take a virtue apart and look at what's inside. The effect is often like a slap:

  • Modesty: "Refusing praise is a wish to be praised twice" (paraphrased).
  • Gratitude: "The gratitude of most men is merely a secret desire to receive greater favors" (paraphrased).
  • Mercy: the clemency of princes is often just a policy for winning the people's affection (paraphrased).
  • Moderation: the moderation of fortunate people comes from the calm that good luck gives their temper (paraphrased) — not character, circumstance.
  • Sympathy: "We all have strength enough to bear the misfortunes of others" (paraphrased). And one he later suppressed but which spread everywhere: in the misfortunes of our best friends, we always find something that doesn't altogether displease us (paraphrased).
  • Hypocrisy: "Hypocrisy is the homage vice pays to virtue" (paraphrased) — precisely because virtue is universally respected, vice has to dress up as it.

His summary: "We would often be ashamed of our finest actions if the world could see all the motives that produce them" (paraphrased). And: "Virtues are lost in self-interest as rivers are lost in the sea" (paraphrased).

Notice his wording: almost always "often," "most," "most men." La Rochefoucauld is not saying virtue doesn't exist; he is saying pure virtue is extremely rare, and most of what we think we have is mixed with something else. And he writes "we," not "they" — he counts himself in. That makes the Maxims not a book of insults but a mirror.

How it changes the way you see things: you take a second look at every performance of selflessness, your own included. More usefully, you become more forgiving of other people's mixed motives — doing good for impure reasons is the norm, not the exception, and demanding pure motives can stop people doing any good at all.

7. The mind is the dupe of the heart: self-deception

La Rochefoucauld's deepest insight is not that we fool others but that we fool ourselves first. "The mind is always the dupe of the heart" (L'esprit est toujours la dupe du cœur, paraphrased): we believe we're judging rationally, but desire has already decided, and reason goes looking for justifications afterward.

He proves it with a string of small observations. "Everyone complains of his memory, and no one complains of his judgment" (paraphrased) — we'll admit to minor failings of capacity, never to doubts about our judgment. "We are never as happy or as unhappy as we imagine" (paraphrased). "Philosophy easily triumphs over past and future ills, but present ills triumph over philosophy" (paraphrased). "Some people would never have fallen in love if they had never heard of love" (paraphrased) — even our most intimate passions may be scripts learned from others.

An analogy: if Gracián teaches you to be a good actor, La Rochefoucauld points out that the actor most lost in the role is the one who has forgotten he is acting. Wear a mask in front of others long enough and you mistake it for your face.

How it changes the way you see things: the real prize isn't seeing through other people; it's keeping a little doubt about your own certainties — the moment I feel surest that I'm acting from pure principle may be exactly the moment to check.

The Spine of the Book

The two books share a starting point: human society is a theater of appearances, driven by interest and self-love rather than by the virtues people profess. This was lived experience for the aristocratic and courtly world of seventeenth-century Europe. Gracián watched the Spanish empire decline and the court tear at itself; La Rochefoucauld went through the betrayals and defeat of the Fronde (he was wounded in the face in battle and nearly lost his sight).

From that shared starting point they head in two complementary directions. Gracián looks outward: since the world is like this, learn to act in it — manage appearances, control information, understand the mechanics of power, read each person's motives — but let all of it serve integrity, and in the end be a saint. What he demonstrates is that clear sight and virtue can coexist; prudence is the form virtue takes to survive in an imperfect world. La Rochefoucauld looks inward: since the world is like this, we too are part of it, and the eyes we judge others with are themselves tinted by self-love. His method of proof isn't argument but a string of observations you can't deny — when you read "we all have strength enough to bear the misfortunes of others" and smile in recognition, that smile is the evidence.

Read together, they yield a complete stance: see human nature clearly, your own included, and still choose to act with grace and integrity. Read only Gracián and you risk becoming a shrewd manipulator who forgets that self-love can blind you too; read only La Rochefoucauld and you risk becoming a cynic who sees through everything and does nothing. One supplies the method, the other the antidote.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: "Gracián is just a Spanish Machiavelli." They resemble each other in describing coolly how power works. The fundamental difference is that Machiavelli (The Prince) treats political success as the end in itself and accepts that a ruler must sometimes learn not to be good, whereas Gracián always ranks prudence beneath virtue, and his three hundred maxims end with "be a saint." Reading the book as a pure power manual is the biggest misreading produced by modern bestsellers, Greene's 48 Laws among them.

Misreading two: "La Rochefoucauld thinks human nature is wholly evil and virtue doesn't exist." His wording is almost always "often" and "most," and he leaves room for a few genuine virtues. What he attacks is our complacency about our own virtue, not virtue itself.

Misreading three: "These maxims are a toolkit of social tricks to apply as written." A maxim is compressed judgment; applied out of context, it often backfires. Gracián's maxims contradict one another precisely to force you to judge for yourself.

The strongest criticisms:

  • La Rochefoucauld is unfalsifiable. If every good deed can be explained as self-love, then "self-love" explains everything and therefore nothing — any counterexample can be reinterpreted as a deeper self-love. Voltaire, in The Age of Louis XIV, credited the little book with doing a great deal to shape French taste, but noted that it contains "almost only one truth: that self-love is the motive of everything" (paraphrased). Later psychology complicated the picture. A series of experiments by the psychologist C. Daniel Batson found that when empathy is aroused, people's willingness to help is hard to explain entirely by self-interest, supporting the existence of genuine altruism; yet Batson's separate "moral hypocrisy" experiments showed that people often want to appear fair rather than actually be fair. La Rochefoucauld was half right.
  • Class and grievance. Critics note that La Rochefoucauld's pessimism owes something to the bitterness of a defeated aristocrat — politically sidelined, used to betrayal at court — generalizing that experience into a universal human nature.
  • Gracián's moral ambiguity. Even granting his virtuous destination, much of his advice — cultivate dependence, hide your aims, find people's weak points — is hard to separate from manipulation in practice. Between "prudence for virtue's sake" and "manipulation in virtue's name" lies only a person's own account of himself — which is exactly the account La Rochefoucauld says self-love is best at doctoring.
  • Elitism. Both books are written for the few: Gracián's discreto, La Rochefoucauld's salon readers. They care about survival and face in courts and high society, and say almost nothing about ordinary people's lives or whether the institutions themselves are just. Gracián himself was disciplined by the Jesuits for publishing without the order's permission and removed from his teaching post late in life — the master of prudence didn't entirely manage to protect himself.

Crossovers

  • Evolutionary biology · Trivers on self-deception: The evolutionary biologist Robert Trivers argued that we deceive ourselves the better to deceive others — someone who truly believes leaks none of a liar's tells. That is almost an evolutionary gloss on "the mind is the dupe of the heart." The difference: La Rochefoucauld exposes self-deception as a moral flaw; Trivers treats it as an adaptation.
  • Neuroscience · Gazzaniga's "interpreter": Studying split-brain patients, the neuroscientist Michael Gazzaniga found that the left hemisphere will instantly invent a plausible reason for an action the right hemisphere initiated — and believe it completely. That is La Rochefoucauld's "the heart decides, reason finds reasons later," caught in the lab. Unlike self-love, though, the interpreter is normal brain function, not necessarily vanity.
  • Buddhism · self-grasping: Buddhism traces much suffering to self-grasping — clinging to an ever-changing stream of body and mind as a permanent "I," then craving and defending on its behalf. La Rochefoucauld's self-love has the same structure: every act orbits a protected self. The disanalogy: Buddhism holds that practice can see through and release self-grasping; La Rochefoucauld's Augustinian tradition holds that without grace no one escapes self-love by their own efforts.
  • Economics · signaling theory: The economist Michael Spence's signaling theory shows how, under asymmetric information, people convey hard-to-observe qualities through observable signals such as degrees. "Things pass for what they seem" is that structure, and Gracián's showing and hiding is signal management. The difference: Spence stresses that signals must be costly to be credible; Gracián's appearances are often cheap.
  • Daoism · Laozi's hidden treasure: The Records of the Grand Historian has Laozi telling Confucius that a good merchant hides his goods as if his shop were empty, and a person of great virtue looks almost foolish. Same family as Gracián's reserve and "don't outshine the master." The difference: Laozi conceals to shed pride and desire; Gracián conceals first of all to survive at court.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. The world is a theater of appearances, run on interest and self-love rather than on the virtues people profess.

2. Gracián: living well is a craft, and judgment is the real rule — his maxims contradict each other precisely to make you judge.

3. Things pass for what they seem, not for what they are; worth that knows how to show itself is worth twice over. (Paraphrased)

4. Without lying, don't tell the whole truth; think with the few, speak with the many. (Paraphrased)

5. The man who has quenched his thirst turns his back on the well — dependence lasts longer than satisfaction. (Paraphrased)

6. Use human means as if there were no divine ones, and divine means as if there were no human ones; in a word, be a saint. Prudence is how virtue survives in an imperfect world.

7. La Rochefoucauld: our virtues are, most often, only vices in disguise. (Paraphrased)

8. Refusing praise is wanting to be praised twice; hypocrisy is the homage vice pays to virtue. (Paraphrased)

9. The mind is always the dupe of the heart: everyone complains of his memory, no one of his judgment. (Paraphrased)

10. Learn only Gracián and you become a manipulator; learn only La Rochefoucauld and you become a cynic — see human nature clearly, your own included, and still act with grace and integrity.
