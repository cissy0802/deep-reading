The Trial

Der Process · Franz Kafka · written 1914–15, published posthumously 1925

中文 →

In One Sentence

A man is arrested on the morning of his thirtieth birthday and never told what he is charged with. He is not locked up; he keeps his job, keeps his flat, keeps flirting — he simply has to work out for himself what he did wrong. A year later two men walk him to a quarry and kill him like a dog. The book is not about bureaucracy being annoying. It is about something colder: guilt is not a fact about you but a relation that can be imposed on you — and once imposed, everything you do to clear yourself becomes evidence that it was warranted.

Where It Sits

Franz Kafka (1883–1924) was a Prague Jew who wrote in German, held a doctorate in law, and spent his working life at the Workers' Accident Insurance Institute for the Kingdom of Bohemia — processing injury claims, disability gradings, appeals and rejections. He was not mocking the machine from outside; he was the man inside it with the rubber stamp. He wrote The Trial in 1914–15, never finished it, never published it, and left instructions for his friend Max Brod to burn the manuscripts. Brod disobeyed, assembled the loose bundles of chapters, and published the novel in 1925. A century later English has a word for what Kafka left behind: Kafkaesque.

The Central Claims

  • An accusation can have no content and still work perfectly. The court never tells Josef K. what he is accused of. That is not sloppiness, it is the mechanism: with no charge you cannot mount a defence, so all you can do is search yourself. The labour of finding the offence is transferred from the prosecution to the defendant.
  • The process is the punishment. The German Prozess means both "trial" and "process." There is no moment of judgment in this book, only hearings, corridors, files and waiting. The painter Titorelli puts it flatly (paraphrased): the verdict does not arrive all at once; the proceedings gradually turn into the verdict.
  • The final product is not pain but shame. K.'s last words are "Like a dog!" — and the narrator adds that it was as though the shame would outlive him. The machine's triumph is that a man who never learned his charge has nonetheless accepted the form of being guilty.

The Core Concepts, One by One

The nameless charge: reversing the burden of proof

The novel opens (paraphrased): "Someone must have been telling lies about Josef K., for without having done anything wrong he was arrested one morning." Two warders barge into his lodgings and eat his breakfast; an inspector informs him that he is under arrest but may carry on with his life and go to the bank as usual. That is the design at the heart of the machine: it does not take your freedom, it simply attaches to you a question mark whose content is never supplied.

Why is this more effective than a cell? Because it makes defence logically impossible. You do not know what to rebut, so the only available move is to go back over your own life. K.'s lawyer explains that the plea he must submit has to account for his entire existence — every event, every trivial act, each one justified. Notice the reversal: in an ordinary court the prosecution must find your crime; here that job has been handed to you, and you do it with great diligence. K. begins in arrogance, treating the whole thing as a farce he can clear up in a sentence or two. The further in he goes, the more he becomes a man permanently auditing himself.

Milan Kundera named the mechanism in The Art of the Novel (paraphrased): it is not the offence that seeks out the punishment, but the punished who go looking for their offence. This is not an abstraction. It happens constantly: an account is suspended for "violation of our policies," unspecified; an internal investigation opens and nobody says into what; someone you love goes cold and will not say why. What do you do? You cannot rebut — there is nothing to rebut — so you start remembering, auditing, drafting the indictment on their behalf. And the charge you write yourself is almost always heavier than any real one. Having read this chapter, you gain a defensive reflex: when someone accuses you but refuses to say of what, that refusal is not hesitation. It is the technique.

Three kinds of acquittal: why the case can never end

The closest thing to an operating manual comes from Titorelli, a painter who makes his living doing portraits of judges and has therefore become the machine's folk expert. He tells K. that a defendant has three roads open to him, and that none of them leads out.

RoadWhat it isThe price
Definite acquittalThe case genuinely ends; the file is destroyedExists only in legend — he has never seen one
Apparent acquittalLow-level judges sign you free; you go home and resume your lifeThe file keeps circulating; one morning the arrest comes again, and again
ProtractionKeep the case pinned at the lowest stage with constant small effortYou must tend it for the rest of your life; it never wins, and never stops

Titorelli's three roads (schematic): one does not exist, one relapses, one costs you the remainder of your life.

This is the most practically useful page in the book. What it exposes is a general structure: when an institution has no terminus, being kept in progress is the verdict. You need not be convicted, only suspended indefinitely — pre-trial detention, an open-ended compliance review, an appeal permanently "under consideration," a visa held in administrative processing for three years. None of these ever declare you guilty. They simply decline to conclude. And a human life is finite, so not concluding is the same as losing.

It changes the question you ask about risk: not "could this go against me?" but, first, "is there any state in which this ends?" A procedure with no stopping condition is already a sentence, however courteous each step of it is.

The court in the attic: power needs no grandeur, only ubiquity

K.'s first hearing is in a tenement in a poor suburb; the courtroom is up in the attic. The air is thick and hot, the gallery is packed, and when K. opens one of the so-called law books on the magistrate's table it turns out to be pornography. The court's offices are permanently lodged in attics everywhere, with laundry strung across the passages and defendants waiting on benches in the corridor, all of them looking unwell — because, as he is told, the attic air does not agree with the accused.

This is the detail readers skim past and the one that does the most damage: Kafka's power is not majestic, it is shabby. No marble, no dome, no centre you could storm and confront. It lives upstairs in the building you already live in. And it goes further: everyone in the novel turns out to be connected to the court — his landlady, the girl next door, his uncle, his deputy manager at the bank, the painter, the lawyer's nurse. Everybody knows a judge. Everybody can offer him a way in.

The coldest scene takes place inside K.'s own bank. He opens the door of a lumber room and finds the two warders who arrested him being flogged by a whipper — because K. complained that they ate his breakfast. He tries to buy them off; it does not work. The next day he opens the same door and finds the same three men, in the same posture, the same scene running again, unchanged. The horror is not the cruelty. It is that there is nobody behind it: no villain, no author, just an unattended loop that happens to be installed in the building he works in.

This rewires how you picture institutional harm. We assume oppression comes from a bad man, and that finding and toppling him ends it. Kafka points at the far more common case: every node is occupied by a minor functionary, every functionary says this is not my department, and the machine grinds the person up all the same. What Hannah Arendt would later name, looking at Eichmann, the banality of evil is the same machine seen from the operator's end — except that Kafka had already drawn it thirty years earlier from the end that gets ground up.

Huld and Block: everyone who lives off your case needs it to continue

K.'s uncle finds him a lawyer, Huld — a name that in German means "grace, favour," and Kafka never chose a name innocently. Huld is permanently bedridden and receives his clients from that bed; his expertise is not law but acquaintance, gossip, and the maintenance of favours with officials. He is always working on the plea. He never finishes it. He never lets K. read it.

Then K. meets Block, a tradesman. Block's case has been running five years. He has retained five lawyers, has all but ruined his business paying for them, and now lives in a maid's room in the lawyer's house, on call at any hour, kneeling by the bed to be lectured, thrilled by a scrap of faint praise. Block is a trailer for K.'s future: what is left of a man after his case has eaten him. He has no life any more. His entire identity is a proceeding.

What this section is really describing is an incentive structure. Anyone paid by the process has their interest tied to its length, not its outcome. Defence counsel is not even formally permitted in this court, merely "tolerated" — so the only product the lawyer can actually sell is hope: a little longer, a few more words in the right ear, we're nearly there. To keep buying that hope the defendant hands over money, time and dignity by instalments. This is not invention. It is the standing condition of any long-horizon dispute — litigation, appeals, claims, grievance procedures, and the whole industry that has grown up around "we'll get your account reinstated."

The transferable move: before anything else, work out how the person helping you gets paid. If their income rises with time and vanishes with resolution, their advice is already pointed in a direction, however sincerely it is given.

Before the Law: the door was made for you alone, and you did not go in

The intellectual summit of the book is its penultimate chapter. K. has been sent to the cathedral to show an Italian client around; the client never comes. In the empty building a prison chaplain — who says he belongs to the court — calls him by name and tells him a story. Kafka published this piece separately in his lifetime, as Before the Law (Vor dem Gesetz):

A man from the country comes to the door of the Law and asks to be admitted. The doorkeeper says: not now. Later, perhaps? Possibly, says the doorkeeper, but not now. The door stands open; when the man stoops to peer through, the doorkeeper laughs and says: if it tempts you so much, try going in despite my prohibition — but note that I am powerful, and I am only the lowest doorkeeper. From hall to hall there are others, each more powerful than the last; even I cannot bear to look at the third.

So the man sits down and waits. He waits years, then decades. He bribes the doorkeeper with everything he brought; the doorkeeper takes it all, remarking only that he accepts it so the man will not feel he has left anything untried. The man grows old and his eyesight fails, and at the end he gathers a lifetime of wondering into one question: everyone strives to reach the Law, so how is it that in all these years no one else has come asking to be let in? The doorkeeper roars into his failing ear: "No one else could be admitted here, because this entrance was made only for you. I am now going to shut it." (paraphrased)

The parable ends and the chaplain and K. immediately start arguing over it like rival commentators. Did the doorkeeper deceive him? Or was he simply doing his duty? Perhaps the doorkeeper is the deceived one — bound for a lifetime to a door he can neither enter nor leave, posted there by someone else. For every reading the chaplain offers, he offers another that cancels it, and then delivers two devastating lines (paraphrased): "The scripture is unalterable, and the commentaries are often only an expression of the commentators' despair," and "you do not have to accept everything as true, you only have to accept it as necessary." K.'s answer is the clearest sentence in the novel: "A melancholy conclusion. It turns lying into a universal principle." (paraphrased)

Why does this matter so much? Because it states the whole book's structure out loud. A man sits at his own private door waiting for a permission that never comes, until he dies — and the door was open the entire time, and he never dared. K.'s position is exactly isomorphic: he is waiting for a charge, an explanation, an absolution, and these do not arrive not because they are being withheld but because they do not exist. That the door was made for him alone means the ordeal is bespoke: no co-defendants, no collective, no precedent to cite.

And there is a further turn of the screw. This is a parable about meaning being inside a door you cannot pass, immediately followed by an exegesis that eats itself. Kafka has pre-emptively written every reader who wants a master key to The Trial into the novel as a character. What it changes is your posture toward difficult texts generally: sometimes the point is not that you have failed to decode the thing, but that its meaning was never located in a correct answer — it is located in your standing at the door.

"Like a dog!": the machine's finished product is shame

The ending moves very fast. On the eve of K.'s thirty-first birthday two gentlemen in frock coats, looking like second-rate actors, come to collect him. He does not resist. He leads the way; he adjusts his stride to theirs; the three of them lock arms and cross the night streets as a single unit. At the quarry they take off his coat and arrange him, then begin passing a butcher's knife back and forth over him — and K. understands that etiquette requires him to take the knife and do it himself, and that he cannot. The blade goes into his heart and is turned twice. His last words: "Like a dog!" And the narrator: it was as though the shame would outlive him.

In his final moment he sees a window fly open in the top storey of a house nearby, and a figure lean out, faint and thin, and stretch both arms out. Who was it? Wanting to help? Objecting? A spectator? The book does not say. The single instant in a whole year when someone might have reached toward him flickers past, and nothing follows it.

Why shame, rather than outrage, or injustice, or protest? This is the last piece of the design. The machine's output is not a corpse, it is a man who has come to agree that he is guilty. K. dies without his charge, yet he has accepted the identity of a person who is on trial — which is why he cooperates, why he feels the discourtesy of not wielding the knife himself, why the indignity of his own death embarrasses him. Outrage points outward, at whoever did this. Shame points inward, at yourself. A system that has converted its victim's anger into shame can no longer be accused, because the victim has crossed over to its side.

This is what lifts The Trial from a satire of officialdom to something genuinely chilling: the deepest injury arbitrary power does is not that it punishes you, but that it gets you to sign the verdict on its behalf. The shape is recognisable everywhere — the person in a long abusive relationship who starts arguing the abuser's case; the target of a pile-on who begins by asking whether they really do have a problem; the laid-off worker whose first thought is that maybe they genuinely weren't good enough.

The Distilled Spine

Compressed to a single line, the novel establishes this: "guilty" need not be a factual judgment about a person. It can be a condition imposed on them — and once imposed, every act of self-rescue by the accused becomes fuel for it. The argument is demonstrated step by step rather than asserted.

Step one: hollow out the accusation. The arrest happens; no charge is named; no liberty is taken. Defence loses its object, and the defendant is forced to search inward. The burden of proof is inverted on page one.

Step two: distribute the court. It is not in a palace downtown but in attics, in the lumber room of his own bank, in the social network of everyone he knows. With no boundary there is no outside — nowhere to appeal to, because he never left.

Step three: remove the terminus. Titorelli's three roads establish that this case cannot be won, only postponed in different styles. Time replaces the judge as the actual executioner.

Step four: make the defendant change professions. K. first treats the case as an interruption; it then consumes his work (his deputy manager absorbs his position piece by piece), his attention, his relationships. Block has already performed the endgame: what remains of a man is a file number.

Step five: explain why there is no exit. Before the Law supplies it. The door stood open, nobody physically barred him, and the permission he was waiting for never existed. Waiting is the verdict. The self-cancelling commentary that follows adds the second blade: there will be no answer about what the story means either.

Step six: close the net. He is killed not in a crescendo of violence but in a shabby ritual that requires his own cooperation to complete. The product is shame — the victim takes the verdict and countersigns it.

So the protagonist of this book is neither Josef K. nor the court. It is process itself: a machine that requires no truth and no malice, only time and your participation, in order to run.

Misreadings, Criticism, Controversy

  • Misreading one: "Kafka predicted totalitarianism." The dates do not work. The book was written in 1914–15; Kafka died in 1924, before Hitler and before the Moscow show trials. The later resonance is real — all three of his sisters died in the camps — but reading The Trial as political prophecy loses its strangest feature: this court is not a police state. It does not imprison K., does not watch him, never compels his attendance. He goes back, again and again, on his own legs. A secret police you can at least hate. This machine does not even supply an object for hatred, which makes it both more universal and harder to escape.
  • Misreading two: "K. is innocent." In the German opening sentence, "without having done anything wrong" sits in the subjunctive (Konjunktiv) — it is K.'s claim, reported, not the narrator's guarantee. The novel runs almost entirely in free indirect discourse, hugging K.'s viewpoint so closely that the reader is never granted an outside vantage. And K. is not especially likeable: he is purely instrumental with women (his neighbour Fräulein Bürstner, the lawyer's nurse Leni, the usher's wife) and cold and superior toward Block, a fellow defendant. Whether he is "guilty" is undecidable — and that undecidability is not vagueness on Kafka's part, it is the architecture.
  • Misreading three: "It's a finished novel and that is the chapter order." It is not. The manuscript existed as loose bundles; Kafka wrote the first and last chapters early, then abandoned the middle, leaving fragments behind. The familiar sequence is Brod's editorial arrangement; the 1990 critical edition prepared under Malcolm Pasley reorders it. More consequentially, Brod read his friend through a religious frame — divine grace, divine judgment — and that frame shaped a century of reception. Part of the Trial you read is an editor's work.
  • Misreading four: "Kafkaesque means the queue at the licensing office." The word has been worn smooth. Kundera flagged what actually marks the Kafkan: a single vast labyrinthine institution one cannot get outside of; the file being more real than the person (K.'s dossier decides his fate and he never once sees it); the punished searching for their offence; and — the element most often lost — that it is comic. Brod recalled that Kafka laughed so hard reading these pages aloud that he had to stop. Strip out the absurd comedy and you are left with a grievance, not with Kafka.
  • Controversy one: whether Kafka can be interpreted at all. Walter Benjamin held that Kafka wrote parables that have lost their doctrine — the form of a teaching story without any recoverable teaching. Adorno went further (paraphrased): every sentence of Kafka says "interpret me," and not one will permit it. Deleuze and Guattari (1975) rejected the theological, psychoanalytic and symbolic readings wholesale, proposing instead minor literature — writing by a minority inside a major language (a Prague Jew writing German), which politicises the language itself rather than encoding a symbol. The only consensus is that any key claimed to unlock The Trial should be treated as suspect.
  • Controversy two: the lure of biography. In July 1914, in a Berlin hotel, Kafka was confronted in person by his fiancée Felice Bauer and two others, and the engagement was broken off; he called it in his diary the tribunal at the hotel, and began The Trial a few weeks later. The thread is genuine. But collapsing the novel into a displaced broken engagement — or into the paternal shadow of Letter to His Father — downgrades a book about institutions and time into a private medical file.
  • Controversy three: the literary weaknesses. Honestly: several middle chapters repeat and stall, and the pacing is not under control — partly because the book is unfinished. There is also a defensible complaint that the women are written almost entirely as conduits to the court (each somehow attached to it, each showing the accused a near-automatic sexual availability). Whether this is Kafka diagnosing K.'s objectifying gaze or a symptom of the author's own remains unsettled.

The Kafka Beyond This Book

Read only The Trial and Kafka looks like a writer of bureaucratic nightmares, which misses two of his three load-bearing walls.

The Castle (1922, unfinished) is this novel's mirror. In The Trial, K. wants out and cannot get out; in The Castle, a land surveyor named K. wants in and cannot get in — summoned to a post he can never confirm he holds, unable to reach a castle that is plainly visible on the hill above him. Read together they complete the proposition: being arbitrarily taken in by an institution and being arbitrarily kept out of it are the same condition — in or out, it is not yours to decide.

The Metamorphosis (1915) moves the same structure into the family and the body. The salesman Gregor Samsa wakes as an enormous insect — no cause, no process. The real content of the story is how his family travels from panic to care to irritation to the verdict that the thing has to go. There is no court here; the sentence is delivered by relatives, and the coldest line of it comes from the sister he loved most. It supplies the dimension The Trial leaves out: the most common venue for being refused recognition as a person is not a courtroom, it is a dinner table.

Letter to His Father (1919, never delivered) gives the private origin. Kafka recalls asking for water one night as a small boy and being carried out onto the balcony and shut there a while. A small thing — but, he writes, from then on he understood something: that this enormous man could treat him that way for no reason at all, and that he had no standing even to ask why. "For no reason," "no explanation is owed," "you have no right to ask" — the entire grammar of the machine in The Trial is already assembled in that letter. Knowing this does not reduce the novel to family damage; it explains why Kafka had almost microscopic sensitivity to this particular form of power.

One more that should not be skipped: In the Penal Colony (1914, the same year) describes an execution machine that inscribes the sentence directly into the condemned man's skin, under a principle stated outright — guilt is never to be doubted, and so the condemned is not told his sentence in advance; he is expected to decipher it from his own wounds. It is The Trial rendered mechanical: in a single year, Kafka wrote the same thought twice.

Ten Sentences

1. "Someone must have been telling lies about Josef K., for without having done anything wrong he was arrested one morning." Note that this is his own claim, reported (the German is subjunctive). From its first line the book has no intention of telling you whether he is innocent.

2. Withholding the charge is not an oversight, it is the move: with nothing specific to answer, you cannot defend — you can only audit yourself, and the work of finding the offence has been legally outsourced to the accused.

3. Titorelli's three roads: real acquittal exists only in legend; apparent acquittal relapses; protraction costs you the rest of your life. A procedure with no stopping condition is already a sentence.

4. "The verdict does not arrive all at once; the proceedings gradually turn into the verdict." (paraphrased) When the institution owns time and you do not, it does not need to win — it only needs not to finish.

5. The court is not in a marble palace but in tenement attics and in the lumber room of his own bank. Power needs no grandeur, only ubiquity — and the shabbiness is camouflage: with no centre, there is nobody to storm.

6. The next day, the whipping scene is running again, identical. The terror is not that someone is doing evil; it is that nobody is there at all — an unattended loop, installed in the building where you work.

7. Huld is forever drafting a plea he never finishes; Block, five years into his case, ends up living in the maid's room. Anyone paid by the process has their interest bound to its length — so find out first how the person helping you gets paid.

8. "No one else could be admitted here, because this entrance was made only for you. I am now going to shut it." (paraphrased) The door stood open and nobody stopped him; the permission he waited for simply never existed.

9. "You do not have to accept everything as true, you only have to accept it as necessary." K.: "It turns lying into a universal principle." (paraphrased) The earliest and still the most complete answer to every "I'm just following the rules."

10. His last words are "Like a dog!" — as though the shame would outlive him. What arbitrary power does most deeply is not punish you; it converts your outrage into shame, so that you countersign a verdict you were never allowed to read.
