The Histories

The Histories · Herodotus (c. 484–c. 425 BCE) · composed c. 430s–420s BCE

中文 →

In One Sentence

Why did the Greeks and the Persians go to war? Herodotus answers with an entire book: he asked around, went to look for himself, set the competing versions side by side and only then gave his own judgment — and in doing so turned storytelling into the investigation of causes. The causes he finds are stacked in layers: personal grudges, wrongs repaid across generations, an empire that could not stop expanding, the rule that human prosperity never stays put, and the great divide between free men who obey only the law and subjects who obey a master.

Where It Sits

Herodotus was born in Halicarnassus (modern Bodrum, Turkey), a Greek city then under Persian rule — he grew up on the edge of an empire, at the seam between two civilizations. His nine books run from Croesus of Lydia (reigned c. 560–546 BCE) to the Greek defeat of the Persian invasion in 479, roughly eighty years. Cicero called him "the father of history" (pater historiae) — and in the same breath noted the "countless fables" in his work. He has worn both hats ever since.

Core Claims

  • Human deeds deserve to be remembered, and their causes can be investigated. The first sentence is the program of the whole book (paraphrased): this is the display of the inquiry of Herodotus of Halicarnassus, so that what people have done may not fade with time, so that the great and marvelous deeds of Greeks and foreigners (barbaroi) may not lose their glory — and above all, to show why they fought one another.
  • History runs on a cycle of retribution and overreach. One side commits a wrong, the other pays it back; a ruler who rises too far, too smoothly, is bound to fall. Empires and individuals trace the same curve.
  • The Greeks won not because they were more numerous or favored by the gods, but because they were free. Freedom here is not doing as you please; it is having the law as your only master. Men fighting for themselves outfight a much larger army driven forward by the whip.

Put together: record the great events of human life, ask why they happened, and the same patterns keep surfacing — injustice invites revenge, success invites overreach, and what finally decides the contest is the kind of rules a people lives by.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Inquiry (historiē): I must report what is said, but I need not believe it

What it is. Our word "history" comes from the Greek word Herodotus uses in his opening line, historiē. For him it was not yet the name of a discipline; it meant asking around, investigating, finding out — the same word the Ionian natural philosophers used for their inquiries into nature. He names his three tools himself (2.99, paraphrased): up to this point I have reported what my own sight (opsis), judgment (gnōmē) and inquiry tell me; from here on I will give the Egyptians' own accounts as I heard them. In other words: what you saw, what you heard (akoē), and what you concluded are to be labeled separately.

The mechanism: lay out the versions, then show your hand. His signature move is not "this is what happened" but "the Persians say this, the Phoenicians say that, and my own view is…". On the birth of Cyrus he says he knows three other versions and has chosen the least inflated (1.95). And he lays down a rule for the whole work (7.152, paraphrased): I am obliged to report what is said, but I am not obliged to believe all of it — and let this hold for my entire account.

Examples. The best illustration is a small detail in 4.42. He has heard that the pharaoh Necho sent Phoenician sailors from the Red Sea to sail around "Libya" (Africa), and that they came home through the Strait of Gibraltar in the third year. The sailors claimed that as they rounded the continent the sun was on their right. Others may believe it, Herodotus writes; I don't. But sailing west around the southern tip of Africa, the sun really would be to the north — on your right. The one detail he refused to believe is the best evidence that the voyage actually happened. Because he wrote down even what he doubted, later readers had something to judge with.

Another is one of the first recorded "experiments" (2.2). The Egyptian king Psammetichus, wanting to know which people was oldest, had a shepherd raise two newborns without ever speaking a word to them. Two years later the children's first word was "bekos" — Phrygian for bread. Herodotus notes that he got this from the priests at Memphis and that the Greeks tell a sillier version. The story may not be true, but the move — report, cite the source, compare versions — was new.

An analogy. Think of an old-school investigative reporter who tags every quote with who said it and whether they saw it or heard it, and only at the end writes "on balance, we believe…" — instead of quietly promoting rumor to fact.

Why it matters. Before him, the past was told as epic: the Muse "tells" Homer what happened, and you don't cross-examine a goddess. Herodotus moved authority from divine revelation to human investigation. You can disagree with him, but you can check his sources. That is the founding step of history — and of empirical inquiry in general.

How it changes the way you see. You start tagging every piece of information: who said this? Did they see it, or is it third-hand? What are the other versions? When the truth can't be pinned down, the honest move is not to pick the most appealing story but to preserve the disagreement itself.

2. Cause (aitiē): the cause is also the blame

What it is. When he promises to explain why the Greeks and Persians fought, the word is aitiē, which in Greek means both "cause" and "fault, responsibility." To ask why a war happened was, for Herodotus, to ask who did wrong first.

The mechanism: a chain of reprisals. Book 1 opens with what "learned Persians" say: it all began with stolen women. Phoenicians carried off Io, Greeks carried off Europa and Medea, Paris carried off Helen — and, the Persians add, kidnapping women is wrong, but raising an army over one is going too far. Having laid out these myths, Herodotus says: I won't rule on whether any of that is true; I'll start with the man I know for certain first wronged the Greeks — Croesus of Lydia, the first to subjugate the Greek cities of Asia Minor. That is the crucial step: he moves the starting point from myth to the verifiable human world.

Every link after that is retribution (tisis). Croesus himself is paying for the crime of his ancestor Gyges, who murdered a king and took his throne; the oracle had said vengeance would fall on the fifth generation. When the Ionian Greeks revolted against Persia and Athens sent twenty ships to help, Herodotus comments (5.97, paraphrased): those ships were the beginning of evils for Greeks and barbarians alike. He adds, dryly, that it proved easier to fool thirty thousand Athenians than one Spartan king. When Darius heard that the Athenians had burned Sardis, he shot an arrow into the sky, asked Zeus to let him punish them, and ordered a servant to say three times at every dinner: "Master, remember the Athenians" (5.105).

Never just one cause. His real sophistication is that he never gives a single reason. For Xerxes' invasion of 480, the book runs several layers at once:

Inherited revengeWiping out the shame of his father Darius' defeat at Marathon (490)

An analogy. An accident report: the driver was distracted (individual), the road had a history of crashes (history), the rota had him on a fourteen-hour shift (structure), and it was pouring that night (chance). Picking one layer and calling it "the real cause" is laziness.

Why it matters. This is the first systematic attempt to break "why" into layers. When Thucydides later separates the "truest cause" from the reasons people gave in public, he is walking further down the same road.

How it changes the way you see. When someone says "the root of this conflict is X," you ask what the other layers are. And you notice that every side's account of how it started begins, conveniently, with the other side's offense — which is exactly what the stolen-women prologue is about: everyone picking the starting point that suits them.

3. "Human happiness never stays in one place": Solon, Croesus and the envy of the gods

What it is. Before the narrative proper begins (1.5), Herodotus says, in effect: I will cover small cities and great ones alike, because many that were great long ago have become small, and those that are great in my day were once small. I know that human prosperity never stays in the same place. That is the ground tone of the whole book.

The central story: Solon visits Croesus (1.30–33, and 1.86). Croesus, the richest man alive, shows the Athenian lawgiver Solon his treasuries and asks: who is the happiest man you have ever seen? Solon names Tellus of Athens — his city prospered, he lived to see grandchildren, and he died gloriously defending his country and was buried at public expense. Second happiest? The Argive brothers Cleobis and Biton, who yoked themselves to a cart and hauled their mother all the way to the temple of Hera; she prayed that the goddess give them the best thing a mortal can have, and that night they lay down in the temple and never woke. Croesus is irritated: and what about me? Solon replies that a human life of seventy years is some twenty-six thousand days, no two alike; a man is entirely a matter of chance. Until he has ended his life well, do not call him happy — only lucky.

It comes true. Croesus asks the Delphic oracle whether to attack Persia and is told that if he does, he will destroy a great empire. He assumes it means Persia's; it means his own. Captured by Cyrus and placed on a pyre, he remembers Solon and calls his name three times. Cyrus asks what he means, reflects that he too is mortal, and orders the fire put out (the text says it took a rainstorm sent by Apollo to do it). Croesus later gives Cyrus one of the book's most famous lines (1.87, paraphrased): no one is fool enough to choose war over peace — in peace sons bury their fathers, in war fathers bury their sons.

The mechanism: divine envy (phthonos theōn) and overreach (hybris). Solon also says (paraphrased) that the divine is jealous and fond of upsetting things. This is not pettiness on the gods' part; it is a law of the cosmos: whatever grows too tall gets cut down. Polycrates, tyrant of Samos, was so frighteningly lucky that his ally, King Amasis of Egypt, advised him to throw away his most treasured possession and break the streak. He tossed a jeweled ring into the sea — and a few days later it turned up inside a fish a fisherman brought him as a gift. Amasis concluded he was doomed and broke off the friendship; Polycrates was later lured to his death by a Persian governor and crucified (3.39–43, 120–125). Before the invasion, Artabanus warns Xerxes (7.10, paraphrased): you see how the god strikes the largest animals, the tallest houses and trees with his thunderbolts, while the small ones don't provoke him. Xerxes doesn't listen. When a storm wrecks his bridges across the Hellespont, he has the water given three hundred lashes and a pair of shackles thrown in — that is hybris: a mortal who thinks even the sea should obey him. Yet the same Xerxes, reviewing his vast army at Abydos, weeps: in a hundred years not one of these men will be alive (7.46).

An analogy. The star fund manager who doubles his money five years running: the more he wins, the more leverage he takes on, until one swing wipes him out. In modern terms, Herodotus' "envy of the gods" is the way success systematically tempts people into risks they can't see.

Why it matters. It gives the book its shape: one power after another traces the arc of rise, excess and fall, and Persia's invasion of Greece is the grandest performance of all. Persia's defeat is not only a military story; it is a moral story about excess.

How it changes the way you see. Judge a person, a company, a country not by its best year but by how it finishes. The real question is not "how successful are you now?" but "what is your success turning you into?"

4. "Custom is king of all" (nomos): every people thinks its own ways are best

What it is. The Greek word nomos covers custom, convention and law. The best-known passage is 3.38. Darius summons the Greeks at his court and asks what price would make them eat their dead fathers. Nothing on earth, they say. He then summons the Callatiae, an Indian people who do eat their dead parents, and — with the Greeks listening through an interpreter — asks what price would make them burn their dead fathers. They cry out and beg him not to say such terrible things. Herodotus' conclusion (paraphrased): if you asked everyone to choose the best customs from all the customs in the world, each, after careful thought, would choose their own. So Pindar was right: custom is king of all.

The mechanism: customs aren't argued into existence, they're lived into it. They soak into you from childhood until "the way we do it" simply means "the way it is done." Which is why Herodotus concludes that the Persian king Cambyses, who mocked the Egyptian gods and stabbed the sacred Apis bull, must have been mad — only a madman would ridicule other people's sacred customs.

The ethnography exists to show this. A huge share of the book is "digression": all of Book 2 on Egypt, most of Book 4 on the Scythians, the nomads of the steppe north of the Black Sea. The Egyptians, he says, do almost everything backwards from everyone else: the women go to market and trade, the men stay home and weave (2.35). Custom can even kill: the Scythian king Scyles secretly joined Greek Bacchic rites, was found out by his people, deposed and executed (4.78–80).

An analogy. Your phone's default settings: you never notice you're using any, until you pick up someone else's phone and everything is "backwards."

Why it matters. In a world that lumped all foreigners together as barbaroi (originally just "people whose speech sounds like babble," not yet "savages"), Herodotus shows unusual curiosity and respect for Egyptians, Persians and Scythians. He praises the Persians for teaching their sons three things: to ride, to shoot, and to tell the truth. More than five centuries later Plutarch would attack him as a "barbarian-lover" (philobarbaros).

How it changes the way you see. Much of what feels self-evident to you is just "how we've always done it here." But Herodotus is no nihilistic relativist. He doesn't say all customs are equally good; he says every people will prefer its own, so mocking what others hold sacred is a kind of madness. It is a conclusion about respect, not about "nothing is right or wrong."

5. Free men have the law as their master: why Greece won

What it is. The Persian Empire commanded dozens of peoples and an army of hundreds of thousands (millions, by his count); the Greeks were a quarrelsome scatter of small cities that often fought each other. The ultimate question Herodotus has to answer is how the small beat the large. His answer is concentrated in one conversation (7.101–104). Xerxes asks Demaratus, an exiled Spartan king now at the Persian court: with no master forcing them, will the Greeks really stand against me? Demaratus replies (paraphrased): they are free, but not free in everything. They have a master, and that master is the law (nomos), which they fear far more than your subjects fear you. And the law always commands the same thing: however many the enemy, do not flee; hold your ground and win or die.

The mechanism: a war you fight for yourself is different from one you are whipped into. At Thermopylae, Herodotus tells us, Persian officers drove their men forward with whips (7.223). And on Athens after it expelled its tyrants, he gives one of his rare outright verdicts (5.78, paraphrased): equality of speech (isēgoriē) is a fine thing; under tyrants the Athenians were no better at war than their neighbors, but once free they became the best by far — because when oppressed they worked for a master, and once free each man worked for himself. Two Spartan envoys on their way to Persia to offer their lives are urged to switch sides and take high office; they answer that the Persian general knows how to be a slave but has never tasted freedom — if he had, he would tell them to fight for it not just with spears but with axes (7.135).

The Persians debated it too. 3.80–82 is the earliest surviving debate about forms of government. After seven Persian nobles overthrow a usurper, they argue over what to set up. Otanes argues for rule by the people: a monarch can do whatever he likes without being held to account, whereas equality before the law (isonomia) is "the fairest of names." Megabyzus argues for oligarchy: the mob is more reckless than any tyrant. Darius argues for monarchy: nothing beats rule by the single best man. Four of the seven side with Darius. Herodotus insists that some Greeks refuse to believe Persians could have had such a debate — but it happened. This matters: the line between freedom and despotism is not, for him, a matter of Greeks being nobler by birth. It is a choice of institutions — and the Persians could have chosen the other road.

On the battlefield. At Marathon (490) he records about 6,400 Persian dead against 192 Athenians (6.117). For the Spartans who fell at Thermopylae (480) the epitaph reads: "Stranger, go tell the Spartans that here we lie, obedient to their words" (7.228). And before Salamis he makes a claim he knows will offend (7.139, paraphrased): to call the Athenians the saviors of Greece is simply the truth — rather than surrender, they took Themistocles' reading of the oracle about the "wooden wall" (the wooden wall meant ships) and abandoned their city for the fleet.

An analogy. Two kinds of company: in one, fines drive everything and people just try not to get penalized; in the other, the rules apply to everyone equally and people treat the work as their own. In normal times you can't tell them apart; when it's time to go all out, that difference is everything.

Why it matters — and the twist. This is the fountainhead of the Western "freedom versus despotism" story. But Herodotus did not write a simple hymn. The very last section (9.122) suddenly jumps back to Cyrus. Some Persians propose leaving their rugged homeland for richer country; Cyrus says fine, but be ready to go from rulers to ruled — soft lands breed soft men. The Persians choose to stay on poor land and rule. Why end there? Many scholars read it as a mirror held up to his Athenian contemporaries: by the time he was writing, the "saviors of Greece" had become a naval empire collecting tribute from their allies. The curve of rise, excess and fall — whose turn is next?

How it changes the way you see. A group's strength comes not only from its resources but from whether each member feels the cause is their own — and what victors most need to watch for is becoming the very thing they defeated.

The Spine of the Argument

Nine books, compressed into one line:

① The question: rescue human deeds from oblivion and find out why Greeks and Persians fought. The stolen-women myths are self-serving starting points; he begins instead with the verifiable Croesus.

② The empire expands (Books 1–4): Lydia falls to Persia; three Persian kings swallow the Near East and Egypt and campaign against the Scythians. At every stop he pauses for geography and customs — the "digressions" lay out a map of the world and keep demonstrating that custom is king.

③ The spark (Books 5–6): the Ionian revolt, the twenty Athenian ships, the burning of Sardis; Darius' revenge, and his defeat at Marathon.

④ The great invasion and the great failure (Books 7–9): driven by ambition, revenge, imperial momentum and dreams, Xerxes comes with everything he has and lashes the sea; the Greeks win against the odds at Thermopylae, Salamis and Plataea.

⑤ The themes close: the strong fall through excess (divine envy), free men fighting for themselves defeat an army driven by the whip — and the last page hands the same warning to the winners.

How does he make his case? Not with archives, which barely existed, but with years of travel and countless interviews — Egyptian priests, Greek settlers on the Black Sea, the family memories of Athens and Sparta, stories from the Persian side — plus terrain and monuments he saw for himself. His "proof" consists of three habits: name the source, set out the versions, state the judgment. What the book ultimately establishes is a method: knowledge of the past can be gained through human inquiry, and can be checked and contested by others.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "Herodotus was a storyteller; Thucydides was the real historian." The contrast was first drawn by Thucydides himself (1.22, paraphrased): my work lacks the romantic element and may be less pleasant to listen to, but it is meant as a possession for all time, not a showpiece for a moment's applause. He also quietly corrects "others" — for instance the belief that each Spartan king has two votes, which comes straight from Herodotus. But Thucydides could only nitpick because Herodotus had already invented inquiry. And their interests differ: Thucydides focuses on one war and on power politics; Herodotus wants to understand the whole known world — religion, customs, geography, the human heart. Many scholars now see him as the common ancestor of history, ethnography and geography.

Misreading 2: "He's selling Greek superiority and Eastern barbarism." The frame of free Greece versus despotic Asia is real, and later "Orientalist" narratives leaned on it. But he praises Persian honesty and courage, records the Persians' own constitutional debate, credits Egypt with much of Greek religion, and states plainly that many Greek cities went over to Persia (Artemisia, queen of his own hometown, fought for Xerxes at Salamis). The weightier critique comes from the French historian François Hartog in The Mirror of Herodotus (1980): the "others" in the book — the Scythians especially — often serve as a mirror in which Greeks see themselves. He writes about other peoples, but measures them with a Greek ruler.

Criticism 1: "Father of lies" — how much is true? The charge is ancient. Ctesias, a Greek physician at the Persian court, called him a liar; Plutarch wrote On the Malice of Herodotus. Some things are plainly impossible: the gold-digging ants of the Indian desert, "smaller than dogs but bigger than foxes"; Xerxes' host counted at more than 5.2 million people (7.186), which modern historians agree far exceeds what the logistics of the day could support — the real figure was a fraction of that. Chronology also makes the Solon–Croesus meeting very hard to accept. In 1971 the German scholar Detlev Fehling pushed the doubt to its limit, arguing that many of Herodotus' "so-and-so say" citations are invented, a rhetorical device to lend stories credible sources; the American scholar W. K. Pritchett answered him point by point in The Liar School of Herodotos (1993).

But the evidence often sides with him. Darius' own inscription carved into the cliff at Behistun tells how he and six companions killed a usurper posing as a prince — and five of those six names match Herodotus' list. The sun "on the right" is the classic case. A fair verdict: he faithfully reported what he was told, and what he was told was a mix of true and false; his errors lie mostly in his sources, not in deliberate invention.

Criticism 2: divine and rational explanation are tangled together. He examines geography and motive carefully, then treats dreams, oracles and divine envy as genuine causal forces. But in his worldview, human choice and divine order are two layers operating at once — Xerxes chose to invade and was also pushed by fate. He did not write purely secular history, but he did put human motives at center stage.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · Bartlett's reconstructive memory: In 1932 Frederic Bartlett had English subjects retell a Native American tale, "The War of the Ghosts," in chains; with each retelling it drifted toward English story conventions — the very road traveled by "the priests say" and "the Scythians say." The difference: by setting versions side by side, Herodotus left traces of the reconstruction.
  • Psychology · the peak-end rule: Kahneman found that when we remember an experience, we weigh its most intense moment and its ending and largely ignore its length. Solon's "look to the end" likewise lets the ending dominate. The difference: Solon says the end reveals the truth about a life; Kahneman shows a bias of memory.
  • Neuroscience · the critical period for language: Psammetichus asked whether language is innate or learned. Genie, a girl found in Los Angeles in 1970 after a childhood of isolation, later learned many words but never mastered full grammar. The difference: the modern answer is not an "original language" but an innate capacity that needs early linguistic input to unfold.
  • Distributed systems · keeping conflicting versions: In Amazon's 2007 Dynamo paper, when replicas disagree the system doesn't silently pick one; it returns the conflicting versions for the application to reconcile — the same design as "the Persians say… the Phoenicians say…": preserve the divergence, don't quietly overwrite it. The difference: Dynamo must end with one value; Herodotus often leaves the verdict to the reader.
  • Cultural evolution · the hidden wisdom of custom: Joseph Henrich points to manioc in South America: laborious traditional processing happens to remove its cyanide, though the people doing it can't say why. The parallel: rules that look absurd to outsiders may be the residue of generations of trial and error. The difference: Herodotus only says each people prefers its own customs, not that customs are useful.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① History begins with one act: refusing to let human deeds be erased by time, and asking why they happened.

② What you saw, what you heard and what you concluded must be labeled separately — the starting point of all empirical knowledge.

③ I am obliged to report what is said, but not obliged to believe it all (paraphrased).

④ A "cause" is also a "blame": every side's story of how it started begins with the other side's offense.

⑤ There is never just one cause: ambition, inherited revenge, imperial momentum and fate all work at once.

⑥ Human prosperity never stays in one place; until a man has finished his life, call him lucky, not happy.

⑦ In peace sons bury their fathers; in war fathers bury their sons (paraphrased).

⑧ Custom is king of all: everyone, on reflection, chooses their own — so mocking what others hold sacred is madness.

⑨ Free men are not masterless; their only master is the law — and people working for themselves beat people whipped into battle.

⑩ Soft lands breed soft men: what winners must watch for most is becoming the very thing they defeated.

Deep Read · You now have the through-line of The Histories: inquiry as a method, the chain of cause and blame, rise-and-fall and the envy of the gods, custom as king, freedom versus despotism — and the long quarrel over the "father of lies."
