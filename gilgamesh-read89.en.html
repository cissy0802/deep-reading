The Epic of Gilgamesh

Anonymous · Standard Babylonian version traditionally credited to Sîn-lēqi-unninni · c. 1300–1000 BCE

中文 →

In One Sentence

The oldest long poem humanity still reads is not about a hero winning. It is about a man who can win anything losing the one contest that cannot be won: his friend dies, and for the first time he understands that he will too. He walks to the edge of the world to buy his way out, exhausts every route, fails on each, and comes home empty-handed to the city wall he started from. The answer is hidden inside that return: immortality is not available in your body. It is available only in what you build and in whoever says your name afterward.

Where It Sits

It is an Akkadian poem, pressed in cuneiform into clay tablets. The version we read — the Standard Babylonian text — took shape somewhere between 1300 and 1000 BCE, and ancient catalogues attribute it to a scholar-priest named Sîn-lēqi-unninni, who is better understood as its final editor than its author: Sumerian poems about Bilgames circulated by about 2100 BCE, and an Old Babylonian Akkadian version by about 1800 BCE. The tablets came out of Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh in the 1850s; in 1872 George Smith of the British Museum read the flood story on Tablet XI aloud and set off a sensation, because it predated the Genesis flood by more than a thousand years. It is the earliest surviving long narrative literature — and the first time a human being turned the fear of death into art.

The Central Claims

  • Death is not a plot point here. It is the only antagonist. The first half is a flawless heroic adventure — a sworn friendship, a quest, a slain monster, a goddess refused — and the second half demolishes it. Strength, fame and divine blood are all worth nothing in front of one corpse. The poem's real architecture is heroic narrative punctured by a funeral.
  • Only two kinds of immortality are actually on offer, and neither is in the body. One is a name — being spoken of afterward. The other is a durable thing left behind: a wall, an institution, a text. The hero is made to exhaust every bodily route precisely so that this conclusion is forced rather than asserted.
  • Becoming human is a trade, not an upgrade. Enkidu gains language, loyalty and a city, and permanently loses the right to run with the animals. Gilgamesh gains clear sight of death and permanently loses the ability to be a carefree tyrant. This four-thousand-year-old poem insists that every real maturation is an irreversible loss.

The Core Ideas, One by One

Two-thirds divine: an arithmetic that cannot work out

The poem opens by handing its hero a strange recipe: Gilgamesh is "two-thirds god, one-third man" — his mother is the goddess Ninsun, his father a mortal king of Uruk. The fraction makes no biological sense, but it is not genetics. It is a proposition: a person can be almost divine in power and still die, because deathlessness is not a matter of degree. It is a threshold. The gap between gods and people is not how strong you are; it is which side of that line you were born on — and two-thirds of divinity will not get you across.

This is the engine of the whole book. He is king of Uruk, he built its wall, nobody can beat him, and he is so unchecked that he grinds down his own people (the poem says he exhausts the young men and takes the brides, until the city complains to heaven). And that very record of never being refused is why he later breaks so completely. Someone who has never met a wall he could not push over takes the first non-negotiable thing much harder than the rest of us. Think of the person who sails through life untouched until a parent's diagnosis introduces them, at forty, to the category of things that do not bargain. That is exactly the collapse this poem describes — written four thousand years ago.

Enkidu and Shamhat: civilization is a trade with no refunds

The gods do not answer Gilgamesh by killing him. They answer by building him a rival: Enkidu, a shaggy wild man who grazes and drinks with the gazelles and springs the trappers' snares. The hunters cannot handle him, so they fetch a temple woman from Uruk, Shamhat. She spends "six days and seven nights" with him at the watering place, and afterwards the animals bolt at the sight of him. He has been domesticated, and domestication is a one-way door. The poem's own explanation is plain: he had gained reason, his understanding had broadened — and so he no longer belonged to the herd. Then she teaches him to eat bread, drink beer, rub himself with oil and put on clothing. In Mesopotamia, bread and beer are the definition of a human being (raw food is for animals; fermented, processed food is for people). Those four lessons finish the job.

This scene is often read as a bit of exotica. It is in fact the book's first serious argument: enculturation is not addition, it is exchange. Enkidu gets speech, friendship, honour and a city, and permanently loses the steppe. The poem is careful enough to reopen the ledger on his deathbed: he first curses Shamhat, wishing her a life in the gutter, until the sun god Shamash points out that without her he would never have tasted bread or fine beer, never worn a garment, and never met Gilgamesh — whereupon he withdraws the curse and replaces it with a blessing. This is the first time in literature anyone puts the question "would I trade my innocence for my sight?" on the table and answers yes. Every person who left a small town and can never quite go back recognizes themselves in that reversal.

"Making a name": the only defence against death in a world with no heaven

To see why the two of them must go and cut down that cedar forest, you first need to know where Mesopotamians believed the dead go. The answer is a dark place called the House of Dust, which Enkidu dreams before he dies: its residents are feathered like birds, and clay is their food, dust their bread (paraphrased). No judgement, no reward, no paradise; king and slave chew dirt side by side in the dark. With no afterlife worth having, the only place left to work is this life.

Hence the culture's central obsession: making a name (Akkadian šuma šakānu, literally "to set up a name"). When Gilgamesh talks Enkidu into the expedition, the reasoning is brutally explicit: our days are numbered, we die anyway, so let us do something that people will still be repeating; and if I fall, at least I will have made a name (paraphrased). Look closely at the logic — it is not an attempt to live longer. It concedes that life is short and proposes trading the finite thing for a transmissible one. This is the earliest written economics of meaning: when you cannot extend the quantity, extend the echo.

It also explains the poem's first line: "He who saw the Deep" (Sha naqba īmuru; naqbu carries "abyss," "source," and "the totality of things" at once). The text presents itself as an inscription on stone, stored in a lapis lazuli box, and it tells you to go to the wall of Uruk, take it out, and read it. A poem about names outlasting bodies was built as the very thing it recommends — it is its own evidence. Four thousand years later we are still saying the name, which is a rather strong result for the method.

Humbaba and the refusal of Ishtar: the heroic story is inflated so the puncture is louder

The two great exploits look like standard heroic fare. They are in fact scaffolding, carefully erected in order to be knocked down.

First, the march to the cedar forest and the killing of its giant guardian, Humbaba. Humbaba was appointed by the god Enlil to protect that forest — which means what they kill is a watchman doing his job. Once subdued he begs for his life, and it is Enkidu who insists on the kill, a detail that will later be read out as one of the charges against him. (A Tablet V fragment acquired by the Sulaymaniyah Museum in Iraq in 2011 added roughly twenty lines describing the forest as a noisy place full of monkeys and birdsong, along with the pair's unease after felling the trees. It makes the monster-slaying look a great deal more like an invasion and a clear-cut — though reading it as an ancient ecological parable is still our inference, not the poet's intent.)

Second, the goddess Ishtar propositions the returning hero, and he refuses her to her face, itemising what became of her previous lovers: the shepherd Dumuzi, wept for year after year; the bright bird with its wing broken; the lion, for whom pits were dug; the stallion given the whip and the bit; the gardener Ishullanu, turned into a small crawling thing. The list says one thing: with her you will not die, but you will be turned into something else — you survive by being transformed out of yourself. Humiliated, she extracts the Bull of Heaven from her father Anu; they kill that too, and Enkidu throws a haunch of it in her face.

Only after this unbroken winning streak does the bill arrive: the gods convene, rule that one of them must die, and choose Enkidu. He does not fall in battle. He lies sick for twelve days and dies in bed — the poem deliberately denies him a hero's death. That is the hinge of the entire work: a hero can win every fight and still lose to an illness.

The maggot: the first time literature made death this concrete

What Gilgamesh does next is the most startling passage in the poem. He refuses the fact. He will not release the body — until, and the line is delivered with terrible calm, a maggot drops out of the dead man's nose. That is when he breaks: he tears off his royal robes, puts on animal skins, and runs into the wilderness howling like a lion.

Notice what the detail is doing. Until that second, "death" was a word to him. When the maggot falls, death stops being a concept and becomes a specific body, spoiling, that he has been holding. That is the real reason he sets out — not a philosophical deduction that he is mortal, but having seen what mortality looks like. The line he then repeats to everyone he meets runs, roughly: I weep for my friend Enkidu; I am afraid of death; that is why I wander.

It is also the sharpest thing the poem says about grief: we usually begin to fear death not when we fall ill, but after we bury someone. Another person's death is the trailer for your own. In four thousand years no psychology book has put that more precisely than the maggot does.

The flood: an administrative exception that cannot be repeated

At the end of the world he finds Uta-napishti, the one survivor of the great flood, the man the gods made deathless. His question is blunt: how did you do it, and can I do the same?

Which brings us to the Tablet XI story that shook Europe in 1872. The gods resolve to wipe out humanity; Ea, god of wisdom, is sworn to silence and so talks to a reed wall — an elegant piece of oath-dodging: I told no man anything, I was merely muttering to a fence — and Uta-napishti, inside, overhears. He builds the boat, loads family, livestock and craftsmen; the flood runs seven days; the gods themselves cower at the edge of heaven like dogs; the boat grounds on Mount Nimush; he releases a dove, a swallow and a raven, and the raven does not return, which means the waters have gone down; he sacrifices, and the gods gather over the offering "like flies." Enlil finally comes aboard, touches his forehead and his wife's, and grants them eternal life.

But inside the poem the flood is not a curiosity. It is an argument. Uta-napishti finishes the story and draws the conclusion himself: my deathlessness was an amnesty, not a route. His question back to Gilgamesh runs, roughly: so who, now, is going to call the gods into session for you? That assembly only met because the world was being drowned, and the world will not be drowned twice. The door to immortality did exist. It opened once and was welded shut. This is a startlingly modern answer: not "there are no miracles," but "there was one, and it isn't yours."

Seven nights without sleep: a small test that punctures a large delusion

The old man then sets a test. You want to beat death? Start with its little brother: stay awake seven days and seven nights. Gilgamesh sits down and falls asleep on the spot. He sleeps for seven days.

The evidence is the beautiful part. Uta-napishti's wife bakes one loaf a day and sets it by his head. When he wakes, the first is mouldy and hard as stone and the last is still warm — a row of loaves has become a clock he cannot argue with. His first instinct is to deny it ("I only dozed off a moment"), and the bread shuts him up.

The mechanism rewards a second look. Sleep is the daily sample of death: every night you lose consciousness, lie defenceless, and let hours pass without noticing. If you cannot beat the small death that comes every night and reliably lets you go, on what basis do you expect to beat the large one that doesn't? And he does not hold out heroically for six days before failing; he goes under immediately. The poem will not even grant him a dignified defeat. It is the least merciful line the epic draws under human self-regard: the problem is not that you didn't try hard enough, it is that you never reached the starting line.

The snake takes the plant: a failure with no villain in it

The old man relents at the last moment and gives up one final secret: on the sea floor grows a thorned plant called "The Old Man Grows Young." Gilgamesh ties stones to his feet, sinks, and comes up holding it.

What follows is the passage that hurts most, because it is the most like real life. He does not eat it. He decides to carry it home to Uruk, test it on an old man first, and only then take it himself — reasonable, prudent, exactly what a responsible king would do. On the way he finds a pool of cool water and goes in to bathe. A snake smells the plant, carries it off, and sheds its skin as it goes. He sits down on the bank and weeps.

Count the cruelties. First, he succeeded — he did not fail to get it, he got it and lost it. Second, what destroyed him was not an enemy but one second of caution and one swim. Third, the immortality was not destroyed; it was merely handed to another species — snakes renew themselves by shedding, and we do not. The poet refuses to let him lose to a demon king and insists on losing him to a passing snake, because the point is precisely this: most real failures have no antagonist, no climax and no lesson. They simply happen. And a near-miss is harder to digest than a clean defeat — which anyone who has ever almost had something already knows.

The wall: he circled the world, and the answer was under his feet the whole time

The ending is startlingly quiet. Gilgamesh walks back to Uruk with Urshanabi the ferryman, and his last speech is an instruction to climb the wall and pace it out: look at the brickwork, see whether the foundations were not laid by the Seven Sages, take the measure of the city — the town, the date grove, the clay pit and the temple of Ishtar; three and a half square miles in all.

This is not an idle flourish. These lines repeat, almost word for word, the opening lines of the poem. The epic closes a circle: what you went to the ends of the earth for was sitting at the place you left from. And the "answer" is deliberately made small. Not a revelation, not eternal life — a stretch of brick and a set of measurements.

Why does that count as an answer? Because the wall satisfies every condition that "immortality" can actually satisfy inside this worldview: it outlives the man who built it, it protects people who are not born yet, and it carries his name. He asked not to die and was given something else — that what he made will not die — and the poem holds that this is the highest version of the thing our species can obtain. The restraint is still rare four thousand years later. It promises no afterlife and offers no consoling philosophy; it simply puts a smaller, harder, genuinely achievable object in your hands. You will not last. Something you make can last longer than you.

The Spine of the Argument

The epic's real structure is a proof by elimination with a strict running order. The hero is made to try every available answer to death, each one fails in front of the reader, and the one left standing at the end is the conclusion.

The route he triesHow it fails
Force: win everything (Humbaba, the Bull of Heaven)Wins every fight; his friend dies of an illness anyway

Not an adventure story: an elimination. Five doors are nailed shut so the sixth can be the answer.

Running underneath that is a second line just as deliberate: the whole book is a double crossing between wildness and the city. Enkidu walks out of the wilderness into Uruk and gains humanity at the cost of the herd; after his death Gilgamesh walks out of the city into the wilderness, in skins, unshaven, eating raw meat, until he has nearly reverted to Enkidu's starting condition; then he walks back. He has to become Enkidu before he can understand Enkidu, and he has to lose the city before he has earned that wall. Read the first and last tablets against each other and the structural self-awareness of this poem stands comparison with any modern novel.

One thing that reliably confuses new readers: the standard version runs to twelve tablets, but Tablet XII is an appendix — a near-literal translation of the second half of a separate Sumerian poem, Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld, and it does not fit (Enkidu is alive again in it). The narrative completes itself perfectly at the wall on Tablet XI. Tablet XII is related material attached by ancient editors, not a sequel.

Common Misreadings & Real Criticisms

Misreading 1: treating it as the first "hero's journey." Force it into the Campbellian template — departure, trials, return with the boon — and you read it backwards. This hero brings back nothing. He brings back empty hands and a speech about failure. It is better understood as the first counter-example to heroic narrative: the genre is played at full volume, then dismantled on stage.

Misreading 2: "the flood story proves the Bible copied it." Too crude. The scholarly consensus is that both draw on one long-running Mesopotamian flood tradition — the older and fuller version sits in the Atrahasis epic, where the gods send the flood because humanity has become too noisy to sleep through, a motive considerably more archaic than Genesis's moral one — rather than one manuscript being copied from another. Exactly how that tradition reached the Hebrew text, by which route and in which century, is still argued over. "Shared inheritance" is defensible; "direct plagiarism" overreaches.

Misreading 3: declaring the two men's relationship a romance in the modern sense — or denying its intensity. The text is not coy: Gilgamesh dreams of an axe and a fallen meteor and "caresses it like a wife" (paraphrased), and his mother reads the dream as the companion to come; he later mourns Enkidu "like a widow." At the same time, second-millennium-BCE Mesopotamia had no category of sexual orientation, so stamping a modern identity label on the pair is an anachronism. The honest position: the emotional and physical intimacy is written at very high intensity, and the question of which modern box it belongs in is one the text does not answer — because it does not have the question.

Misreading 4: assuming we have a complete, stable work. We do not. It has been assembled from hundreds of broken tablets scattered across several cities and more than a millennium, there are still conspicuous gaps, and it is still changing — new fragments periodically restore dozens of lines, as the 2011 Tablet V piece did. Read it knowing that you are holding a text still under construction, and that part of any translation's smoothness was supplied by the translator.

Criticism 1: the "oldest" label is oversold. "The first work of literature" does not survive scrutiny — earlier Sumerian compositions exist, including the temple hymns credited to the priestess Enheduanna. The accurate claim is narrower: it is among the earliest surviving long narrative poems, and the earliest that still lands on an ordinary reader. The second half of that sentence is where its real weight is.

Criticism 2: modern readings project heavily. Taking Humbaba as an ecological parable, Enkidu as the noble savage, the whole poem as an existentialist manifesto — all moving, all suspect, all twentieth-century categories. The poet's own concerns were probably more concrete: the legitimacy of kingship, the order of the city, sacrifice and omens. A good ancient text can carry modern readings; a good reader keeps track of which half the text said and which half we added.

Criticism 3, about the present: the supply chain for these tablets is not clean. In 2021 the United States repatriated to Iraq a tablet known as the Gilgamesh Dream Tablet, which had entered the market through the illicit antiquities trade and been bought by an American company and donated to a museum. A poem about what survives has a modern history that includes plunder and laundering. Worth knowing while you read it.

A last note that is not a criticism but a warning: first-time readers often find it hard going — formulaic repetition, long ceremonial speeches, lines that break off mid-thought. Its power is not in narrative momentum but in the accuracy of a handful of moments: the maggot in the nostril, the row of loaves hardening day by day, the snake making off with the plant. Those images have not dulled in four thousand years.

Ten Sentences

1. The oldest long poem we have is not about winning. It is about a man who wins every fight losing to the one thing that cannot be fought: his friend dies, and only then does he learn that he will too.

2. "Two-thirds god, one-third man" is not genetics but a proposition: deathlessness is a threshold, not a quantity, and no amount of strength gets you over it.

3. Enkidu becomes human in six days and seven nights, and afterwards the animals run from him. Civilization is not addition but exchange — language, bread and friendship bought with the permanent loss of the way back. Dying, he curses the woman who brought him in, then takes the curse back and blesses her: literature's first answer to "would I trade innocence for sight?"

4. In a world where the dead go to a House of Dust to wear feathers and eat clay, the only defence against death is making a name: since the quantity cannot be extended, extend the echo.

5. The hinge is not a lost battle but an illness: the hero can beat any enemy and cannot stop Enkidu lying sick for twelve days. And what finally breaks him is the maggot falling from the dead man's nose — death stops being a word and becomes a body that is spoiling.

6. The flood is an argument, not a digression. Uta-napishti's immortality was a one-off amnesty, and his question back is the whole point: "so who is going to convene the gods for you?" The door opened once and was welded shut.

7. Told to stay awake seven nights, he is asleep at once, and a row of loaves — the first mouldy, the last still warm — convicts him. If you cannot beat the small death that lets you go every morning, you will not beat the one that doesn't.

8. He does find the rejuvenating plant, then loses it to a snake while bathing, because he prudently wanted to test it on someone else first. Most real failures have no villain, no climax and no moral. They just happen.

9. At the end he tells the ferryman to climb Uruk's wall and measure the city — lines that repeat the poem's opening almost verbatim. What he crossed the world for was at the place he left: not eternal life, but brickwork with a name on it.

10. So the conclusion this four-thousand-year-old poem reaches is small, hard, and still unbeaten: immortality is not in you. It is in what you build and in whoever says your name — you will not last, but something you make can last longer than you.
