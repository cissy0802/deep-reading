The Tale of Genji

Genji monogatari · Murasaki Shikibu · written c. 1000–1012

中文 →

In One Sentence

Across fifty-four chapters, four generations and some seventy years, Murasaki Shikibu is demonstrating a single proposition: the more finely a person can feel the world, the more certainly that person will lose it — and the capacity to be moved anyway is the only thing about a human being worth writing down. The book judges nobody, concludes nothing, and stops in mid-air. What it does instead is harder: it treats being moved as the highest human faculty, and then spends seventy years watching that faculty drain the person who has it.

Where It Sits

Murasaki Shikibu (c. 973 – c. 1014 or later) came from the zuryō class — provincial-governor gentry, the outer ring of Kyoto power. Her father, Fujiwara no Tametoki, was a scholar of Chinese; she learned the classics faster than her brother by listening in, and he is supposed to have sighed that it was a pity she was not a boy (her diary, paraphrased). Widowed young, she entered court as lady-in-waiting to Empress Shōshi, whose father was Fujiwara no Michinaga, the man at the absolute summit of regency politics. She wrote her book about the centre of power from behind a curtain at the centre of power. Place it on a world timeline and the effect is startling: three centuries before Dante, four before Chaucer, six before the European novel matures — and from its first pages it is doing interior life, what a person is thinking and what he knows he is thinking. The claim that it is the world's first novel is contestable (honestly handled below); the claim that it is the earliest surviving long psychological novel is very hard to argue with.

The Core Claims

  • Fiction is not there to reward virtue and punish vice. The measure of a person is not whether they act rightly but whether they know mono no aware — whether a fallen blossom, a letter, another person's humiliation can actually reach them. This is the book's unstated ethics, and it is what Motoori Norinaga dug out eight centuries later, permanently rewiring Japanese aesthetics.
  • Nobody in this book loves a person; they love a resemblance. Every woman Genji loves stands in for another woman, in a chain that begins with a mother whose face he never registered and runs on for three generations after his death. It is the engine of the novel and its deepest cruelty: the beloved is never once seen as herself.
  • Sin is not punished here; it is re-staged. The child Genji fathers on his father's Empress ascends the throne. Thirty years later a young courtier fathers a child on Genji's own principal wife, and Genji, holding the baby, suddenly understands that his father probably knew all along. There is no divine retribution in this book, only symmetry — consequence is not a punishment sent from outside, it is the shape time turns out to have.

The Core Concepts, One at a Time

Mono no aware: not "sadness" but "the capacity to be reached"

Aware began as an exclamation — roughly the sound you make when something lands: ah. It specifies no direction. Blossoms opening produce it; blossoms falling produce it; so does a man losing his composure in the rain, or an enemy suddenly looking pitiable. So mono no aware does not mean sorrow. It means the thing got in, and something in you answered — it names the responsiveness, not the mood. The figure scholars usually quote is that the word appears over a thousand times in the tale. A word repeated at that rate is not vocabulary; it is skeleton.

This became the foundation of Japanese aesthetics through one book: Motoori Norinaga's Tama no Ogushi (1796). Before him the standard reading was Confucian and Buddhist — Genji sleeps with his father's consort, everything he loves eventually rots, therefore the tale is a warning against licentiousness. Norinaga dismissed that as a moral imposed by later hands (paraphrased) and replaced it with a claim nobody has since overturned: fiction has its own ethics, and its purpose is to make a reader know aware, not to sort people into good and bad. His famous image is worth keeping intact: to grow lotuses you need a pond of muddy water; the tale writes forbidden love not to praise it, but because only a love that cannot be had, cannot be spoken, and cannot end well will force the deepest aware to the surface (paraphrased). The mud is the method; the lotus is the point. Standing at the edge complaining about the mud is asking a lotus why it isn't clean.

Why it matters: it hands you a system for valuing narrative that is not Aristotle's. The Western default is a hero with a flaw, actions with consequences, and an audience purged through pity and fear — plot is the engine. The aware route has no engine, only resonance. Value comes not from what happened but from whether the people present were able to receive it. This is exactly why Genji reads, notoriously, as "plotless": it spends chapters on seasons, on blended incense, on who sent which poem under what colour of sky, because in this value system those are the main line, and coups and deaths are the background hum.

How it changes your seeing: accept that sensitivity is the highest human faculty and you discover immediately that it is priced in losses. The more a person can be moved, the more will be taken from them. In the late books Genji has everything — honorary rank equal to a retired emperor, a mansion laid out in four seasonal quarters, a daughter who becomes Empress — and then Murasaki dies, and he is left with nothing capable of moving him. That is the book's honest invoice for being able to feel.

Kaimami: an architecture engineered so that desire must happen

To see why love in this book takes the form it does, start with the physical conditions of aristocratic women in Heian Kyoto: they were close to invisible. Daytime meant sitting behind a hanging blind and a movable cloth screen; travel meant sitting inside an ox-carriage; and a woman's actual name was not given out — the labels readers use, Murasaki no Ue, Yūgao, Aoi, are all nicknames coined afterwards from her poems or her address, and "Murasaki Shikibu" is itself a nickname (Shikibu from her father's post). A woman's real name was among the most private facts about her.

Which makes being seen a charged, transgressive event. Kaimami means, literally, "looking through the gap in the fence": a man catches sight of a woman through a slit in a blind, past the edge of a screen, or in the instant a gust lifts something. This is not one device among many; in this book it is the only way love ever starts. The most consequential instance is in chapter five: Genji, eighteen and ill with malarial fever, is convalescing in the northern hills when he looks through a hedge and sees a girl of about ten in tears — someone has let her pet sparrow escape. The child resembles Fujitsubo, the woman he cannot have. The longest thread of destiny in the book begins with a bird flying off.

The other instance is fatal. In chapter thirty-four a small cat on a long cord bolts across a room and drags the blind fully open, and the Third Princess, standing inside, is seen entire by the young courtier Kashiwagi out on the veranda. A cat lifts a curtain, and a few years later Genji is holding a baby that is not his. Hanging the largest causes on the smallest, most accidental objects is Murasaki Shikibu's signature move.

The same architecture dictates the media of love. If you cannot see the person, what remains to fall for is narrow: the phrasing of a poem, the hand it is written in, the scent of an incense a lover blended personally (the recipe was read as character), and hearsay. Some 795 poems sit inside the narrative — not ornament, but the dialogue itself. And this is the technical precondition for everything that follows: when a woman can only be known as "like" something, she can at any moment be replaced by another woman who is also like it.

Katashiro and yukari: the chain of substitutes, which is the real plot

A katashiro was a Shinto proxy doll: you transferred a misfortune onto the effigy and floated it away downstream. Yukari means an affinity or connection — being loved because you resemble, or are related to, someone else. From end to end, The Tale of Genji is a chain of proxies, each link a copy of the last.

It starts from an absence. Genji's mother, the Kiritsubo Consort, held low rank, was harried to death by the other women of the palace, and died when he was three; the Emperor's grief is compared on the opening page to the Chinese emperor Xuanzong mourning Yang Guifei in Bai Juyi's Song of Everlasting Sorrow — the foundational work of Japanese literature opens by quoting a Chinese poem. The Emperor then brings to court a young woman said to look strikingly like the dead consort: Fujitsubo. Genji falls in love with her partly because she is beautiful and largely because everyone at court keeps telling him she looks like his mother. His whole life is spent pursuing a face he never actually saw.

The chain of substitutes: every woman's "reason to be loved" is written in the row above her. The last woman in the book breaks the chain with vows and silence.

Why this matters: it is a psychological mechanism we only got a word for a millennium later — transference — built here as narrative structure. And Murasaki Shikibu's handling of it is colder than any romantic treatment would be. Every substitute in the book knows perfectly well that she is one. Murasaki's lifelong grievance is not that Genji fails to love her; it is that she can never establish whether she is loved as herself. The moment the Third Princess arrives, she understands that her turn to be replaced has come. The third part is crueller still: after Genji's death the identical machinery restarts in Kaoru, who loves Ōigimi, loses her, and locates a woman who looks like her. New people, same chain.

How it changes your seeing: it puts one question into your hands — am I in love with this person, or with the vacancy I am using them to fill? Murasaki Shikibu's answer is bleak: mostly we can only love the shape of an absence, and the living person merely happens to fit. What redeems the book is that it also writes the other side — the living refusing to stay a shape. Ukifune's final refusal is the most forceful act in the entire work.

Sukuse and the mirror of consequence: sin is not punished, it is re-staged

Sukuse is a Buddhist term for karmic destiny carried from a previous life and unalterable in this one. Heian aristocrats used it to explain whatever could not otherwise be explained: why this person and no other, why this rank, why there was no escape. It works roughly like our word "fate," but more precisely: it is not randomness, it is a debt entered in a previous ledger and payable in this one.

The moral architecture of the book runs entirely on that ledger, and it settles by mirroring, never by punishment:

  • First time: the young Genji sleeps with Fujitsubo, his father's Empress. She bears a son whom the Emperor raises as his own to the end of his life, and who becomes Emperor Reizei. Genji's political fortune is made.
  • Second time, thirty years later: Genji accepts the Third Princess as principal wife. Kashiwagi, undone by that one glimpse when the cat lifted the blind, forces his way to her; she conceives, and bears Kaoru. Genji finds Kashiwagi's letter and understands everything.

The keystone is a single thought in chapter thirty-six. Holding this nominal son, Genji remembers his own father holding the infant Reizei — and wonders whether the old Emperor had in fact known all along, and simply said nothing. Murasaki Shikibu stages no thunderbolt, no trial, no scene of repentance. She simply lets the same event happen a second time, seats the offender in the victim's chair, and lets him do the arithmetic himself. Kashiwagi, after a few barbed remarks from Genji at a banquet, sickens with dread and dies; the Third Princess takes vows in her early twenties; Genji does nothing, and can do nothing, because he knows exactly which end of the transaction he is standing at.

How it changes your seeing: we habitually imagine consequence as an external force, somebody arriving to settle accounts. This book's version is that consequence is the moment you finally stand on the other side and see what you once did from where it landed. No punishment is more complete, and none requires less of a god.

Rokujō and the living spirit: the unconscious, described nine hundred years early

The Lady of Rokujō is the widow of a crown prince — very high rank, impeccable cultivation, ferocious pride, and seven years older than Genji. She is the one woman in the book too formidable to serve as anyone's substitute, and it destroys her. Mono no ke was the Heian catch-all for a possessing spirit. An ikiryō is worse: not the ghost of the dead but the soul of a living person leaving her body without her knowledge to do harm.

The trigger is tiny. At the Kamo festival procession in chapter nine, the retainers of Genji's wife Aoi shove Rokujō's carriage out of the good position and damage it, and she is left in public view, pushed to the back, watching other people be seen. A humiliation that is public and cannot be answered. Aoi's labour then goes catastrophically wrong; priests are called to exorcise; and the spirit speaking through Aoi uses Rokujō's turns of phrase.

The detail that raises the hair, though, belongs to Rokujō herself. She has been drifting through the same dream — she goes somewhere, she strikes and shakes a woman lying there — and when she wakes, her hair and robes smell of the poppy seed burned in the exorcism rites, and no amount of fresh incense will lift it. That is how she learns. She does not want to be doing this; she is nearly dead of shame over it; and it does not obey her.

Why it matters: Murasaki Shikibu describes, exactly, a part of the mind that does not take instruction from its owner and leaves physical evidence of its errands. Swap the Heian supernatural term for a modern one and the ikiryō is repressed, unacknowledgeable jealousy acting around consciousness — nine centuries before Freud, and better written, because she never explains it. She just gives you the smell of the poppy seed. Note too that she never condemns Rokujō. What she draws is not a wicked woman but a proud one whose dignity was publicly crushed and who is permitted no way to be angry about it; the vengeful spirit is her only means of speech. In a society where a woman cannot decently be seen to rage, possession is the one available exit for rage.

How it changes your seeing: next time someone injures another person "for no reason" and cannot afterwards explain themselves, ask first — is there an anger here that was never allowed to be spoken? It does not evaporate. It leaves by a route its owner never approved.

Taking vows, and impermanence: the only exit, and three deliberate silences

Renunciation in this book is almost never a religious impulse. It is a social death that functions as release: once your head is shaved and the precepts taken, you are out of marriage, out of courtship, out of the system in which a woman's worth is confirmed by whether a man comes to visit. Which is why it is the one exit the women of this book ask for, over and over.

But the exit requires a man's consent. Murasaki, gravely ill in her last years, asks repeatedly to take vows, and Genji refuses her every time, on the grounds that he cannot bear to let her go. He loved her all his life, and he also kept her beside him all her life, and would not grant her even the small freedom she asked for at the end. She dies in autumn among the dew (chapter forty), and the entire following chapter does one thing: Genji walks the year around in order, meeting her absence at every season. At its close he burns her letters and prepares to leave the world.

Then comes the most famous blank in Japanese literature: chapter forty-two, Kumogakure — "Vanished into the Clouds" — a title with no text under it. Not one word. Murasaki Shikibu declines to write Genji's death. Whether the chapter was always empty or once had text now lost is still argued; as reading experience it is unimprovable. The man called "the Shining One" from page one leaves without a line.

The third silence is the ending. The last of the Uji chapters is called The Floating Bridge of Dreams. Kaoru finally learns that Ukifune did not drown and has taken vows, and sends her young brother with a letter. She sits behind the blind, will not acknowledge the boy, will not take the letter, and says nothing. Riding home, Kaoru's thought is that some other man must be hiding her. The book stops there — no reconciliation, no close. Murasaki Shikibu lets her last heroine veto the entire game of pursuit and substitution by refusing to speak, and lets her last hero fail to understand that this is what has happened.

How it changes your seeing: the loudest place in a story can be where it shuts up. The three blanks — the unwritten death, the unwritten farewell, the unwritten ending — are the book's best formal statement about impermanence: real loss does not come with a scene to round it off. It simply has no sequel.

Three movements: part one proves a man can obtain everything; part two takes it back; part three shows that this was never about one man, but about the structure of the life itself.

So what does it actually establish? Not a proposition, but a complete price list for the capacity to feel. And its method of proof is unusual: it does not argue, it runs controlled repetitions. The same transgression, staged twice. The same love, staged five times. The same river at Uji made the endpoint of two different women. When one thing recurs across seventy years in different bodies, the reader no longer needs to be told that this is how life works; they have watched it. That is Murasaki Shikibu's mode of demonstration: state nothing, and let the structure speak.

Misreadings, and the Real Objections

Misreading one: it is an erotic romp, a playboy's catalogue of conquests. This is the negative image of the old moralising reading, and it is today's lazy version. Read the thing and you will find that Murasaki Shikibu almost never narrates a "conquest" admiringly: every affair is written together with what it costs the woman, and the accounting gets colder as the book goes on. The eight chapters of part two are the book's own reckoning with its own hero.

Misreading two: "the world's first novel." Discount this. Greek and Roman prose fiction long predates it — Chariton's Callirhoe (first century), Petronius's Satyricon, Apuleius's Golden Ass (second century). The fair formulation is that it is the earliest surviving work of full novelistic scale with sustained interior life: across fifty-four chapters characters age and change, remember things from twenty years earlier, and misconstrue one another's motives. That "characters possess internal time" is genuinely without an earlier complete example anywhere.

Misreading three: Heian standards dispose of the question of Genji's conduct. This is the sharpest live controversy and deserves an honest answer. Genji takes Murasaki into his own house when she is about ten and consummates the marriage when she is around fourteen; the text states plainly that she was shocked and humiliated and would not look at him for days. When Kashiwagi forces himself on the Third Princess, the text records her terror and her having no one to tell. The decisive point is that this suffering is written by Murasaki Shikibu herself — it is not imported by modern readers. Heian polygyny and the visiting-marriage system, in which a husband called at night on a wife who stayed in her own family's house, explain why Genji's contemporaries did not regard his conduct as criminal; explanation is not absolution. And the reason the book withstands modern reading is precisely that its author is clearer about what the women pay than any man in it.

The counter-case against Norinaga. Mono no aware is now so standard that it reads as common sense, but scholars point out its cost. Norinaga was central to kokugaku, the nativist school, and his thesis — that Japan possesses an aesthetic and an emotional order owing nothing to foreign teaching — gave him a motive to suppress how much of the tale is Buddhist scaffolding (impermanence, karmic destiny, renunciation, rebirth) and how much is Chinese (the very first page invokes Bai Juyi). Reading Genji through aware alone loses half its bones: it is a book about the capacity to feel, and it is also, thoroughly, a book about impermanence. Later scholars — Norma Field, The Splendor of Longing in the Tale of Genji (1987), and Haruo Shirane, The Bridge of Dreams: A Poetics of The Tale of Genji (1987) — moved the centre of gravity back to substitution, politics and structure rather than sentiment.

Disputes about the text itself. First, the Uji chapters differ in manner from what precedes them; the poet Yosano Akiko proposed they might be the work of Murasaki Shikibu's daughter, Daini no Sanmi, and modern stylometric studies have weighed in, but nothing is settled and single authorship remains the mainstream view. Second, whether the blank Kumogakure chapter was designed or lost is equally unresolved. Third, no manuscript in the author's hand survives; what we read descends from collated lineages, chiefly the recension associated with Fujiwara no Teika — we are reading an edited book, not an original.

The most practical objection: it is genuinely hard to read, and that is not entirely the reader's fault. Heian Japanese routinely drops subjects and signals who is acting on whom through levels of honorific verb-forms — the more exalted the person, the more elaborate the verb. To a court audience this was precise information; to everyone since, including modern Japanese readers, it is close to cipher, which is why most Japanese people read a modern translation (Yosano Akiko, Tanizaki, Enchi Fumiko and Setouchi Jakuchō each produced one). Add that characters have no names, are referred to by office and residence, and get re-labelled whenever they are promoted, and a great many readers are defeated by the machinery rather than the material. In English the choice matters enormously: Arthur Waley's free and Edwardian version, Edward Seidensticker's spare one, Royall Tyler's, which preserves the honorifics and the naming-by-title — choosing a translation is nearly equivalent to choosing a different book.

Ten Sentences

1. Mono no aware is not sadness; it is the capacity for something to get in and for something in you to answer — and this book's ethics never ask whether you are good, only whether you have it.

2. Norinaga's verdict (paraphrased): to grow lotuses you need muddy water — the tale writes forbidden love not to praise it but because only impossible feeling forces the deepest aware to the surface.

3. Sensitivity is priced in losses. The more a person can be moved, the more is eventually taken away — Genji ends up with everything, and nothing left that can move him.

4. In a world where women are invisible, love can only begin with a stolen glimpse: an escaped sparrow and a cat that drags a blind open are enough to decide three generations.

5. Every woman Genji loves stands in for another, and the chain starts with a mother he lost at three and never saw clearly. He spends a lifetime pursuing a face he does not have.

6. Sin is not punished; it is re-staged. Holding another man's child, he wonders whether his own father knew all along — retribution is not a thunderbolt, it is finding yourself in the opposite chair.

7. Rokujō wakes and smells the exorcists' poppy seed in her own hair, and so learns what she did in the night. Murasaki Shikibu never explains the unconscious; she just hands you the smell.

8. Where a woman cannot decently be seen to rage, possession becomes the only exit for rage — and vows the only exit for freedom, which Murasaki begged for all her life and Genji refused.

9. The three loudest passages are all blank: Genji's death is a chapter title with no text; Murasaki's death is followed by a chapter in which nothing happens; the book ends with a woman behind a blind saying nothing.

10. The last chapter is called The Floating Bridge of Dreams: the woman used as a substitute vetoes the whole game with silence, and the man can only think that someone else must be hiding her. A thousand years ago, the final scene is about the crossing between two people that never quite gets made.
