The Knowing-Doing Gap

The Knowing-Doing Gap: How Smart Companies Turn Knowledge into Action · Jeffrey Pfeffer & Robert I. Sutton · 2000 (folding in their 2006 follow-up, Hard Facts, Dangerous Half-Truths, and Total Nonsense)

中文 →

In One Sentence

Most companies are not short of knowing what to do; they know and still don't do it. What they lack is not knowledge but the capacity to turn knowledge into action, and what stands in the way is a set of habits the organization breeds for itself: talk in place of action, precedent in place of thinking, fear in place of trying, metrics in place of judgment, and internal rivalry in place of cooperation. Pfeffer and Sutton want readers to stop asking "what else do we need to learn?" and start asking "why can't we do what we already know?"

Where It Sits

Both authors are Stanford organization scholars: Pfeffer teaches organizational behavior at the business school, Sutton studies organizations and innovation in the engineering school. This is that rare management book that is anti-knowledge in spirit: it offers no new knowledge, only an explanation of why existing knowledge sits idle. This site also has Pfeffer's Power (Deep Read 972), which is about how individuals win influence inside organizations; this one is about why an organization as a whole knows and still cannot act.

The Core Claims

  • The gap between knowing and doing is more common, and more costly, than any shortage of knowledge. Companies spend enormous sums every year on training, consultants, business books and "knowledge management" systems (paraphrased), yet little of it changes behavior: people finish the course, read the report, and go back to doing what they did before.
  • The knowledge that really matters is mostly acquired by doing, and can only be passed on by doing. So writing it down and filing it in a database does not solve the problem; how much an organization "knows" shows up, in the end, in what it actually does every day.
  • The gap is not caused by lazy individuals; the organization manufactures it systematically. It has five recognizable sources, and each maps onto something managers can change: how talking in meetings is rewarded, how past practice is enshrined, how mistakes are punished, how performance is measured, and how employees are pitted against each other.

The Key Concepts, One by One

1. The knowing-doing gap: the problem is not what you know but whether you can do it

What it is. The knowing-doing gap is the situation in which an organization clearly knows how something should be done (it has read the research, hired the consultants, studied a rival's showcase) and still never acts on that knowledge. In their fieldwork the authors kept running into the same scene: managers who could describe "best practice" fluently, then went back to their own units and did none of it.

The Toyota example. The book's most persuasive case is the Toyota Production System. For decades Toyota has been remarkably open: competitors can tour its plants, shelves of books and articles describe its methods, and everyone can rattle off the vocabulary, from just-in-time (parts arrive exactly when needed, so no inventory piles up) to kanban (cards used as signals to replenish stock). Yet very few companies have managed to replicate Toyota's performance. The authors give particular attention to NUMMI, the joint venture between General Motors and Toyota in Fremont, California. It was built out of an old GM plant notorious for bitter labor relations and poor quality, and under Toyota's management its quality and productivity rose sharply; yet for years GM struggled to spread what NUMMI had learned to its other plants (paraphrased). The answer was sitting in GM's own backyard, and it still would not travel.

Why it can't be copied: why before how. The authors' explanation is that visitors carried home the techniques (the kanban cards, the cord beside the line that any worker can pull to stop it, the charts on the whiteboards) while the real thing at Toyota was the philosophy behind them: a belief that frontline workers can solve problems, that every line stoppage is a chance to learn, that improvement is continuous rather than a one-time fix. Copying the practices without the philosophy is like copying someone's workout plan without copying the reason he goes to the gym every day: the movements are identical, and you quit within three months. Hence the book's first prescription: why before how.

Tacit knowledge. Behind this lies a more general point. Much crucial knowledge is tacit knowledge, a term from the philosopher Michael Polanyi, whose summary was that "we know more than we can tell": riding a bicycle, or sensing whether a customer really intends to buy, is knowledge that resists being written down and passes mainly between master and apprentice, or among people who work side by side. On this basis the authors criticized the knowledge-management fashion of the day: most projects spent their money on databases and intranets, storing the part that is easy to write down, while the part of knowledge that decides success never makes it into the database at all. Their conclusion: knowing comes from doing, and from teaching others how.

How it changes your view. Next time you want to "study it a bit more" or "bring in a consultant," ask first: is knowledge really what we lack? Or have we known for a while and simply not acted? The two problems have entirely different solutions, and most organizations treat the second as if it were the first.

2. Talk substitutes for action: the smart-talk trap

What it is. This is the first of the five sources and the one the authors spend most time on. In many organizations, saying something, discussing it, or writing a plan for it quietly comes to count as having done it. The meeting ends, a decision is recorded, everyone leaves feeling the problem is handled. The mission statement goes up on the wall, and the culture is assumed to exist. The authors call this the smart-talk trap, the title of an article they first published in the Harvard Business Review in 1999.

The mechanism: why empty talk gets rewarded. First, inside organizations people who talk well are seen as smart, and smart people get promoted, so everyone has an incentive to talk rather than do. Second, criticism sounds smarter than construction. The authors cite a 1983 study by the Harvard psychologist Teresa Amabile, "Brilliant but Cruel": participants read positive and negative versions of the same book review, and the authors of the negative reviews were rated as more intelligent and more discerning (paraphrased). In a meeting room this means the fault-finder looks clever while whoever proposes a concrete plan exposes it to attack, and so more and more people criticize and fewer propose. Third, complex jargon and polished slides make people look learned while making it harder for listeners to translate any of it into specific actions.

An analogy. It is like someone who researches a training program for six months and buys all the gear, yet never once sets foot in the gym. The polish of a plan is often inversely proportional to how much of it gets carried out, because planning itself delivers the satisfying feeling of already making an effort.

The way out. The prescriptions are concrete: end every meeting by asking who will do what by when, and check at the next meeting; reward people who get things done rather than people who describe them beautifully; use plain language, because plain words convert more easily into action; and ask every critic, "So what would you do instead?" Their third prescription is exactly this: action counts more than elegant plans and concepts.

How it changes your view. You start separating two kinds of meeting output: "we reached consensus" and "Sam brings the prototype by next Wednesday." Only the second is an actual step forward.

3. Memory substitutes for thinking: bound by the way things were done

What it is. The second source is that organizations replace "what should we do now?" with "this is how we have always done it." Precedent, which should be a reference point, becomes the answer. A practice once worked, so it is written into procedures, retold by veterans, and absorbed into the company's identity; even after the environment changes, nobody dares to touch it.

The mechanism. The authors point to several forces that turn memory into shackles. Past success persuades people that "we know how to do this," so they stop asking questions. Existing procedures, structures and evaluation systems were all built around the old practice, so changing one thing means changing a whole chain. And people who have been inside an organization for a long time come to see the old way as natural, no longer noticing that it is only one possibility among many. Newcomers are often best placed to see the problem, and they are also the ones with the least voice.

Memory is not all bad. The authors are not asking anyone to forget history. Organizational memory spares people from starting from zero every time, and good routines are themselves accumulated knowledge. The trouble is repetition without reflection: remembering "we do it this way" while forgetting "why we started doing it this way." Once the reason is forgotten, nobody can tell whether it still holds. This is the other side of the coin from the first concept: those who copy Toyota keep only the "how," those who cling to tradition remember only the "how," and both have lost the "why."

The way out. The authors recommend continuous learning by doing: try new practices on a small scale, see what happens, adjust; encourage newcomers to ask "why do we do it like this?"; and periodically take routines off the shelf and argue the case for them afresh.

How it changes your view. When you hear "we've always done it this way," you ask: what problem was it originally meant to solve, and does that problem still exist?

4. Fear prevents acting on knowledge: drive out fear

What it is. The third source is fear. When mistakes are punished, telling the truth invites retaliation, and the next round of layoffs could land on anyone, people instinctively choose the safest course: try nothing new, report no bad news. Knowledge in such an organization does not vanish; nobody dares to use it. Frontline employees know where the problems are and keep quiet; managers know what needs changing and won't take the risk.

Mechanism and examples. Here the authors borrow from W. Edwards Deming, the statistician and quality pioneer who taught quality management in postwar Japan. One of his famous "14 Points for Management" is "drive out fear," on the grounds that frightened people hide data and dress up reports, so managers end up looking at a distorted world. The authors also cite the Harvard scholar Amy Edmondson's research on hospital nursing units (paraphrased): she expected units with better team climates to make fewer medication errors, but the data showed they reported more. The reason was that in openly led units nurses felt safe admitting mistakes, while in units with harsh managers the errors happened just the same and were simply hidden. Fear does not reduce mistakes; it only reduces how much you know about them.

Mistakes are the price of action. The book's fourth prescription: there is no doing without mistakes, and what matters is how the company responds. Any genuinely new attempt means some failures; if every failure requires a scapegoat, people soon learn not to try. The authors admire organizations that treat mistakes as learning material, and they distinguish two kinds of failure: failure in a reasonable attempt deserves forgiveness, while failure caused by carelessness and lack of effort deserves scrutiny.

An analogy. An organization is like a nervous system, and bad news is its sense of pain. Punishing people who report bad news is like cutting the pain nerves: you can rest your hand on the stove without feeling anything, until the wound festers.

How it changes your view. "Problems reported" stops being a number where lower is always better: few reports may simply mean nobody dares to speak.

5. Measurement obstructs good judgment: the number becomes the goal

What it is. The fourth source is measurement. Managers believe that "what gets measured gets managed," so they put metrics on everything. The authors' critique: many organizations measure too much, measure in overly complicated ways, measure things that lag too far behind, and measure what is easy to measure rather than what matters. When people are judged by these numbers, they chase the numbers rather than the thing the numbers were supposed to stand for.

The mechanism. First, with too many metrics people are lost, and in the end they attend only to the few tied to bonuses. Second, financial measures are outcome measures: they tell you what happened last quarter but not what to change; the authors prefer process measures, numbers that reflect how work is actually being done, such as how long it takes for a problem to go from discovered to solved. Third, metrics crowd out judgment: an employee sees that something would plainly help the customer, but it isn't in the evaluation, so it doesn't get done. Fourth, metrics displace philosophy: a company that claims to value long-term customer relationships but measures only this quarter's sales has turned its slogan into empty words.

An example (illustrative). A call center measures "average call length," intending to improve efficiency; agents learn to hang up as fast as possible, customers' problems go unsolved, they call back, and total workload rises. It is a familiar version of the problem the authors describe: the metric gets optimized and the goal gets sacrificed.

The way out. The seventh prescription: measure what matters and what helps turn knowledge into action. Measures should be few, simple, consistent with the company's philosophy, and useful for guiding the next step; above all, a metric is a tool that supports judgment, not a substitute for it.

How it changes your view. Faced with any metric, you ask two questions: what real thing does it stand for? And if someone chased only this number and ignored that real thing, what would happen?

6. Internal competition turns friends into enemies

What it is. The fifth source is internal competition. Many companies believe competition breeds efficiency, so they manufacture it internally: forced ranking (sorting employees into fixed proportions of top, middle and bottom performers and pushing out those at the bottom), departments fighting over budgets, rewards for individuals but never for teams. The authors' verdict: once colleagues become rivals, knowledge stops flowing. Why should I share my method with you, if learning it lets you leapfrog me?

"False analogies." The authors take particular aim at business's favorite metaphors from war and sport: business as battlefield, employees as players, as if internal combat would forge the fiercest warriors. But on a sports team, competition among teammates exists to beat the other side on match day; in many companies, internal competition ends up meaning players fighting their own teammates for the ball in the middle of the match. Hence the sixth prescription: beware of false analogies; fight the competition, not each other.

The evidence. The authors draw on research in psychology and education on cooperation versus competition: on tasks that require interdependence and the exchange of information, cooperative groups usually outperform competitive ones (paraphrased). The reason is not hard to see: turning knowledge into action often takes one person's idea, another's resources and a third's execution, and competition destroys precisely that exchange.

How it changes your view. When you design incentives, you add a question: will this system make people more willing to help their colleagues, or more eager to watch them fail?

7. The remedy: eight prescriptions, and doing as learning

The last two chapters turn to companies that crossed the gap and to prescriptions anyone can follow. The positive exemplars include Toyota, the software company SAS Institute and the menswear retailer Men's Wearhouse, among others (paraphrased). What they share is not knowing more but having a clear philosophy, leaders who model the behavior themselves, tolerance for trial and error, and a high degree of internal cooperation. Men's Wearhouse founder George Zimmer, for instance, put great weight on training store staff, with managers doing much of the teaching themselves (paraphrased), which is exactly "knowing by teaching others."

The eight prescriptions line up roughly with the five sources:

The eighth is the master switch. Employees ignore what the company says and watch what leaders do: which meetings they spend their time in, which projects get the money, whether a mistake triggers a blame hunt or a review. A leader who spends three hours listening to presentations and five minutes on the shop floor is telling everyone, through action, that presentations matter more than the floor. Closing the knowing-doing gap is ultimately a question of leaders' own behavior.

The Skeleton of the Argument

The whole book compresses into one line: companies invest heavily in knowledge but rarely get changed behavior in return → because crucial knowledge is tacit, gained by doing and passed on by teaching, not shipped in documents and databases → and organizations systematically stop people from doing: they reward talkers, enshrine past practice, punish mistakes, let metrics replace judgment, and set colleagues against each other → managers built all five, so managers can dismantle them → the direction of change: make the philosophy clear, let people learn by doing, tolerate mistakes, drive out fear, measure little but well, cooperate internally, and have leaders model it with their own time and resources.

What is the evidence? Mainly in-depth fieldwork and interviews at a range of companies, supplemented by research in organizational behavior and psychology (such as Amabile's finding that critics seem smarter, and the studies on cooperation and competition), plus well-known contrasting cases like Toyota and NUMMI. It is a cases-plus-literature-review book, not a controlled experiment.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: the authors are against learning and planning. No. They are against learning and planning that replace action. A good plan is part of action; the trouble is that many plans end the moment they are written.

Misreading two: the authors are against measurement. Also no. They object to measuring too much, measuring the wrong things, and letting metrics stand in for judgment; they argue for measures that are few, sharp, aligned with the philosophy, and useful for deciding the next step.

Misreading three: "just do it" is enough. The book's "action" is not blind activity but action grounded in a philosophy and designed to be learned from. Their insistence on reasons (the why) and on learning from mistakes exists precisely to prevent action for its own sake.

Criticism one: dated cases, and exemplars change. The book came out in 2000, and many examples date from the 1990s. Later events showed that crossing the gap is not permanent: Toyota fell into crisis with its massive recalls of 2009 and 2010, and its own leadership acknowledged that it had expanded too fast for quality management to keep up; Men's Wearhouse founder Zimmer was ousted by his board in 2013. This does not overturn the argument, but it reminds readers that an exemplar is a company that got certain things right at a certain moment, not a permanent model.

Criticism two: the evidence is mostly case-based and subject to selection bias. The authors chose companies they considered successful and then generalized from what they had in common; companies with the same clear philosophy and internal cooperation that nonetheless failed are not in the sample. Generalizing from winners cannot tell you whether those traits caused success or merely happen to accompany it. Interestingly, Pfeffer and Sutton themselves later attacked exactly this habit of treating success stories as evidence, in Hard Facts.

Criticism three: easier said than done. "Drive out fear," "cooperate internally," "lead by example": hardly any manager would disagree, and the real problem is how to do it under quarterly earnings pressure, shareholder demands, and layoffs that are sometimes genuinely necessary. The book offers more direction than operational steps. The greatest irony is that the book itself falls into the knowing-doing gap: readers finish it, nod, agree, and go back to business as usual. The authors know this too: change comes from doing, not from reading.

Criticism four: the case against internal competition may go too far. Letting several teams explore different directions in parallel and then picking the best is also a useful form of internal competition. The authors' real target is zero-sum design, where one person's win requires another's loss.

Pfeffer and Sutton Beyond This Book

Hard Facts and evidence-based management. Six years later the two co-wrote Hard Facts, Dangerous Half-Truths, and Total Nonsense (2006), which pushes The Knowing-Doing Gap one step further: the earlier book asks "why don't you act on what you know?"; this one asks, "is what you think you know actually true?" They proposed evidence-based management, borrowed from evidence-based medicine: a doctor's prescription should rest on the best current research evidence, not on habit, authority, or the pitch of a pharmaceutical rep. In their view management lags far behind medicine, and managers make decisions on three unreliable bases:

  • Casual benchmarking: copying whatever a successful company does without asking whether that practice caused its success or whether it fits your situation. This is the flip side of the "copy Toyota's kanban" problem in The Knowing-Doing Gap.
  • Doing what seems to have worked in the past: assuming that what worked at the last company, or in the last era, will work again, which corresponds to this book's "memory substitutes for thinking."
  • Untested ideology: managers acting on beliefs they hold deeply, such as "money is the best motivator," without ever having looked at the evidence.

The body of the book tests six popular "half-truths" one by one: Is work fundamentally different from the rest of life? Do the best organizations have the best people? Do financial incentives drive performance? Is strategy destiny? Is it really "change or die"? Are great leaders in control of their companies? Their answers are mostly "partly true, but badly exaggerated." On "the best organizations have the best people," for instance, their view is that systems and context often shape performance more than individual talent does; a star who changes companies frequently performs noticeably worse.

Running through the book is what they call the attitude of wisdom: acting decisively on current knowledge while staying ready to doubt whether what you know is right. That supplies the half of The Knowing-Doing Gap most easily misread: the earlier book urges action, but action presupposes knowing correctly. Together the two books make the full argument: first check that your knowledge survives the evidence, then turn it into action.

Sutton's The No Asshole Rule. Sutton's 2007 book The No Asshole Rule carries the chapters on fear and internal competition down to the interpersonal level: people who belittle and humiliate colleagues, however strong their individual results, leave those around them afraid, silent and defensive, and the organization pays far more for that than it gains from their output. Sutton argues that companies should state outright that such behavior will not be tolerated, and then actually enforce it, which is itself a test of the knowing-doing gap.

Pfeffer's "people as assets" thread. In his earlier The Human Equation (1998), Pfeffer argued that companies that treat employees as assets (employment security, serious training, fewer status distinctions, shared information) do better in the long run. The Knowing-Doing Gap continues that line: good people management is rare not because nobody knows about it, but because those who know cannot bring themselves to do it.

Crossovers

  • Eastern philosophy · Wang Yangming's unity of knowing and acting: In the Instructions for Practical Living, the Ming philosopher Wang Yangming says there has never been anyone who knows and does not act; to know and not act is simply not yet to know. This is more radical than the authors: a set of values on the wall that nobody practices is not organizational knowledge at all. Alike in using action as the test of knowledge; unlike in that Wang speaks of personal cultivation, the book of institutions that can be redesigned.
  • Buddhism · talking about food does not fill you: The Shurangama Sutra warns against Ananda's vast learning without practice, likening it to someone who talks about food all day and is never full. Alike in seeing talk pose as practice; unlike in that the sutra turns you inward, the book asks you to change what the organization rewards.
  • Evolutionary biology · Muir's hens: At Purdue, William Muir bred only the most productive individual hen each generation; within a few generations the flock pecked itself to pieces, while hens selected by whole-cage output became far more productive. Alike in that rewarding individuals can wreck the group; unlike in that hens are selected by genes, while people can change the system.
  • Economics · Goodhart's law: The British economist Charles Goodhart observed that once a statistical indicator is used as a target for control, the regularity it captured breaks down. Alike in showing that a metric gets chased away from its meaning; unlike in that Goodhart was talking about monetary policy, the book about employee evaluation.
  • Distributed systems · blameless postmortems: Operations practice such as Google's Site Reliability Engineering advocates blameless postmortems: after an outage, look for systemic causes, not someone to punish. Alike in trading away punishment for honest information about failure; unlike in that software leaves logs, while human concealment is hard to detect.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. Most organizations lack not knowledge but the capacity to turn knowledge into action.

2. Crucial knowledge is tacit: gained by doing, passed on by teaching others to do, and impossible to load into a database.

3. Anyone can tour Toyota and few can learn from it, because they copy the techniques and leave the philosophy behind: why before how.

4. Holding the meeting, recording the decision and writing the plan is not doing it; promoting the best talkers is what breeds empty talk.

5. Criticism sounds smarter than construction, so meeting rooms fill with fault-finders and empty of people with proposals.

6. Remember "we do it this way" and forget "why we do it this way," and memory has replaced thinking.

7. Fear does not reduce mistakes, only your knowledge of them; there is no doing without mistakes, so what counts is how you respond.

8. Metrics should be few, precise and aligned with the philosophy: a tool for judgment, not a replacement for it.

9. Fight the competition, not your colleagues; a system that makes people hide their best methods is hollowing out the organization.

10. Where leaders put their time and money is the organization's real signal; and before you act, test what you think you know against the evidence.
