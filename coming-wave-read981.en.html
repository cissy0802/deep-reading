The Coming Wave

The Coming Wave · Mustafa Suleyman with Michael Bhaskar · 2023

中文 →

In One Sentence

Artificial intelligence and synthetic biology are arriving together, and like every major technology before them they will keep getting cheaper and spreading further — history says waves like this have almost never been "contained," and this time, failing to contain it may cost more than we can afford. The whole book is a search for a narrow path between "impossible" and "necessary."

Where It Sits

Suleyman co-founded DeepMind in 2010 with Demis Hassabis and Shane Legg (Google later bought it), then started Inflection AI, and since 2024 has run Microsoft's AI division. This is a risk warning written by someone who builds the wave — which is both where the book gets its weight and where it is most often challenged.

Core Claims

  • The default state of technology is proliferation. Once a useful general-purpose technology appears, its cost falls, its users multiply, and it ends up in everyone's hands. Attempts in history to lock one away have almost all failed.
  • The coming wave, centered on AI and synthetic biology, is harder to contain and more dangerous than any before it. It has four features: asymmetric power, hyper-fast evolution, near-limitless uses, and growing autonomy.
  • That leaves us with a great dilemma: let it run and we risk catastrophe; clamp down with total surveillance and we get dystopia. Stopping isn't an option either — modern societies depend on the growth technology brings. The only way out is a "narrow path" of constraints applied at many levels at once: technical, corporate, national, international, cultural.

Put together: containment has almost never worked, and this time it has to; and success won't look like a single off switch but like a whole stack of imperfect, overlapping guardrails.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Technological waves: proliferation is the default

What it is. By a "wave" Suleyman means a cluster of technologies that erupts around a single general-purpose technology: fire, writing, agriculture, printing, steam, electricity, the internal combustion engine, the computer. Each solves not one problem but changes how almost every industry works. His central observation is that once a wave forms, it proliferates — prices fall, performance rises, use spreads, until it is as ubiquitous as air.

The mechanism: demand plus the cost curve. Useful things are wanted; wanted things get made cheaper; cheaper things get used more. The book traces the internal combustion engine: when Carl Benz built his first car in the 1880s it was a rich man's toy; Ford's assembly line made it a family purchase; a century or so later well over a billion vehicles are on the world's roads. Printing too: within decades of Gutenberg, presses had sprung up across Europe and the price of books kept falling. Nobody "decided" that cars or books should spread. Incentives did the pushing.

An analogy. Water running downhill: technology flows from the few who have it to the many who want it. A dam only holds it up for a while — and builds pressure behind it.

Why it matters. This is the foundation of the book. If technology stopped on its own, or a government could order it to stop, everything else would be moot. Suleyman asks the reader to accept an uncomfortable premise first: AI won't stay in a few companies' data centers. It will reach everyone the way phones did — including the people you'd least like to have it.

How it changes the way you see. When you size up a new technology, don't ask who can use it now; ask who will use it, and for what, ten years from now when it's a hundred times cheaper. What only a top lab can do today may run on a laptop in a few years.

2. The containment problem and the pessimism-aversion trap

What it is. "Containment" in this book is a broad term: the ability to monitor, restrict, control and, if necessary, shut down a technology — not just a technical switch but laws, norms, business structures and treaties. The "containment problem" asks whether we can keep that ability over a wave of technologies whose default is to spread. His answer comes in two halves, which serve as the poles of the book: containment is not possible; containment must be possible.

The one half-success: nuclear weapons. Why was nuclear technology largely contained? Because it is enormously expensive and hard to build (enriching uranium takes vast industrial plants), and it has essentially one use. There is the Non-Proliferation Treaty, and the number of nuclear-armed states is still in single digits. Even so, the Cold War was full of accidents and miscalculations that nearly ended in disaster — the best-contained technology in history only just scraped through. AI and synthetic biology are the opposite: cheap, accessible, endlessly useful.

Why it won't stop: incentives. A whole chapter lays out the forces driving the wave, and nearly every one is strong. Great-power rivalry: he describes how AlphaGo's defeat of Lee Sedol in 2016 and of Ke Jie the next year landed in China like a "Sputnik moment" — the jolt the Soviet satellite gave America in 1957, which kicked the space race into high gear. An open research culture in which papers and code are published freely. Enormous commercial returns. Genuine need, to tackle climate, disease and aging. And the ambition of the builders themselves. Each player, taken alone, is acting rationally; together they make a car with no one at the brake.

The pessimism-aversion trap. This is Suleyman's most personal concept: elites — in tech, politics and academia — instinctively look away when faced with potentially very dark prospects. He describes raising serious AI and biotech risks at meetings and getting polite silence, a change of subject, or a label of alarmism. The reasons aren't hard to see: admitting a problem may have no solution is painful; tech culture prizes optimism; and the bearer of bad news pays a career price. So the questions most in need of discussion are discussed least in exactly the rooms best placed to act on them.

An analogy. A company where everyone knows a core system is fragile, yet no one wants to be the first to say so in the meeting — because saying it means someone has to own it, and everyone would rather believe it "can't be that bad."

How it changes the way you see. When your first reaction to a serious risk is "let's not be so negative," stop and ask: have I actually assessed this, or do I just not want to think about it?

3. Four features of the new wave — and "capable" AI

What it is. Suleyman argues this wave differs not only in speed but in breaking all four conditions that made containment possible before:

FeatureMeaningWhy it makes containment harder
AsymmetryTiny actors can have enormous impactA small team, a drone or a piece of code can do damage that once took a state
Hyper-evolutionExtremely fast iteration and falling costsRegulation and social norms can't keep up; this year's rules are obsolete next year
Omni-useBeyond "dual-use": usable for almost any purposeThe same model can design drugs or poisons; banning only the "bad uses" is very hard
AutonomySystems increasingly act without step-by-step human directionTools traditionally had a human hand on the handle; autonomous systems may do things no one directly ordered

Table: Suleyman's four features of the coming wave, and how each erodes the possibility of containment.

A real case of omni-use. In 2022, researchers at a US drug-discovery company, Collaborations Pharmaceuticals, ran an experiment: they flipped the objective of a molecule-generating model built to avoid toxicity so that it would seek it. Within hours the model proposed some forty thousand potentially lethal molecules, including the known nerve agent VX and many new compounds with similar structures. Same tool, one parameter changed. That is what omni-use means.

The synthetic-biology half. The Human Genome Project cost around three billion dollars and took more than a decade to finish in 2003; sequencing a whole human genome now costs a few hundred dollars. CRISPR gene editing (published in 2012 by Jennifer Doudna, Emmanuelle Charpentier and colleagues) made editing DNA feel like editing a document, and benchtop DNA synthesizers are bringing the "printing" of genetic sequences out of big labs. The cost of reading, editing and writing life is falling exponentially — and an engineered pathogen needs no missile to reach the whole world.

Artificial capable intelligence (ACI) and the Modern Turing Test. Rather than arguing over whether AI is "really" intelligent or conscious, Suleyman says, look at what it can get done. He proposes an intermediate stage, ACI: AI that sits between today's chatbots and science-fiction superintelligence and can carry out complex, multi-step tasks on its own. His yardstick is a Modern Turing Test: give an AI $100,000 and see whether, over a few months, it can research a market, design a product, get a manufacturer to make it, sell it, and turn the money into $1 million. His point: what changes the world isn't AI that chats but AI that gets things done.

Why it matters. The four features turn a vague unease into a checklist. Score any new technology on asymmetry, speed, range of uses and autonomy; the higher the score, the harder it will be to contain.

How it changes the way you see. You ask less "is AI conscious?" and more "can AI get something done with nobody watching — and what is it getting done?"

4. The grand bargain and the fragility amplifiers: a state pulled two ways

What it is. The modern nation-state, Suleyman says, rests on a "grand bargain": citizens hand some of their freedom, and the right to use violence, to a centralized state in exchange for security, order and public services. The bargain holds because the state vastly outmatches any individual or group. The new wave undercuts exactly that premise.

The mechanism: amplify destructive power and disperse it. He calls the new technologies "fragility amplifiers": wherever there was already a crack, it widens faster. A familiar example: in 2017 the WannaCry ransomware infected hundreds of thousands of computers in over 150 countries within days, forcing many hospitals in Britain's National Health Service to cancel operations and fall back on pen and paper — and the exploit it used reportedly originated with the US National Security Agency before it leaked. A weapon built for a state, once loose, becomes a weapon anyone can turn on states. Add deepfaked video and voice, automated disinformation and cheap drones, and the trust and information channels states rely on in a crisis all come under strain.

Two opposite directions. States may deform in two opposite ways. One is centralization: AI makes mass surveillance cheap and precise, letting authoritarian states see and manage every individual as never before. The other is fragmentation: when small groups, big companies and even individuals gain capabilities once reserved for states — providing services, launching attacks, organizing communities — power leaks downward and outward. He calls this "Hezbollahization," after Lebanon's Hezbollah, which is both an armed group and a provider of schools, hospitals and welfare: a quasi-state inside the state. The same wave can push one country toward Big Brother and another toward a heap of loose sand.

An analogy. A building's load-bearing walls: when only the management office had tools that could knock down walls, everyone was happy to hand them the keys. Now every tenant can buy a power drill, and management must either put cameras in every flat or watch the building get remodeled beyond recognition.

How it changes the way you see. AI risk is not only "will the machines get out of control?" but "will the institutional framework we use to coordinate with one another get out of control?"

5. The great dilemma and the narrow path

What it is. Stack the previous ideas and you get the book's core predicament, "the dilemma": open things up and we may get catastrophe — an engineered pandemic, a massive infrastructure collapse; lock things down to prevent it and we may get dystopia — a state that watches everyone. There is a third option, stopping altogether, which he sees as just as fatal: modern societies need continued growth and innovation to deal with climate change, aging populations and resource pressure. Stagnation is a catastrophe of its own.

The way out: ten layers of containment. Suleyman offers no master solution, only a set of mutually reinforcing measures running from the layer closest to the code all the way out to society:

LayerGist (paraphrased)
1 Technical safetyAn "Apollo program" for AI safety research; build constraints and shutdown capability into the design
2 AuditsLet outsiders check what models and labs are doing; transparency is the basis of trust
3 Choke pointsUse the few critical nodes in the supply chain — cutting-edge chips and fabrication tools — to slow the spread and buy time
4 MakersCritics can't just stand outside; people who understand the risks should help build
5 BusinessesDesign corporate structures that bind profit to purpose
6 GovernmentsStates need the capacity to understand and regulate technology, and to own it where necessary
7 AlliancesInternational treaties and cooperation, in the spirit of nuclear non-proliferation or the Montreal Protocol on the ozone layer
8 CultureA safety culture that admits failure openly and learns from accidents
9 MovementsPublic pressure and participation to push all of the above into practice
10 The narrow pathMake the nine layers work together as a whole — no single layer is enough

Table: Suleyman's "ten steps toward containment," from the innermost technical layer to the outermost social one.

Take choke points. Production of the most advanced AI chips is concentrated in very few hands: the most advanced lithography machines come almost exclusively from ASML in the Netherlands, and leading-edge chip fabrication is dominated by TSMC. In October 2022 the United States imposed export controls on advanced chips and chipmaking equipment bound for China. Suleyman treats measures like these as ways to buy time — they can't hold the wave back forever, but they can slow it enough for institutions to catch up.

An analogy. Flood defense: no single levee stops every flood. What works is reservoirs, levees, drainage, early warning, insurance and evacuation plans all at once — each leaks, but stacked together they keep disaster within survivable bounds.

Why it matters. It turns containment from a yes-or-no question into a question of degree: not "can we control it completely?" but "can we make the worst outcomes rare enough, late enough and small enough?"

How it changes the way you see. You grow wary of two opposite voices: "technology is unstoppable, regulation is pointless," and "one good law will sort it out." The first is surrender, the second is illusion; what Suleyman wants is a middle path that is laborious and never finished being repaired.

The Spine of the Argument

① History: major technologies arrive in waves and proliferate by default; successful containment is almost unknown, nuclear weapons being the barely-passing exception.

② This wave: AI and synthetic biology arrive together with asymmetry, hyper-evolution, omni-use and autonomy, making them far harder to contain than nuclear technology.

③ The drivers: great-power rivalry, open research, profit, real needs and personal ambition push together; no one can stop unilaterally.

④ The consequences: the technology amplifies existing fragilities, shakes the state's grand bargain, and pushes states toward surveillance or fragmentation.

⑤ The dilemma: openness risks catastrophe, lockdown means dystopia, and stagnation is a dead end too.

⑥ The way out: ten imperfect but overlapping layers of containment along a narrow path — containment is not a destination but a condition that has to be maintained forever.

How does he make his case? With two things. First, historical analogy: engines, printing and nuclear weapons yield the generalization that proliferation is the rule and containment the exception. Second, insider observation: Suleyman lived through the decade-plus in which deep learning went from lab curiosity to industry, and much of what he says about the pace of capability gains and the industry's incentives comes from that experience. The first supplies the general law; the second supplies the evidence for why this time is faster and harder. It reads more like a serious risk assessment than a rigorous academic argument.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "It's an AI doom book." It isn't. Suleyman explicitly rejects stopping, and believes AI can deliver huge benefits in medicine, energy and science; his stance is "optimistic about technology, serious about risk." The catastrophe scenarios are there to argue for containment, not to prophesy. Compared with genuine doomers — Eliezer Yudkowsky, say, who argues that whoever builds superintelligence ends humanity — he is far more moderate.

Misreading 2: "Containment means banning." Containment in the book is closer to "controllability": being able to see, slow down, correct and, if necessary, switch off. A ban is the last and clumsiest tool in the box.

Criticism 1: Can a wave-maker credibly call for dams? The most common and most forceful objection. While writing the book Suleyman was running an AI startup, and soon after publication he joined Microsoft to lead its consumer AI business — he is inside the very race he says no one can brake. Critics argue that "we're dangerous, so we need regulation" can in effect help a few big firms raise barriers to entry — "regulatory capture," where the regulated help write the rules and the rules end up protecting the leaders. His early health project at DeepMind also ran into trouble: in 2017 the UK Information Commissioner's Office ruled that London's Royal Free hospital trust had failed to comply with data-protection law in sharing some 1.6 million patients' records with DeepMind. In the book, Suleyman doesn't hide that he is part of the race.

Criticism 2: "Inevitable" is technological determinism. Many historians of technology reject the claim that technology spreads by default and can't be stopped, and counterexamples aren't hard to find. Human reproductive cloning has been all but sealed off worldwide by norms and law. When He Jiankui announced the birth of gene-edited babies in 2018 he was condemned globally and sent to prison, and germline editing remains tightly restricted. These cases show that when a society reaches enough consensus, proliferation can be slowed dramatically. Suleyman's likely reply: those technologies carry far weaker commercial and military incentives than AI.

Criticism 3: The ten steps are too broad. Many reviewers found the diagnosis much stronger than the prescription. "More audits," "international alliances," "a safety culture" all point the right way, but how to implement them, who enforces them and how to agree on them amid great-power rivalry get little space.

Criticism 4: Attacks from opposite directions. On one side, the open-source community and some researchers (such as Yann LeCun, formerly Meta's chief AI scientist) argue the real danger is AI monopolized by a few companies and governments, and that openness is the check — precisely where Suleyman's worry about open proliferation puts him on the other side. On the other, scholars focused on AI ethics and fairness argue that dwelling on long-range catastrophic risk distracts from real harms now: bias, surveillance, labor exploitation, disinformation. Suleyman tries to straddle both, and each side thinks he leans toward the other.

What still stands. The diagnosis of the pessimism-aversion trap, the analytical frame of omni-use and asymmetry, and the catastrophe-versus-dystopia structure are frequently cited in policy debate; chip export controls, safety evaluations of frontier models and the international AI safety summits have all moved, to varying degrees, in directions the book sketches.

Crossovers

  • Sociology · Perrow's "normal accidents": Studying the Three Mile Island accident, Charles Perrow argued that in any system both interactively complex and tightly coupled, accidents are normal, not aberrations — the very structure of a "fragility amplifier." The difference: Perrow concluded some technologies shouldn't be used at all; Suleyman thinks stopping is itself a catastrophe.
  • Philosophy · Heidegger's "enframing": In "The Question Concerning Technology," Heidegger argued that modern technology is a way of seeing that turns everything into a resource on call (Gestell). The parallel: both deny technology is a neutral tool. The difference: Heidegger fears our way of seeing is being remade; Suleyman fears power is being redistributed.
  • Psychology · optimism bias: Neuroscientist Tali Sharot found that people underestimate their odds of bad outcomes and update less on bad news than good. The pessimism-aversion trap looks like its group-level version. The difference: Suleyman stresses the social and career costs that keep people quiet, not just a cognitive bias.
  • Political economy · "the narrow corridor": In The Narrow Corridor (2019), Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson argue that liberty survives only in a tight passage: a state too weak leaves society in disorder; a state too strong and unchecked becomes despotism; only when state and society push against each other and grow stronger together do you stay in the corridor. That is nearly the same shape as catastrophe versus dystopia. The difference: they describe centuries of institutional evolution; Suleyman faces technology that turns over every few years.
  • Evolutionary biology · the Red Queen: In 1973 Leigh Van Valen proposed that species must keep evolving just to hold their place in the race against predators and pathogens. Containment is the same: it is never "done." The difference: evolution has no designer, while containment requires deliberate coordination.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① The default state of technology is proliferation: useful things get cheaper, and cheap things reach everyone.

② Containment is not possible; containment must be possible — the book lives between those two sentences.

③ Nuclear weapons were largely contained because they are expensive, hard to build and single-purpose; AI and synthetic biology are none of those.

④ Each player, taken alone, is rational; together they make a car with no one at the brake.

⑤ The risks most in need of discussion are discussed least in the rooms best placed to act — that is the pessimism-aversion trap.

⑥ Asymmetry, hyper-evolution, omni-use, autonomy: the higher a technology scores on these four, the harder it is to govern.

⑦ Stop arguing about whether AI is conscious; see whether it can turn $100,000 into $1 million on its own.

⑧ New technologies are fragility amplifiers, pulling states toward surveillance and fragmentation at once.

⑨ Letting go is catastrophe, clamping down is dystopia, standing still is a dead end — what's left is a narrow path.

⑩ Containment is not a switch but a net of ten layers, each leaky, that together can hold — and it will always need mending.
