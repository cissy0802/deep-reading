The Righteous Mind

The Righteous Mind: Why Good People Are Divided by Politics and Religion · Jonathan Haidt · 2012

中文 →

In One Sentence

Moral judgments aren't reasoned into existence — they are felt first and justified afterward; every moral sense is built from a handful of evolved "taste receptors," and different people and different camps mix them in different proportions — which is why good people split into sides that are sincere, furious, and baffled by each other.

Where It Sits

Haidt is a social psychologist (first at the University of Virginia, later at NYU's Stern School of Business) and the leading voice of the intuitionist school in moral psychology. This book is the assembled result of twenty years of work: his own experiments, anthropology, evolutionary theory and American political polarization, fused into a single theory of where morality comes from.

The Core Argument

The book has three parts, and Haidt boils each one down to a slogan:

  • 1. Intuitions come first, strategic reasoning second. Our sense of whether something is right or wrong arrives almost instantly as an intuition; reasoning shows up later as a defense attorney, not a judge.
  • 2. There's more to morality than harm and fairness. Human morality rests on at least six foundations: care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity and liberty. Western liberals lean almost entirely on the first two; conservatives — and most cultures on earth — use all six.
  • 3. Morality binds and blinds. Humans are "90 percent chimp and 10 percent bee" — selfish, yet capable of losing ourselves in a group. Morality evolved to bind people into teams, and once we're on a team we stop being able to see the other side's point.

Put together: fights over politics and religion are not, at bottom, clever people versus stupid ones or good people versus evil ones. They are clashes between different recipes of moral intuition — and each side is blinded by its own recipe.

The Key Concepts, One by One

1. The rider and the elephant: reasoning is the press secretary, not the decider

Haidt's signature image: the mind is a rider on the back of an elephant. The elephant is intuition — the automatic, fast, emotionally loaded processes that make up the vast bulk of mental life. The rider is conscious reasoning — slow, effortful, able to put things into words. It feels as if the rider is steering. Haidt's view is that the rider is mostly the elephant's servant: whichever way the elephant leans, the rider's job is to come up with reasons for going that way. He can occasionally talk the elephant round, but most of the time he explains rather than decides.

The image rests on solid evidence. The best known is moral dumbfounding. Haidt told subjects stories that were harmless but disturbing. A family's dog is killed by a car; they've heard dog meat is tasty, so they cook and eat it, and nobody sees. A man buys a whole chicken at the supermarket every week, has sex with it, then cooks and eats it. Two adult siblings, Julie and Mark, decide on a trip to sleep together once; they use two forms of contraception, both consent, they never do it again, tell no one, and feel closer afterward. Was it wrong?

Most people say "wrong" at once and offer reasons: the children could be deformed (but they used double contraception), it will damage their relationship (but the story says they grew closer)… Once each reason is knocked down, they don't change their verdict. They say something like "I can't explain why, I just know it's wrong." The judgment stays put while the reasons are swapped out one after another — conclusion first, justification later, caught in the act.

Haidt formalized this as the social intuitionist model, first laid out in a 2001 paper whose title says it all: "The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail." The dog wags the tail, not the other way round. In the model, reasoning's main job is social: we reason to persuade others and to look defensible in front of them. What actually changes a person's intuitions is usually someone else's reasons — especially from people we like and trust.

More evidence converges from different directions:

  • Emotion before judgment. The neuroscientist Antonio Damasio studied patients with damage to the ventromedial prefrontal cortex (the region that feeds emotional signals into decision-making). Their IQ was intact and they could list pros and cons perfectly well, yet they couldn't settle on which day to book an appointment. Without the elephant, the rider just goes in circles.
  • Split-second verdicts. The psychologist Alexander Todorov showed people the faces of two unfamiliar candidates for a moment and asked which looked more "competent." That snap read predicted roughly two-thirds of U.S. Senate races.
  • Partisan "addiction." During the 2004 election, the psychologist Drew Westen put committed partisans in a brain scanner and had them read self-contradictory statements by their own candidate. Reasoning areas barely stirred; when a piece of exculpatory information resolved the contradiction, reward-related areas lit up. Haidt's reading: finding an excuse for your own side feels to the brain like a small reward — partisanship may genuinely have an addictive streak.

So what is reasoning for? Haidt borrows the Ring of Gyges from Glaucon in Plato's Republic: if you owned a ring that made you invisible, so that you'd never be caught doing wrong, would you still behave? Glaucon thought not — people care about seeming just, not being just. Haidt says psychology sides with Glaucon. Philip Tetlock found that people think open-mindedly only when they know in advance they'll have to account for themselves to an audience whose views are unknown and who cares about accuracy. The rest of the time, reasoning is public relations. Haidt calls it the "inner lawyer" or "press secretary."

One contrast is easy to remember (Haidt takes it from the psychologist Thomas Gilovich): faced with a conclusion we want to believe, we ask "Can I believe it?" — one piece of supporting evidence will do. Faced with one we don't want to believe, we ask "Must I believe it?" — one counterargument and we can toss it. That is confirmation bias at full strength in morality and politics.

How it changes the way you see things: when you argue with someone you try to beat their rider with logic, but their rider is just a lawyer, and the elephant behind him hasn't budged. To persuade, talk to the elephant first — build goodwill and trust, make the other person feel understood, and only then will their rider go looking for reasons on your side. The reverse holds too: when you feel utterly certain about a moral judgment but your reasons keep changing, that's probably your elephant talking.

2. The narrow morality of WEIRD people: beyond harm and fairness

When Haidt was a graduate student, mainstream moral psychology (in the tradition of Piaget and Kohlberg) held that morality is the gradual development of a child's ability to reason about not harming others and treating them fairly. Haidt suspected this was just one local morality.

In 1993 he took those "harmless taboo" stories to two Brazilian cities and to Philadelphia and put them to people of different social classes. Philadelphia college students mostly said "if no one was hurt, it isn't wrong." Working-class people in both Brazil and the U.S. were far more likely to call the acts wrong even with no victim. Class mattered more than country. The Western-educated were the odd ones out.

He then spent three months in Bhubaneswar, in Orissa, India, following the lead of the cultural psychologist Richard Shweder. Shweder divided morality into three "ethics": the ethic of autonomy (people are individuals, and morality protects individual rights and welfare); the ethic of community (people are first members of families, castes and nations, and morality means fulfilling the duties of your role); and the ethic of divinity (people are vessels of the divine, and morality means keeping body and soul pure). There, food, cleanliness and the order of elders and juniors were all moral matters.

This is where an acronym comes in handy: WEIRD — coined in 2010 by Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan — Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic. The overwhelming majority of psychology's subjects are university students from WEIRD societies, and on many measures they are outliers within the human species. WEIRD people tend to see the world as a collection of separate individuals rather than a web of relationships — so morality naturally narrows to "don't harm each other, don't cheat."

How it changes the way you see things: "anything goes as long as it doesn't hurt anyone" feels like universal common sense, but across history and the present-day world it is a minority view. That doesn't mean accepting every rule of every other society, but you'll stop writing off people who disagree with you as simply ignorant — they may be sensing with receptors your morality doesn't use.

3. Moral Foundations Theory: the righteous mind is a tongue with six taste receptors

This is the book's central model. With colleagues including Jesse Graham and Craig Joseph, Haidt developed Moral Foundations Theory. His metaphor: the righteous mind is like a tongue with several kinds of taste receptor. Every human tongue senses sweet, sour, salty, bitter and savory, yet cuisines combine them in wildly different proportions. Likewise, all humans share the same few moral receptors, and different cultures and political camps blend them into different "moral cuisines."

Each foundation is a mental module that evolved to solve a recurring adaptive challenge. It is set off by certain "original triggers" and, in modern life, has spread to many "current triggers" (a distinction Haidt borrows from the anthropologist Dan Sperber's "proper" versus "actual" domains). The table below condenses the book's summary chart:

A few key details:

  • Sanctity is the hardest foundation for WEIRD people to grasp, and one of the most explanatory. An omnivore has to stay alert to what might make it sick, and disgust is the alarm. Later the alarm spread to bodies, sex and even ideas — hence "the body is a temple," hence the feeling that some acts defile. Secular liberals use it too: the fixation on "natural" food and "polluted" environments runs on the same foundation.
  • Fairness means two different things on the left and the right. Haidt later refined it: for the left, fairness is mostly equality; for the right it is mostly proportionality — people who contribute more should get more, and freeloaders should be punished, a kind of folk karma. Both sides say "fair"; they mean different things.
  • Liberty was added to explain an anomaly. American libertarians (who favor minimal government and put individual liberty above all) scored low on almost every foundation yet were intensely sensitive to being controlled — they needed a receptor of their own.

Haidt's team collected hundreds of thousands of Moral Foundations Questionnaires at YourMorals.org and produced the book's most famous chart: liberals rely mainly on care and fairness (plus liberty), while conservatives weight all the foundations roughly equally. The conservative moral cuisine, in other words, uses more spices. Haidt uses this to explain why Republicans often connect with voters better: their language hits more receptors at once, including loyalty, authority and sanctity — channels liberals barely hear.

The most awkward finding: Haidt and colleagues had thousands of Americans fill out the questionnaire as the other side would (answering as a typical liberal or a typical conservative). Conservatives and moderates impersonated liberals quite accurately. The worst at imagining the other side were the "very liberal" — who particularly underestimated how much conservatives care about care and fairness, assuming they don't care about suffering. Haidt's reading: if you run on one or two foundations, it is hard to imagine others reasoning with foundations you don't have, so the only explanation left is that they must be bad.

How it changes the way you see things: next time a political view makes you think "how could anyone believe that?", ask a different question: which receptor is it hitting? Opposing gay marriage, objecting to flag burning, demanding respect for the police — these are often not indifference to harm but moral signals sent on a channel you haven't switched on. Seeing the channel isn't agreeing, but it's where conversation can start.

4. 90 percent chimp, 10 percent bee: group selection and the "hive switch"

Part three asks a deeper question: why did morality grow this way — why do people sacrifice themselves for groups and rage at "betrayal" and "desecration"? Haidt's answer is the boldest and most contested step in the book: multilevel selection, the idea that natural selection operates not only between individuals (and genes) but also between groups.

The arithmetic goes like this. Within a group, selfish members outdo selfless ones, so individual-level selection favors selfishness. But between groups, groups with more selfless members who pull together beat groups that are a rabble. The biologists David Sloan Wilson and E. O. Wilson summed it up (paraphrased): selfishness beats altruism within groups; altruistic groups beat selfish groups. Haidt also quotes Darwin himself in The Descent of Man (paraphrased): a tribe with many members who are loyal, brave and ready to sacrifice for the common good would triumph over most other tribes — and this would be natural selection.

He goes further, borrowing the biological idea of major transitions (from John Maynard Smith and Eörs Szathmáry): several leaps in the history of life happened when formerly independent, competing units merged into a higher-level individual — single cells into multicellular organisms, solitary insects into "superorganisms" like ants and bees, where each worker lives almost entirely for the hive. Humans, Haidt argues, went through something like half a major transition. Through shared intentionality (the developmental psychologist Michael Tomasello's term for our ability to form joint goals with others and divide the labor — something chimps can't do) and gene-culture coevolution, we acquired a measure of "beeness." Hence the slogan: we are 90 percent chimp and 10 percent bee — most of the time out for ourselves, but carrying a capacity to lose ourselves in a group.

Haidt calls that capacity the hive switch: under certain conditions the "I" temporarily dissolves into "we," and people feel an ecstasy beyond the self. Many things can flip it:

  • Moving in sync. The historian William McNeill recalled drilling for hours on end in the army during World War II and feeling a strange sense of personal enlargement, which he called "muscular bonding," arguing that communal dance and drill are humanity's ancient technology for binding groups.
  • Collective effervescence. The sociologist Émile Durkheim's term for the electricity that runs through a crowd chanting and dancing together in ritual — the individual feels lifted by a force larger than himself. Raves, stadium singalongs and mass marches manufacture it today.
  • Awe. The feeling of being tiny before a starry sky or the Grand Canyon can also briefly turn the self off.

The hive switch is the engine of morality's binding and the source of its blinding: what it produces is often parochial altruism — generosity toward insiders, indifference or hostility toward outsiders. The same psychology that gets neighbors to give blood can get a mob to do what none of its members would do alone.

How it changes the way you see things: fervor for a team, a nation or a faith isn't a failure of reason; it is an ancient design doing its job. You'll see why good organizations deliberately cultivate rituals and shared experiences — and be a little warier of any group that asks you to forget yourself.

5. Religion is a team sport: Durkheimian societies

The "New Atheists," led by Richard Dawkins and Daniel Dennett, treat religion as a false belief, an evolutionary byproduct, even a "virus of the mind." Haidt says they make a category error: they test religion as a set of claims about the world, when its main function isn't to describe the world but to bind people together. Looking only at belief is like studying football fans' conviction that their team is "the best" while ignoring the stadium, the singing and the strangers hugging.

One line of evidence comes from the anthropologist Richard Sosis's study of some two hundred nineteenth-century American communes: religious communes lasted far longer than secular ones, and the more a religious commune demanded of its members (giving up alcohol, fasting, uniform dress…), the longer it survived — a pattern that didn't hold for secular communes. The costs work precisely because they are sacralized, beyond negotiation, so no one feels free to free-ride. Survey work by the sociologist Robert Putnam found that devout Americans give more money, volunteer more and help neighbors more than secular Americans — and what predicts that generosity is not what they believe but how tied they are to people in their congregation. Belonging, not believing, does the work.

From this Haidt draws a contrast between two kinds of society: a Millian society (built on John Stuart Mill's principle of individual liberty — do as you like so long as you don't harm others) and a Durkheimian society (built on groups, families and order, where individuals find their footing by being bound by norms). Liberal morality is designed for the former, conservative morality fits the latter better, and Haidt argues that because of our "10 percent bee," people in a purely Millian society tend to suffer anomie (Durkheim's word for the rootless disorientation that follows when norms dissolve).

How it changes the way you see things: to judge a tradition or an organization, don't only ask "are its claims true?" — ask "whom does it bind together, how tightly, and at what cost?"

6. Moral matrices: you can only talk once you can see the other side's

Borrowing the film The Matrix (a simulated world its inhabitants live inside without noticing), Haidt says every political camp lives inside a moral matrix — a shared web of narratives, sacred objects and no-go zones, woven from one particular set of foundations. Inside the matrix everything is obvious; people outside it look insane.

Haidt's final position is yin and yang. Liberals are good at seeing victims and pushing for change. Conservatives are good at protecting "moral capital" — the norms, institutions and trust that took centuries to build and are hard to regrow once torn down. Libertarians remind everyone that markets often solve problems better than governments. Each is a necessary corrective to the others.

How it changes the way you see things: you're inside a matrix too. Wisdom isn't escaping every matrix (you can't); it's being able to make out the outline of at least one other — what it holds sacred and what it is protecting.

The Spine of the Book

The whole book compressed into one chain of argument:

  • Mechanism: moral judgment is fast intuitive perception; reasoning is after-the-fact social justification (evidence: moral dumbfounding, emotional brain damage, snap judgments, partisan brain scans).
  • Content: the intuitions are organized into several "moral foundations," each with its own evolutionary history; WEIRD liberals use two or three, most cultures and conservatives use more (evidence: cross-cultural fieldwork and hundreds of thousands of questionnaires).
  • Function: the foundations helped humans form groups that could cooperate and compete; selection between groups and the hive switch gave us partial "beeness," and religion is its most developed form.
  • Conclusion: so divisions over politics and religion are inevitable — each camp is bound by its recipe and blinded by it. The way forward isn't to crush the other side with logic but to approach their elephant first and see their matrix.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Common misreadings

  • Misreading 1: "Reasoning is useless; people are irrational." Haidt's point is that your own reasoning rarely changes your intuitions, but other people's reasoning can — and people with different views checking each other can produce good collective reasoning.
  • Misreading 2: "The book says conservative morality is superior." Haidt says only that conservatives use more foundations and so find it easier to understand the other side — not that more foundations means more right. Every side has blind spots.
  • Misreading 3: "The foundations are hard-wired genetic programs." He borrows the psychologist Gary Marcus's line: nature writes only the first draft; experience revises it.

Criticisms

  • Group selection is the biggest fight. Most evolutionary biologists — Steven Pinker in his 2012 essay "The False Allure of Group Selection," along with Dawkins and Jerry Coyne — argue that human cooperation is explained by kin selection (helping relatives helps shared genes) and reciprocity (I help you today, you help me tomorrow), with no need for selection between groups; and because groups constantly mix through migration, "altruistic groups" can hardly hold a stable edge. The step Haidt leans on remains unsettled.
  • How many foundations there are isn't stable. Five (then six) has an element of arbitrariness, and the questionnaire's factor structure isn't clean in many countries' data. In 2023 Mohammad Atari, Graham and colleagues released a revised questionnaire (MFQ-2) that splits fairness into "equality" and "proportionality" — and leaves liberty out. The theory is still being rebuilt.
  • The "all morality is harm" rival. The psychologist Kurt Gray's theory of dyadic morality holds that judging something immoral ultimately means perceiving a perpetrator–victim structure; with "harmless taboos," people quietly imagine a victim. If he's right, the six foundations could collapse into one: perceived harm.
  • Intuitionism may overreach. Dual-process work by Joshua Greene and others (for example, Paxton, Ungar and Greene's 2012 experiments) finds that given time and a good argument, reasoning really does change moral judgments. The rider may be stronger than Haidt allows, especially over long reflection.
  • Some experiments didn't survive the replication crisis. Several "disgust priming" studies the book draws on (hypnotically induced disgust making moral judgments harsher, a bad smell making people sterner) shrank sharply or nearly vanished in later replications and meta-analyses. The broad claim that emotion shapes moral judgment is still widely accepted; the dramatic version in which a whiff of disgust flips a verdict should be discounted.
  • Is the symmetry a false balance? Some critics think Haidt portrays left and right too symmetrically; others note that "conservatives use all the foundations" rests mainly on American data and is unstable across cultures.

Haidt Beyond This Book

The Happiness Hypothesis (2006) is where the rider and elephant come from — and it's about happiness, not politics. Haidt treats ancient wisdom from the Buddha, the Stoics, the Bible and others as "hypotheses" and tests them with modern psychology. His conclusion is that happiness comes from "between" — from getting the right relationships between yourself and others, your work, and something larger than you. It is the personal version of this book's "hive" section.

The Coddling of the American Mind (2018, with Greg Lukianoff) applies moral psychology to the next generation. It attacks three "Great Untruths" popular on campus — "what doesn't kill you makes you weaker," "always trust your feelings," and "life is a battle between good people and evil people" — which are the exact opposite of what cognitive behavioral therapy teaches. The last of them is this book's "moral blinding" turned into an institution.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · The blind men and the elephant (Udāna): in a Pali sutta, a king has men blind from birth each touch one part of an elephant; the one who feels an ear says it's like a winnowing basket, the one who feels a leg says it's like a pillar, and they fall to fighting. The Buddha uses it for sectarians who each cling to one side — the same structure as camps that use only a few moral receptors. The difference: the Buddha implies there is a vantage point from which the whole elephant is visible; Haidt thinks no one fully escapes their matrix.
  • Chinese philosophy · Xunzi on human nature and ritual: Xunzi held that people are born with desires that, left alone, breed conflict, so the sages created ritual (li) to "divide" — to assign roles, mark hierarchy and temper desire so the group can live together. That is strikingly close to Haidt's Durkheimian society, where norms and order are not shackles but devices for living in groups. The difference: Xunzi says ritual is a deliberate human artifice; Haidt says the need for order was installed by evolution.
  • Distributed systems · Quorum consensus: a distributed database has independent nodes vote, so as long as a majority is healthy, a few faulty nodes can't corrupt the result. Haidt's claim that individual reasoning is biased but diverse people together can correct each other runs on the same principle: reliability comes from redundancy and diversity, not from any one perfect node. The difference: consensus algorithms assume failures are independent, while partisan biases are highly correlated — an echo chamber where everyone is wrong together is exactly the correlated failure consensus systems fear most. A university or newsroom that becomes ideologically uniform has removed the quorum's founding assumption.

The Book in Ten Sentences

1. Moral judgment is delivered by intuition first; reasoning arrives as the defense lawyer. The dog wags the tail, not the tail the dog.

2. The mind is a rider on an elephant: the rider thinks he's steering, but mostly he's finding reasons for where the elephant is already going.

3. For conclusions we like we ask "Can I believe it?"; for ones we don't, "Must I believe it?" — that's the whole secret of confirmation bias.

4. We care more about seeming just than being just; reasoning is first of all PR.

5. To persuade someone, talk to the elephant first: goodwill and trust come before their rider will look for reasons on your side.

6. The righteous mind is a tongue with six taste receptors — care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, liberty — and the camps differ only in their recipes.

7. "Anything goes if it doesn't hurt anyone" is a local WEIRD view, not humanity's default setting.

8. We are 90 percent chimp and 10 percent bee; the hive switch lets us lose ourselves in a group — and go blind to outsiders.

9. Religion is first a team sport: what matters is often not what you believe but whom you belong with.

10. Morality binds and blinds: you can't step outside your own matrix, but you can learn to see someone else's.
