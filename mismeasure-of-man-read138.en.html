The Mismeasure of Man

The Mismeasure of Man · Stephen Jay Gould · 1981 (revised and expanded edition, 1996)

中文 →

In One Sentence

For more than a century — first with skulls, then with IQ tests — scientists kept "proving" that the poor, women, immigrants and people of color were innately less intelligent; Gould went back to their raw data and argued that the trouble was never just sloppy measurement but two deeper mistakes: treating "intelligence," an abstraction, as a physical thing inside the head (reification), and then collapsing the rich, many-sided variety of human minds onto a single scale from low to high (ranking) — and numbers are superbly good at dressing up a prejudice you already held as objective fact.

Where It Sits

Gould (1941–2002) was a Harvard paleontologist and evolutionary theorist (co-author of the theory of punctuated equilibrium) and one of the finest science essayists of the twentieth century. This book is his most sustained assault on "biological determinism." It won the National Book Critics Circle Award the year it came out, and it remains one of the most cited — and most fiercely contested — books in the history of science, psychometrics and research ethics.

The Core Argument

  • Biological determinism is a social theory wearing a lab coat. Its claim is that the social and economic gaps between groups — classes, races, sexes — flow from inborn, heritable differences, so the social order simply mirrors biology. Gould sets out to show that the claim returns in every generation with a new measuring instrument, and that each generation's "evidence" collapses on inspection.
  • Science is a socially embedded human activity, and bias gets in less through fraud than through unconscious choices. The book has almost no villains. Its subjects are mostly sincere, diligent people who believed themselves objective — which is exactly why their errors are worth studying: they show how expectation quietly decides what you measure, how you measure it, and which data count as "anomalies" to be thrown out.
  • The real errors are conceptual: reification and ranking. Even with flawless measurement, once you assume "intelligence" is a single, locatable, heritable, rankable thing, you will read a hierarchy out of any data at all — and that assumption has never been demonstrated.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Reification and ranking: the two mental moves behind biological determinism

Reification literally means "making into a thing": treating an abstract concept as if it were a concrete entity. "Intelligence" starts life as a loose label for a big bundle of different abilities — doing sums, building sentences, finding your way, reading people, remembering faces. Reification is the quiet extra step: deciding that there exists a single thing called intelligence, sitting somewhere in the brain, present in a definite amount, expressible as one number.

An analogy. "Nice weather" is a useful summary of temperature, humidity, wind and sunshine. But if someone claimed that niceness was a substance in the air, measurable with an instrument ("niceness: 87"), and then ranked cities by it and declared that some cities were simply born to bad weather, you would sense at once that something had gone wrong. Gould's charge is that the history of intelligence testing is the history of measuring and ranking "niceness."

Ranking is the second move: squeezing complex, multidimensional difference onto one linear ladder. Human variation is a landscape with many dimensions; ranking flattens it into a ruler, so every person and every group gets a "position" — and once there are positions, "who's on top" lines up neatly with the social hierarchy already in place.

Why are the two moves so dangerous together? Because they form a chute that runs straight from data to policy: first intelligence is a thing, then it can be ranked, then it is mostly inherited, and finally it cannot be changed. Slide all the way down and the people at the bottom are there "by nature," and helping them is a waste. The whole book is an effort to pull out each board of that chute.

How it changes the way you see things: whenever you meet a single number that ranks people — not only IQ but composite scores, talent indices, university league tables — ask two questions. Is there really one thing behind this number, or many different things weighted and blended together? And: what got thrown away when it was all laid out on a line?

2. Unconscious finagling: Morton's skulls and Broca's brains

Gould's most famous case is Samuel George Morton, a nineteenth-century Philadelphia physician who collected more than a thousand human skulls. He filled them with mustard seed — later with lead shot — poured the filling into a graduated cylinder to get "cranial capacity," and ranked the races: whites (especially those he called "Teutonic") at the top, Native Americans in the middle, Black people at the bottom. Morton's contemporaries regarded him as a model of rigor; he published all of his raw data, which was rare for the time.

Gould reanalyzed that data in a 1978 paper in Science and folded the work into the book. His verdict: Morton did not consciously cheat, but his results were bent by a series of unconscious finagling — choices about which subsamples to include or exclude when averaging a group (smaller-bodied populations have smaller skulls, so whom you include drags whose average down), and a failure to correct for body size and sex. Gould also noticed that when Morton switched from seed to shot, the measured capacity of Black skulls rose more than that of white skulls, and he suggested that with seed, the measurer's sense of how firmly to pack it might have been nudged by expectation. Once corrected, Gould argued, the data show no hierarchy of the kind Morton claimed.

Paul Broca in France is the second case. Broca was a genuinely great neuroscientist — the brain's speech area still bears his name — but he was also convinced that bigger brains meant greater intelligence. Gould follows his reasoning. When Germans turned out to have heavier average brains than the French, Broca pointed out that Germans were larger-bodied, and the gap vanished after correction. Yet when women's brains weighed less than men's, he held that body size explained only part of the difference and the rest reflected genuinely lower intelligence. The same correction was applied, or not, and to whatever degree, depending on whether the conclusion looked right. When one measure produced the "wrong" ranking, he went looking for another. The criteria moved; the conclusion stayed put.

There are many more figures on this road. Some scholars used the theory of recapitulation — "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny," the idea that embryos and children pass through the evolutionary stages of their ancestors — to argue that adults of "lower races" were equivalent to white children. And the Italian Cesare Lombroso held that the "born criminal" was an evolutionary throwback who could be recognized by physical "stigmata" such as long arms, a heavy jaw or a flattened nose; his theory reached courtrooms and influenced verdicts.

Why it matters: the point of these cases is not "people back then were stupid." It is an unsettling mechanism. Bias doesn't need lies; it only needs a hundred small, individually reasonable methodological choices that all lean the same way. Each choice makes sense on its own; together they decide the outcome.

How it changes the way you see things: you start watching for the "researcher degrees of freedom" in any study — which outliers were dropped, how groups were defined, which comparison was chosen — and you realize that the most dangerous distortions often come from the most sincere people, the ones most sure they have no bias at all. (The irony, as the criticism section shows, is that Gould's own reanalysis of Morton was later challenged on exactly these grounds.)

3. What Binet meant, and what America made of it: how IQ became an "inborn number"

The intelligence test was invented by the French psychologist Alfred Binet. In 1905, at the request of French education authorities, he devised a set of tasks to identify children who were falling behind in ordinary classrooms and needed special help. Gould stresses three principles Binet himself held: the score is a practical device, not a measure of some entity called intelligence; the scale is a rough empirical guide for spotting children who need help, not for ranking normal children; and a low score does not mean innate incapacity — with the right training, children can improve. Binet even designed exercises he called "mental orthopedics." He publicly attacked the view that a person's intelligence is a fixed quantity that cannot be increased, calling it a "brutal pessimism" (paraphrased).

Once the test crossed the Atlantic, its meaning was turned inside out. Gould profiles three key figures:

  • H. H. Goddard brought Binet's scale to America, coined the category "moron," argued that feeble-mindedness was governed by a single inherited factor, and tested newly arrived immigrants at Ellis Island — concluding that huge numbers of them were "morons," though they had just stepped off the boat, spoke no English and were exhausted and frightened.
  • Lewis Terman adapted the scale at Stanford into the Stanford–Binet, popularized the "intelligence quotient" (IQ), and advocated using it to sort all schoolchildren and decide who was fit for which occupations.
  • Robert Yerkes ran the mass testing of some 1.75 million US Army recruits during the First World War (the Army Alpha for those who could read, the Army Beta for those who couldn't). Gould describes the testing conditions in detail: rushed sessions, chaotic rooms, many men unable to follow the instructions, and items loaded with cultural trivia — "Crisco is a…" (a brand of cooking fat). The result was that the average "mental age" of white recruits came out at about thirteen, barely above Goddard's threshold for "moron."

The interpretation of the data was worse still. Immigrants' scores rose steadily with the number of years they had lived in the United States — the longer they had been there, the higher they scored. That is close to direct evidence that the test measured familiarity with American culture. But in A Study of American Intelligence (1923), the psychologist Carl Brigham read it the other way: the more recent waves of immigrants (from southern and eastern Europe) were simply inferior stock. Gould sets these works alongside the Immigration Act of 1924, which restricted immigration from southern and eastern Europe, and argues that the test data gave the nativist politics of the day a scientific facade. (Brigham himself publicly retracted his conclusions in 1930.)

Why it matters: in Binet's hands the same set of questions meant "find out who needs help"; in the hereditarians' hands it meant "prove who doesn't deserve help." The tool didn't change. What changed was the theory about what a score stands for.

How it changes the way you see things: the meaning of a measuring tool is set not only by how accurate it is but by what its users believe it measures. A diagnostic tool and a sorting machine can look exactly alike.

4. Factor analysis and g: reification in mathematical form

Gould regarded this as the most original part of the book: an account of how a statistical method — factor analysis — became the mathematical foundation for the idea that intelligence is one thing.

It begins with a real and robust finding. In 1904 the British psychologist Charles Spearman noticed that a person's scores on different mental tests are positively correlated with one another: people good at vocabulary tend not to be bad at arithmetic or at spatial puzzles either. This pattern of across-the-board positive correlation came to be called the "positive manifold." Using factor analysis, Spearman extracted a common component from the correlation table, which he called the general factor, g, and he took it to correspond to a real "mental energy" in the brain.

Here is what factor analysis does, by analogy. You have a cloud of data points scattered in many dimensions, and factor analysis finds a set of axes such that a few of them summarize most of the variation. The crucial point is that how you place those axes is a choice, not a fact of nature. Spearman's method (Gould explains it using modern principal components analysis) runs the first axis along the longest dimension of the cloud, so that this axis "explains" the most shared variation — and that axis is g.

In the 1930s the American psychologist L. L. Thurstone placed the axes differently. He "rotated" them to sit near clusters of related tests, seeking what he called "simple structure," in which each axis correlates strongly with only a small group of tests. In that arrangement, the single all-encompassing g disappears, replaced by about seven "primary mental abilities," such as verbal comprehension, number, spatial ability and memory.

Gould's point: the same data can be described equally well in different coordinate systems; g is a product of one way of describing them and cannot simply be taken as an entity in the brain.

From this Gould draws the book's central argument: the existence of correlations does not mean there is a single entity causing them, and an axis pulled out of mathematics cannot be treated as a thing in the head merely because it is useful. That is reification in mathematical form. He adds a historical twist about Cyril Burt, a leading champion of factor analysis and an authority for the view that intelligence is largely inherited. After Burt's death his twin data were accused of being fabricated, but Gould argued that Burt's "real error" was not the alleged fraud — it was treating g as a real thing.

How it changes the way you see things: when someone "extracts" a composite factor from a pile of data and gives it a name — "g," "national power," a "happiness index" — keep two things apart. Is this factor a handy statistical summary, or a real cause that has been independently established? The more the name sounds like a thing, the easier it is to forget that it is just one chosen axis.

5. Three ways to misread heritability: heritable ≠ unchangeable ≠ the cause of group differences

The last two boards of the determinist chute both concern the word heritability. Heritability is a statistic: in a particular population, in a particular environment, what proportion of the variation in a trait is associated with genetic variation. Gould identifies two popular and fatal misreadings.

Misreading one: high heritability means it can't be changed. Heritability describes how individual differences track genes under existing conditions. It says almost nothing about what would happen in a different environment. A standard illustration: nearsightedness has a substantial genetic component, yet a pair of glasses "fixes" it. Heritable and changeable are simply different questions.

Misreading two: high heritability within groups means the differences between groups are genetic too. This is the point Gould hits hardest, borrowing a classic thought experiment from the geneticist Richard Lewontin:

Schematic: Lewontin's two pots. Heritability within each pot is high, yet the difference between the pots' averages is entirely environmental.

Take a bag of genetically varied seeds and split it at random. Plant half in rich soil and half in poor soil. Within each pot, the differences in plant height are almost entirely due to the seeds — heritability is high. But the difference in average height between the two pots is one hundred percent due to the soil. So even if IQ is highly heritable within a group, it does not follow that the gap in average scores between groups — races, classes — is genetic. If those groups live in systematically different "soil," the gap could be entirely environmental.

How it changes the way you see things: "this trait has a genetic component" carries far less information than it sounds. It doesn't tell you whether the trait can be changed or how, and it doesn't tell you where a gap between two groups comes from. Whenever someone slides from "heritable" all the way to "this group is that way by nature and policy is powerless," at least two concepts have been swapped along the way.

The Spine of the Book

The whole book reduces to one line: biological determinism swaps in a new measuring instrument with every generation — skull capacity, brain weight, bodily stigmata, IQ scores, factor analysis — but underneath runs the same argument, and every link in it fails.

Gould's case has two layers. The first is empirical. He goes back to the raw data and the original texts and shows, case by case, how measurement was bent by expectation: Morton's sample choices, Broca's double standards, the chaos and cultural bias of the Army tests, Brigham's misreading of the years-in-America correlation. This layer asks, "Was their evidence sound?" The answer: often not, and the errors always leaned the same way.

The second layer is conceptual, and it is where the book's real weight lies. Even with perfect measurement, getting from data to "some groups are innately inferior" requires four leaps: intelligence is a single entity (reification); it can be ranked on a line (ranking); it is mainly determined by genes (hereditarianism); and therefore it can't be changed (fatalism). Gould uses factor rotation to show that the first leap is an artifact of method, Lewontin's pots to show that the third cannot be carried from within groups to between them, and Binet's original intent along with examples like nearsightedness to show that the fourth confuses "heritable" with "unchangeable."

Running through both layers is a judgment about science itself: science is done by people and embedded in society; it can correct itself, but correction doesn't happen automatically — someone has to go back to the raw data. In the 1996 revision Gould added a critique of The Bell Curve (Herrnstein and Murray, 1994, which argued that IQ is largely heritable and explains much of America's social stratification), arguing that it rests on exactly those four leaps and that its own data show IQ accounts for only a modest share of the variation in individuals' social and economic outcomes — the old argument, in Gould's view, back in new clothes. The book lands on a moral judgment: few injustices run deeper than denying a person the chance to strive, or even to hope, through a limit imposed from outside but falsely identified as lying within. (Close to the wording of the book's introduction.)

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: "Gould proved IQ tests are meaningless." He didn't, and he didn't claim to. He accepted that tests can usefully predict some kinds of school performance, and he endorsed using them in Binet's way, to find children who need help. What he opposed was treating the score as an innate, fixed "entity" that can rank groups.

Misreading two: "Gould thought intelligence has nothing to do with biology." Also wrong. He didn't deny that the brain is biological or that individuals differ genetically. He denied the whole package: a single intelligence, plus linear ranking, plus a genetic explanation of group differences.

Misreading three: "The book says science is just bias." Quite the opposite. Gould was a committed believer in science, and the book itself uses scientific method — going back to the data, redoing the arithmetic — to correct science's mistakes.

The strongest criticisms deserve to be laid out honestly:

  • The Morton reversal. In 2011 the anthropologist Jason Lewis and colleagues published a study in PLoS Biology that remeasured about half of Morton's surviving skulls. They found Morton's measurements largely accurate and argued that Gould's own reanalysis contained errors and selective choices — in other words, the man accused of bias may have been more accurate, and the accuser may have committed the very sin he described. The philosopher Michael Weisberg, and later Jonathan Kaplan, Massimo Pigliucci and colleagues, responded that Gould did make mistakes, but that Morton's data still cannot support his racial ranking and that the framing of the question was itself flawed. The episode remains the classic irony in discussions of bias in science.
  • Factor analysis, misdescribed. Psychometricians broadly criticize Gould's account of factor analysis as oversimplified. Thurstone's "primary abilities" are themselves correlated; factor-analyze them again (a "second-order factor") and g reappears — something Thurstone himself later accepted. Today's mainstream models of intelligence (such as the "three-stratum" model John Carroll synthesized in 1993) place g at the top with many specific abilities beneath it. g is fairly stable across different test batteries and populations, and it predicts school and job performance. The psychologist Arthur Jensen's review called the book a "debunking of scientific fossils and straw persons." Gould's rejoinder was that predictive validity does not make g a single biological entity, still less make group gaps genetic — and on that point his argument still holds up.
  • Historical causation, overstated. In 1983 the psychologists Mark Snyderman and Richard Herrnstein examined the congressional record and concluded that intelligence testing was not a decisive factor in the debate over the 1924 Immigration Act; nativist politics had other sources. Gould's tight link between testing and legislation is a frequently cited weak spot.
  • Presentism and straw men. Others fault Gould for picking history's worst cases and implying that modern psychometrics is the same kind of thing, while saying too little about how the field had advanced by the late twentieth century.

Seen from today, later research has both supported and corrected Gould. On the one hand, IQ is substantially heritable within populations (twin studies typically estimate around half or more for adults). On the other, average IQ scores in many countries rose by roughly three points per decade over the twentieth century — the "Flynn effect," systematically documented by the political scientist James Flynn. Genes cannot change that fast, which shows that environments can move scores a great deal and makes the inference from "group gap" to "innate gap" harder still. Gould got a number of specific cases and statistical details wrong, but his conceptual critique — heritable ≠ unchangeable ≠ explanation of group differences — remains a warning no one in the field can get around.

Crossovers

  • Quantum mechanics · choice of basis: The same quantum state can be expanded in different bases — different sets of coordinate directions — with components that look nothing alike, while the state itself is unchanged; just as one correlation table can be written as one g or seven abilities. The disanalogy: in quantum mechanics, the basis corresponds to which measurement you actually make and has physical consequences; factor rotation is only a choice of description.
  • Buddhism · Madhyamaka's "mere designation": Nāgārjuna's Middle Way school holds that "chariot" is just a name imputed to an assembly of wheels, axle and pole; taking it as a thing with its own independent nature is grasping. That is Gould's reification exactly — mistaking a useful name for an object. The difference: Madhyamaka says this of everything; Gould says it of one concept.
  • Economics · Goodhart's law: The economist Charles Goodhart observed that a measure turned into a policy target stops being a good measure. IQ's history is the social version: once scores were used to stream pupils and screen immigrants, lives got organized around the score, and the score drifted away from what it was meant to reflect.
  • Machine learning · algorithmic bias: Hiring and credit models learn from historical data; if that history carries discrimination, the model learns it as a neutral-looking score — practically a digital replay of Morton and Brigham, where no one lies and bias is reproduced "objectively" through data. Unlike nineteenth-century skulls, though, a model's skew can be systematically audited and measured.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. Biological determinism says the social hierarchy mirrors a biological one, and it comes back each generation with a new measuring tool.

2. Its two underlying moves are reification (treating an abstraction as a thing) and ranking (squeezing many dimensions onto one ruler).

3. Bias gets into science less through lies than through a hundred small, reasonable choices that all lean the same way.

4. Broca's lesson: whether a correction gets applied depends on whether the conclusion looks right — the criteria move, the conclusion doesn't.

5. Binet built his test to find out who needed help; in America it became proof of who didn't deserve it.

6. Immigrants' scores rose with their years in the country — a measure of cultural familiarity, read as innate worth.

7. Correlation does not imply a single entity causing it; where factor analysis puts its axes is a choice, not a discovery.

8. Heritable is not unchangeable: nearsightedness runs in families, and glasses still fix it.

9. Lewontin's pots: however heritable a trait is within groups, that tells you nothing about whether the gap between them is genetic.

10. Few injustices run deeper than this: denying people their chance through a limit imposed from outside but falsely identified as lying within. (Paraphrased)
