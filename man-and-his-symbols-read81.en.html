Man and His Symbols

Carl Gustav Jung and four collaborators · 1964

中文 →

In One Sentence

Modern people threw out myth, ritual and symbol one piece at a time, and took the loss for a sign of maturity. Jung's answer: you didn't throw them out, you only pushed them somewhere you can't see — and they go on making your decisions, now without supervision. Dreams are the letters they write you every night; learning to read them isn't a program for becoming a better person, but for becoming the person you already are.

Coordinates

Carl Gustav Jung (1875–1961), Swiss psychiatrist and founder of analytical psychology, was once Freud's chosen heir; after their break around 1913 the split came down to this: Freud read the unconscious as a private dump of repressed material, Jung read it as a structured, shared, even creative floor beneath all of us. This is the one book he ever wrote for general readers. A publisher approached him after his 1959 BBC interview; the eighty-something Jung refused, and — as the book's editor John Freeman tells it — changed his mind because of a dream, in which he was addressing not a small circle of physicians but a large crowd of ordinary people, and the crowd understood him. The book has five chapters. Only the first, "Approaching the Unconscious," is Jung's own (written in English, finished shortly before his final illness in 1961); the other four were written by students he chose and vetted page by page: Marie-Louise von Franz (individuation), Joseph Henderson (myth and initiation), Aniela Jaffé (symbolism in the visual arts), Jolande Jacobi (a single analysis). So keep one fact in view: roughly a fifth of this book is Jung's own prose, but the frame is entirely his — it is the one time he set out his life's thinking plainly, in his own hand.

Central Claims

Three claims, circled again and again:

  • The unconscious is not a personal wastebasket; it is structured, and its forms are universal. Mind, like body, has a shared anatomy — and its organs are what Jung calls archetypes.
  • The unconscious does not speak in concepts; it speaks in symbols, and a symbol is not a sign. A sign points at something already known; a symbol points at something not yet fully stated, perhaps never statable. Hence no dream dictionary can exist — only the dreamer, working his way closer.
  • The psyche regulates itself: dreams compensate for whatever the conscious attitude has overdone, and the direction of a life is not "better" but "whole." And most modern neurosis, in Jung's reading, begins where a person severed his own umbilical cord to the symbolic.

The Core Concepts, One by One

Symbol vs. sign: why dreams have no dictionary

The distinction is drawn on the book's first page, and it is the threshold to everything after. A sign stands for something we already know: a red light means stop, H₂O means water. You can translate it across one-to-one, and once translated it is used up — no remainder. A symbol is different: a word or image that may be entirely familiar, but which carries, on top of its obvious meaning, something inexhaustible and not quite thinkable. The cross, the wheel, the serpent, water, the tree — humanity has painted and dreamt them for millennia, and no single reading has ever used them up.

Jung's definition: a thing is a symbol when what it implies exceeds its obvious and immediate meaning (paraphrased). A symbol is always larger than any interpretation of it — not because we aren't clever enough yet, but because what it points at lies outside consciousness's range in the first place.

This single distinction sets the method of the whole book: dreams are symbols, not signs, which is why Jung rejected lookup-table interpretation ("snake = sex") from first page to last.

The consequence is very practical. The same serpent means different things in the dream of a countryman raised to fear snakes, of someone who has just finished a history of medicine, and of someone sitting on a relationship he won't admit to. Jung asked the interpreter to come to each new case as though he had never heard a theory in his life (paraphrased) — which is also where he and Freud part company in practice: Freud had a fairly fixed set of translation rules, Jung had one discipline only, stay next to the image.

How it changes the way you see: you grow suspicious of anything explained away in a single sentence. When someone tells you "you're like this because of something in your childhood," that is a symbol being used as a sign. The things with real power over you — an obsession, a recurring dream, an inexplicable aversion — are precisely the ones that still feel unexplained after the explanation.

The collective unconscious: not ancestral memories, but the shapes the ancestors left

Jung's most famous idea, and the most consistently misquoted. First, what it is not: he is not saying you inherit particular memories or particular myths — he denied that repeatedly. He is saying the psyche has a floor deeper than the personal, and that this floor consists not of content but of dispositions and forms. Over hundreds of thousands of years humans met the same situations over and over (birth, mother, father, danger, the opposite sex, the chief, death, darkness); what settled out of those encounters is not photographs but ready-made patterns of reaction and of imagination.

Hence two layers. The personal unconscious: what you forgot, repressed, never noticed — all of it yours, from this one life. Living there are complexes, which Jung identified early through the word-association test (read out a list of words, time the replies; certain words make people stall, blush, answer beside the point) — knots of strongly charged material that possess autonomy: touched, a complex takes over the room and reasoning stops working. Hence his line: you do not have complexes, complexes have you (paraphrased). The collective unconscious: shared by everyone, neither added to nor subtracted from by your biography; archetypes live there. His evidence is of two kinds — hallucinations of psychotic patients and dreams of people who had never read the myths, containing ancient images absent from their own culture; plus the same motifs recurring in myths, religions and alchemy thousands of miles apart. (That evidential chain needs heavy discounting; see the criticism section.)

Archetype: the axial system, not the crystal

The word most in need of clarity and most often mangled. Jung's own analogy is a fine one: an archetype is to a particular image what the axial system of a crystal is to the crystal. The axial system is a set of geometric rules; it has no material existence of its own, you cannot hold it — yet it determines in advance what shape will grow out of the solution, while every crystal that actually forms is different. In the same way, an archetype is a form, a tendency; every "wise old man" figure you meet is one crystallization of it, each carrying the fingerprints of your culture and your life. Taking the archetype for a particular picture is the number-one accident in reading Jung.

The harder analogy is instinct. A weaver bird builds its species' nest without instruction; the yucca moth completes a pollination sequence far too intricate to be learned by trial and error. Nobody calls that memory; everyone accepts it as an inherited behavioral template. Jung's question: if bodily behavior has such templates, why should imagination not? The archetype is instinct's other face, the one turned toward the psyche: instinct sets how you will act, the archetype sets how you will imagine.

The ones that recur through the book: the shadow (the self you refuse to be), anima / animus (the inner contrasexual figure), the persona (the face turned outward), the mother (nourishing and devouring at once), the hero (departure — ordeal — return), the wise old man (the giver of meaning), the trickster (the figure who wrecks the order and brings back something new, from Coyote in Native American myth to the European fool), and above all the Self. Note that archetypes carry no moral charge: the mother is earth and witch alike, the hero liberator and fanatic alike.

How it changes the way you see: you start noticing why certain stories work everywhere. Star Wars, founder biographies, conversion narratives run on the same skeleton; advertising that hangs a product on the Good Mother, the Hero or the Sage is borrowing the same leverage. Seeing it doesn't stop you being moved — but you learn which string was plucked when you were.

The shadow: the person you can't stand mostly lives inside you

The shadow is the part you won't admit is also you: laziness, envy, cruelty, greed, weakness, vanity — whatever doesn't fit the self-image you maintain gets swept here. It is the nearest door into the unconscious, and the first gate to pass.

Its mechanism is projection: what gets pushed out of consciousness doesn't vanish; it goes and stands on someone else so you can see it. The diagnostic is unusually usable. When your dislike of someone's trait is out of proportion — not "he's a bit much," but one mention and you're hot, you have to say something, you're still chewing on it that night — it is usually not about him. The man who scrimps hardest hates extravagance; the man sitting on his ambition hates "operators."

Two things get missed. First, the shadow holds gold as well as dirt: a child trained to be good sends aggression, willfulness and appetite down there, and those are exactly the vitality he'll be short of at forty — so integrating the shadow often means picking strength back up, not "fixing a flaw." Second, the shadow goes collective: when a group projects its shadow onto another group, the other becomes pure evil, and massacre acquires a moral glow. Jung's warning, written between the wars, is blunt: the dangerous thing is not the enemy but the piece of ourselves we are certain we do not have.

How it changes the way you see: "whom I despise" becomes intelligence about yourself. This is the most usable tool Jung offers an ordinary reader — no couch required, just one extra question the next time strong disgust rises: where am I keeping my own portion of the thing I can't stand in him?

Anima and animus: the inner opposite

Jung observed a feminine component in a man's psyche (the anima) and a masculine one in a woman's (the animus); judged by consciousness as "not what someone of my sex should contain," they are pushed under and so appear by projection — love at first sight, inexplicable infatuation, the conviction that this particular person is fated, are mostly you seeing your own inner figure on someone else's face, with little reference to the person actually standing there. Von Franz adds four stages of the anima — biological attraction (Eve), the romantic and aesthetic (Helen), the spiritualized (the Virgin Mary), and wisdom itself (Sophia) — the claim being that this force can develop from sexual drive all the way into a capacity for meaning.

This is the book's most imaginative stretch and the one that survives present-day scrutiny worst (details below). What still holds is the core: everyone carries a set of traits their own gender role has ruled out of bounds; held in the dark, those traits come back as love, obsession and unaccountable moods. Strip the gender labels and the observation stays sharp.

The persona: wear it long enough and it won't come off

Persona originally meant the mask worn by actors in Greek and Roman theatre, and Jung borrows it for the face you present: professional identity, social role, the whole repertoire of doing things properly. The mask is necessary — social life is impossible without one. There is exactly one danger: you take the mask for yourself. The more flawlessly someone lives as "the good doctor," "the good mother," "the one who can carry it," the more is left outside the door, gathering force in the shadow. It is why people so often collapse after retirement or handing over a post: not because the job is gone, but because when the mask comes off there turns out to be nothing grown underneath.

Ego and Self: you are not the center of the circle

This is the hinge of Jung's system. The ego is the center of consciousness — the thing that says "I," the self you know about. The Self (nothing whatever to do with Freud's id) is the center and the totality of the whole psyche, conscious and unconscious together. The ego is one small territory on the Self's map, as Earth is to the solar system. We habitually assume "I" is the whole thing; Jung says that is a mistake about scale.

Schematic, not anatomy: the ego centers consciousness only, while the Self centers the whole psyche — and is also the whole circle.

The Self cannot be looked at directly (whatever is looked at becomes a content of consciousness); it appears only through symbols. Chief among them: the mandala — Sanskrit for "circle," those figures with a center, a fourfold division and layered symmetry, from Tibetan Buddhist cosmograms to the rose windows of Gothic cathedrals to the circles patients draw unprompted; and the cosmic man — Purusha in India, Adam Kadmon in Jewish mysticism, Pangu in China, the myths in which the whole world is one person's body. Von Franz reports a moving instance: the Naskapi hunters of Labrador hold that each person carries a "Great Man" (mista'peo) inside, who needs neither priests nor doctrine and speaks only in dreams; the more seriously you take your dreams and follow them, the more often he comes and the more he helps.

How it changes the way you see: your grip on "self-management" loosens a little. The turns you didn't plan, even the ones that wreck your plan — going cold on work you spent years building, being dragged off by an interest that makes no sense — are not necessarily loss of control. They may be the larger center correcting course.

Individuation: not becoming a better person, but the person you are

This is the title of von Franz's chapter and the destination of the book. Individuation is the process by which a person gradually becomes the unique whole he potentially is — claiming the shadow back, withdrawing projections from other people, demoting the persona from a face to a garment, and finally opening a line between ego and Self that traffic can pass along.

Von Franz's image is a pine tree: it grows by its own inner plan into a complete and singular pine, not a perfect one (it may be bent by wind, thin on one side). Here is the distinction most worth keeping from the whole book: individuation aims at wholeness, not perfection. Perfection works by deletion — cut off the unpresentable parts; wholeness works by annexation — bring the cut-off parts back. So an individuating person usually comes out rougher than before, not smoother.

Nor is it a straight line. Von Franz describes the movement as a spiral: you return to the same old problem again and again, apparently going nowhere, though each turn stands at a different height. And it typically begins in earnest in the second half of life — the first half's work is to build an ego and get a footing in society (make the mask well), the second half's is to turn around and ask whether any of it was yours. Jung's well-known clinical observation: many of his middle-aged patients had nothing clinically wrong with them at all — their trouble was that life had lost its meaning (paraphrased).

How it changes the way you see: the default script for "growth" gets swapped out. The mainstream script is addition and optimization — stronger, more disciplined, more efficient. Jung's script is annexation and correction: what you have to recover is not more, but the parts you threw away yourself.

Dreams and compensation: the unconscious rebalancing you every night

Jung's first principle of dream interpretation is compensation: a dream's job is to pull back whichever side the conscious attitude has overextended. The psyche, like the body, is self-stabilizing — the harder you hold yourself in one posture by day, the more reliably you dream its opposite. A man who idealizes his boss past all reason dreams the boss ridiculous; a mild soul certain he has no aggression dreams himself hitting someone; a man who has inflated dreams himself shrunk. A dream is not whispering; it is calibrating.

Jung recounts one case that unsettled him. A keen mountaineer dreamt repeatedly of stepping into empty space and falling. Jung read it as an unmistakable warning, urged restraint, even spoke to the man's friend; the man shrugged it off, and some months later fell to his death on a climb, taking a companion with him. Jung's reading carries no mysticism: the man had a drive to climb, to go toward danger, that his consciousness would not acknowledge — the unconscious saw it perfectly well and had to shout in pictures. A dream is not a prophecy; it is a risk report you refuse to read.

Methodologically, the parting of ways with Freud is called amplification. Freud's free association starts from an element of the dream and follows the chain outward; Jung's objection is practical: go that way and you arrive at the person's complexes no matter where you started, so the dream itself was wasted (paraphrased). He asks for the reverse — circle the image, returning to it again and again: first what it means to this dreamer, then set beside it the comparable images from myth and folklore, the way you would establish a blurred word by collecting its use across dozens of contexts, until this particular occurrence gives up what it is saying.

One more part is needed here: psychological types. Jung divided basic attitude into introversion and extraversion (both words are his coinages), and distinguished four functions: thinking (what is it), feeling (what is it worth to me), sensation (that it is there), intuition (where it came from and where it is going). Whichever function you use most easily, you necessarily have a least-developed inferior function that has sunk into the unconscious and turned primitive and emotional. So dreams tend to knock from exactly your weakest direction: a man who has made his living by thinking all his life will be tripped up by a matter of feeling, and will handle it with a clumsiness that seems unlike him.

Modern symbolic poverty: the gods didn't leave, they changed names

This is what the "man" of the title really refers to, and Jung's most prophetic passage. Over several centuries the modern world disenchanted nature step by step — no god in the river, no spirit in the tree, thunder is discharge, dreams are the brain discharging — and we are proud of it, taking ourselves to be finally free of superstition. The cost: a person is cut off from the whole symbolic apparatus in which he once housed his fear, his desire and his sense of meaning — and that energy doesn't vanish, it withdraws into the unconscious and comes out elsewhere. Jung's line runs roughly: his gods and demons have not disappeared at all, they have merely acquired new names — and they now keep him restless, vaguely apprehensive, and endlessly in need of pills, alcohol and various -isms.

Which gives him a particular reading of twentieth-century political fanaticism: abandoned religious energy does not evaporate, it goes looking for a new vessel — a leader, a nation, a race, a slogan will all serve, and because none of them is classified as religion, no ritual or doctrine is there to set limits, so it burns hotter and with fewer edges. Swap "-ism" for algorithmically served identity or some apocalyptic narrative and the structure of the passage is unchanged.

How it changes the way you see: rationalization starts to look like a trade with a price rather than a free gain. A society that cuts away ritual, myth and shared symbolic life gains clarity and pays for it — those functions come back online in cruder, less governable form.

The Distilled Skeleton

Five chapters, running one line from "how the unconscious speaks" to "how you should live":

  • 1. Approaching the Unconscious (Jung): the rules — symbol is not sign; dreams are self-regulation; the unconscious has a personal and a collective layer; archetypes are form, not content; modern illness begins with the loss of symbols. The foundation.
  • 2. Ancient Myths and Modern Man (Henderson): the foundation laid out anthropologically — the fixed cycle of hero myth (birth, ordeal, struggle with the shadow, sacrifice, return) and how initiation moved a person into the next stage by a symbolic death and rebirth. Modern society abolished these rites, so the young improvise substitutes.
  • 3. The Process of Individuation (von Franz): the same material inside one person — shadow, anima/animus and Self entering in turn. The longest and most important chapter.
  • 4. Symbolism in the Visual Arts (Jaffé): the same archetypes tracked through art history; the abstraction and fragmentation of modern art read as the case notes of symbolic disorder.
  • 5. Symbols in an Individual Analysis (Jacobi): one real case with its full dream series, so the reader can watch the first four chapters happen to a particular person.

What does it actually prove? Strictly, nothing — Jung's method is phenomenological: collect a great mass of dreams, visions, myths and artworks, point to the structures that recur, and say "these cross-cultural convergences need an explanation." The collective unconscious is the explanation he offers. Its strength is coverage, not experiment; so is its weakness.

Misreadings & Honest Criticism

  • "Jung wrote this book" — a fifth of it. Four of five chapters are by students, and the rigor is uneven; when readers today cite "Jung's four stages of the anima," that is von Franz's scheme.
  • An archetype is not an image, still less a character template. Screenwriting manuals and the Campbellian hero's journey turned archetypes into a cast list (mentor, threshold guardian, shadow villain). Useful — but that is mistaking the axial system for the crystal: the archetype is form without content, and the content is always supplied by a culture and a person.
  • The shadow is not "the evil half." It is everything consciousness has excluded, including large amounts of undeveloped capacity; reading integration as "admitting I'm bad" is both inaccurate and useless.
  • The hardest blow: falsifiability. Amplification can connect any image to some myth — there are enough myths in the world that a match can always be found. Which means the theory has almost no way of being wrong, and by Popper's criterion a theory that can't be wrong isn't a scientific one. Jung called himself an empiricist, but his "empiricism" is interpretive, not repeatable.
  • The showcase evidence has a problem: the "Solar Phallus Man." Jung repeatedly cited a schizophrenic patient who described a tube hanging from the sun whose swinging made the wind — an image closely matching a Mithraic liturgical text the patient could not have read, therefore (Jung argued) it came from the collective unconscious. The historian Richard Noll showed that an accessible version of the material existed before the patient's hospitalization, and that Jung's account of dates and details shifted over time. This does not refute the collective unconscious, but it does turn the most-cited piece of evidence for it into one that can no longer be used.
  • The gender essentialism of anima/animus has aged badly. "Masculine = logos, feminine = eros" carries the body heat of the 1950s, and passages describing "the animus-possessed woman laying down the law" now read as stereotype dressed in psychology. Post-Jungians (Hillman's line among them) have largely de-gendered the idea, keeping only the core: the opposite that the ego excludes comes back as infatuation and mood.
  • The inherited mechanism never arrived. If archetypes really are innate psychic forms, what corresponds to them in genes and brains? A century on there is no identifiable mechanism, and some of Jung's formulations carry a Lamarckian scent of acquired characteristics, which modern genetics does not permit. The charitable reading treats archetypes as a convergence produced by common brain architecture plus common human situations — but that is already somebody else's rewrite. Relatedly: MBTI is not Jung. Built by Myers and Briggs on his typology, it chops continuous tendencies into sixteen boxes, and its test–retest reliability has long been criticized; Jung himself objected to using types as labels.
  • Jung the man needs stating honestly. From 1933 he presided over the General Medical Society for Psychotherapy and edited its journal, and in that period published writings distinguishing "Germanic" from "Jewish" psychology — a lasting and serious charge against him. He later conceded that he had "slipped" politically (paraphrased); the argument has never been settled. Reading his theory does not require defending this.
  • And the balancing entry: the influence is real. Introversion and extraversion, complex, archetype, shadow, persona — these words long ago entered ordinary language, and imagery work, art therapy and the entire hero's-journey narrative industry start here. As a testable science it does not stand up; as a language for understanding symbol and meaning, nothing has replaced it.

The Jung Outside This Book

This is the popular version, and it deliberately leaves out Jung's most difficult and strangest work. Without that, you end up with a picture of a warm old man who talked about dreams and myths — which is a distortion.

Alchemy (Psychology and Alchemy, 1944, and after) — the labor of his later decades, and the piece most easily taken for a man gone off the rails. The argument is in fact cool-headed: medieval alchemists watched their vessels for decades, and no gold ever came out of the matter — but their laboratory notebooks are full of visions and symbols (blackening, whitening, reddening; the marriage of king and queen; the serpent eating its tail). They were projecting unconscious contents onto matter, which makes the alchemical literature an illustrated record of unconscious process; and the goal of turning base matter into gold is, structurally, individuation.

Synchronicity — proposed late in life after his exchanges with the physicist Wolfgang Pauli: certain coincidences have no causal connection whatever and yet fit exactly in meaning (his stock example: an over-rational patient is recounting a dream of a golden scarab when a near-identical beetle taps at the consulting-room window). He called it an acausal connecting principle. It is his most contested and least defensible construction — most scientists read it as over-interpretation of coincidence — but it is genuinely the capstone of his system.

Answer to Job (1952) — his fiercest small book. Rereading the story of a God who lets Satan torment a righteous man, he reaches a near-blasphemous conclusion: Job stands morally above the God who answers him with a display of power, and the divine image itself requires human consciousness in order to become whole. His subject was never whether God exists, but what this psychic fact called God has done to human beings.

The Red Book and active imagination — after the break with Freud in 1913, Jung passed through a period he called his confrontation with the unconscious: he deliberately let visions come, spoke with the figures in them, painted them, and copied the whole thing out in Gothic script into a large illuminated volume (The Red Book, published only in 2009). He later formalized the practice as active imagination: not waiting for a dream, but entering an image while awake, letting it move on its own, and holding a conversation with it from an ego that does not get swamped. Nearly all his theory is the after-the-fact ordering of that private episode — it did not come out of a laboratory, it was carried back from one man's nearly ungovernable interior.

Ten Sentences

1. A symbol is not a sign: a sign points at something known and leaves no remainder, while what a symbol implies always exceeds its obvious meaning — which is why dreams have no dictionary, only this person and this situation.

2. The unconscious has two layers: the personal one holds what you pushed down (complexes), the collective one is the floor all humans share (archetypes) — and you do not have complexes, complexes have you.

3. Archetypes are form, not content: an archetype is to an image what a crystal's axial system is to the crystal — the system has no shape of its own, yet decides what shapes can grow, and no two actual crystals come out alike.

4. The shadow is the part of you that you refuse to own; it doesn't disappear, it stands on other people so you can see it — disproportionate dislike of someone is intelligence about yourself — and it holds not only dirt but the vitality you were trained to discard.

5. When a group projects its shadow onto another group, the other becomes pure evil and massacre acquires a moral glow: the danger is never the enemy, it is the piece of ourselves we're certain we don't have.

6. The mask is necessary; taking it for your face is the accident. The more flawlessly you play the role, the more is left outside the door — people rarely collapse from losing a position, they collapse when the mask comes off and nothing has grown underneath.

7. The ego centers consciousness only; the Self centers the whole psyche. Assuming "I" is the whole thing is an error about scale — and the mandala and the cosmic man are both saying so.

8. Dreams work by compensation: whatever posture you hold by day, you dream its opposite by night. A dream is not a prophecy; it is a risk report you refuse to read.

9. Individuation aims at wholeness, not perfection — perfection works by deletion, wholeness by annexation; and it moves in a spiral, returning to the same old problem at a different height each time.

10. Disenchantment did not rid modern people of their gods and demons, it only changed their names: into anxiety, addiction, fanatical -isms and an emptiness nobody can name. Jung's last word is that attention returned to dreams and symbols is what lets the "Great Man" start speaking again.
