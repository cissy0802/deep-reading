The Anxious Generation

The Anxious Generation: How the Great Rewiring of Childhood Is Causing an Epidemic of Mental Illness · Jonathan Haidt · 2024

中文 →

In One Sentence

In the early 2010s, adolescents across the developed world became, within a few short years, markedly more anxious, more depressed and more likely to harm themselves. Haidt's explanation: childhood was rewired. The play-based childhood — outdoors, unsupervised, a little risky — was replaced by a phone-based childhood built around smartphones and social media; we overprotected kids in the real world and left them almost unprotected online. His remedy is four norms that only work if communities adopt them together.

Where It Sits

Haidt is a social psychologist at NYU's Stern School of Business, best known for The Righteous Mind, on moral psychology, and The Coddling of the American Mind, written with Greg Lukianoff. This book is the sequel to Coddling's argument that overprotection makes kids fragile, with the target moved from campus culture to phones and platform design. It became a long-running bestseller in 2024 and is the most fought-over popular psychology book in years.

The Core Claims

  • Between 2010 and 2015 came the "Great Rewiring." Smartphones spread, front-facing cameras arrived, and image-and-likes platforms such as Instagram became the main stage of teenage social life. Almost at the same moment, rates of anxiety, depression and self-harm among adolescents in the US, the UK, Canada, Australia and the Nordic countries turned upward together, girls most sharply. That coincidence of timing is where the whole argument begins.
  • The cause has two halves, and both matter. One is the decline of the play-based childhood, under way since the 1980s and 90s, as parents frightened of strangers and injuries stopped letting kids roam and play on their own. The other is the sudden arrival of the phone-based childhood after 2010. The first emptied out kids' chances to grow up in the real world; the second soaked up all the time that was left.
  • This is a collective action problem that individuals can't solve alone. No parent wants their child to be the only one in class without a phone. So Haidt proposes four social norms — no smartphones before high school, no social media before 16, phone-free schools, and far more real-world independence and free play — and calls on parents, schools and lawmakers to push them together.

The Key Concepts, One by One

1. The Great Rewiring: why the inflection point is around 2012

The first part of the book is mostly graphs. In the US National Survey on Drug Use and Health, the share of adolescents who had a major depressive episode in the past year more than doubled over the 2010s (paraphrased); emergency-room visits for self-harm among girls aged 10 to 14 nearly tripled in the same period; the share of college students reporting anxiety climbed steadily. These lines were roughly flat, or even falling, before 2010, and they bend upward together around 2012. Data from the UK, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and the Nordic countries show a similar shape.

Why those years? Haidt lines up the events. The iPhone launched in 2007, but what turned the phone into a portable social stage was 2010: the iPhone 4 added a front-facing camera, and Instagram launched; Facebook bought Instagram in 2012. "Like" and "share" buttons spread around 2009, attaching a public social score to every post. Fast mobile internet turned going online from "sitting down at a computer" into "always on." In a 2022 Pew survey, about 46 percent of American teens said they were online "almost constantly." His key move: teenagers had phones before 2010, but they were flip phones for calls and texts. After 2010 the phone became a social-comparison machine with an infinite feed and a running score.

One detail is easy to miss: Haidt is talking about a generation, not about individuals. People born after 1995, usually called Gen Z, were the first to carry this machine in their pockets through puberty — hence "the anxious generation." Millennials met the smartphone in college or at work, after the brain's most sensitive window had already closed.

This changes how you read the youth mental-health crisis: it is not a cohort of kids suddenly going soft, nor one country's peculiar problem, but a change in dose that showed up at the same time in many countries. No generation in human history has spent six or seven hours a day of its development facing a screen.

2. Overprotected in the real world, underprotected online

Haidt's most quoted line runs roughly: we overprotected children in the real world and underprotected them in the virtual world.

The first half started earlier. From the 1980s, American television ran story after story about missing children, their faces printed on milk cartons, and parental fear of "stranger danger" soared — even though abduction by strangers is extremely rare and street crime kept falling after the 1990s. The result was a culture Haidt calls "safetyism": treating safety as a value that trumps all others, to the point where even emotional discomfort counts as harm. Seven- and eight-year-olds stopped being allowed to walk to school, run to the corner shop or wander the neighborhood; "play" became adult-organized, adult-watched lessons and activities.

Why does that hurt? Here Haidt borrows Nassim Taleb's idea of the antifragile: some systems don't merely withstand stress, they need it to get stronger — the way muscle grows back thicker after strain, or the immune system calibrates itself by meeting germs. Children, Haidt argues, are systems like this. A fall, a fight, getting lost, being rejected — each is a vaccine against fear and conflict. The psychologist Peter Gray's research shows that unsupervised free play, especially slightly risky play (climbing high, rough-and-tumble, roaming far), is where kids practice judging risk, managing their emotions and negotiating rules with peers. Take it away and children are not safer; they just never grow the skill of handling danger.

The second half runs the other way. The same kids who can't go to the corner alone can, in their bedrooms, late at night, with no adult present, meet strange adults, pornography, self-harm communities and an endless stream of social comparison. Platform age limits (US law effectively makes 13 the default) are barely checked; a fake birthday gets you in.

An analogy: it's like a parent who fits every table corner with bumpers and every socket with a cover, then drops the child alone in a city that never closes and has no police. The protection points exactly the wrong way: a death grip where we should let go, an open door where we should stand guard.

This changes how you think about protecting children: the real question isn't "more protection or less?" but where the protection goes.

3. Experience-expectant development, and discover mode vs. defend mode

Why do these years matter so much? Haidt borrows a term from developmental neuroscience, experience-expectant development: during certain windows the brain "expects" particular kinds of experience and wires itself around them. The classic case is language — small children pick up a native accent almost effortlessly within a certain age range; miss that window and you rarely sound fully native. Haidt argues that adolescence is a sensitive period for social learning: the brain prunes and strengthens connections heavily in these years to fit the social world it finds itself in. If that world is a screen, it wires for the screen.

He adds a handy contrast: discover mode versus defend mode. These map onto what psychologists call the behavioral activation system (approach rewards, explore new things) and the behavioral inhibition system (detect threats, pull back). In a safe, supportive setting people shift into discover mode — curious, willing to try, able to learn from failure. Under threat they shift into defend mode — vigilant, avoidant, reading ambiguous signals as danger. A healthy childhood keeps kids in discover mode most of the time, learning through countless small adventures that fear passes. Overprotection, plus the judgment, comparison and bad news that saturate the online world, keeps kids in defend mode for years. Chronic defend mode looks, clinically, a lot like an anxiety disorder.

He stresses one detail: the standard treatment for anxiety disorders, exposure therapy, works by having people approach what they fear, bit by bit, until they find out they're fine. Free play and small risks are natural exposure therapy. We cancelled that training, filled kids' time with a machine that pumps out threat signals, and then wondered why they were anxious.

This changes how you see anxiety: not just a chemical imbalance, but a default posture the environment has trained in — and if the environment doesn't change, pills and talk alone will struggle to pull someone out of defend mode.

4. The four foundational harms: opportunity cost is the big one

How exactly does the phone-based childhood do damage? Haidt lists four "foundational harms" that apply to boys and girls alike:

  • Social deprivation. The American Time Use Survey shows that the time teens spent with friends in person shrank dramatically in the 2010s, falling by nearly half before the pandemic (paraphrased). Online chat isn't a substitute: face-to-face contact comes with eye contact, tone of voice, bodily synchrony and instant feedback, and that is exactly what teaches kids to read other people.
  • Sleep deprivation. Once the phone moves into the bedroom, late-night scrolling eats into sleep. Adolescents need more sleep, not less, and chronic shortfalls drag down mood, attention and learning.
  • Attention fragmentation. Notifications go off a hundred times a day, each one yanking attention away from the task at hand. Adolescence is a key period for the prefrontal cortex (the brain region for planning, focus and self-control), and a brain that is constantly interrupted struggles to build the capacity for sustained attention.
  • Addiction. Platform design borrows from the slot machine. The psychologist B. F. Skinner found that when rewards come unpredictably — a variable-ratio reinforcement schedule — animals press the lever hardest and are slowest to quit. Pull-to-refresh, like counts and infinite scroll are levers built for humans.

Behind all four sits a more basic idea from economics: opportunity cost — the price of doing one thing is everything else you could have done with that time. Even if what's on the screen were entirely harmless, Haidt notes, American teens spend roughly seven to eight hours a day on screen entertainment according to Common Sense Media's surveys, and those hours come out of sleep, exercise, in-person play, reading and daydreaming. The phone's biggest harm may lie not in what it gives kids but in what it takes away.

This changes how you judge any technology: it's not enough to ask "is the content good?" You also have to ask "what is it replacing?" Something can be "fine" minute by minute and still be ruinously expensive if it swallows every other possibility in the day.

5. Why girls are hit harder, and why boys "disappear"

The starkest feature of the data is the sex difference: girls' depression, anxiety and self-harm rose earliest and fastest. Haidt offers four reasons:

  • Visual social comparison and perfectionism. Platforms like Instagram revolve around curated, edited images, and girls tend to be more sensitive to comparisons of looks and social standing. In 2021 the whistleblower Frances Haugen leaked Facebook's internal research to The Wall Street Journal; one slide read, in substance: we make body image issues worse for one in three teen girls.
  • Relational aggression. Boys' aggression tends to be physical; girls' tends to be relational — exclusion, rumor, shifting alliances. Social media is the perfect amplifier for relational aggression, and it follows the child home, around the clock.
  • Emotions and symptoms spread more easily among girls. Girls share feelings more intensely with one another — a strength — but it also lets anxiety, eating disorders and even certain symptoms spread online almost like contagion. In 2020–21, doctors in several countries reported waves of teenage girls suddenly developing Tourette's-like tics, in step with the popularity of related TikTok videos.
  • Harassment and predators. Girls face higher risks of sexual harassment online and of strange adults asking them for pictures.

The boys' story is different, and slower. Haidt sees it as a retreat: boys withdraw from the real world into video games, pornography and online forums, showing up as falling behind in school, risk-aversion in real life and "failure to launch" — no job, no relationship, still living at home after graduation. If the phone-based childhood traps girls in a comparison contest that never ends, it pulls boys into a virtual world that is simply easier. He concedes that boys' disconnection from the real world began well before 2010; the smartphone accelerated it.

This changes how you think about "the same technology": it can hurt different groups by completely different routes, which is why averages mislead — a "small effect" computed across all teenagers can hide a large effect concentrated in one group.

6. Spiritual degradation: a loss that's hard to measure

One chapter is often skipped, though Haidt himself cares about it a great deal: spiritual elevation and degradation. It plugs into a dimension of his moral psychology, sanctity/degradation — the intuition that some things feel lofty and pure and make us want to be better, while others feel base and contaminating and make us smaller.

Even without religion, he argues, people need some of what religions traditionally supplied, and he lists several: shared rituals, moving in sync with others (singing, dancing, working together), silence and focus, experiences of self-transcendence, being slow to anger and quick to forgive, and awe in nature. The phone-based childhood pushes the wrong way on every one: it is isolating, disembodied, noisy and self-focused, it rewards instant outrage and public shaming, and it means kids hardly ever look up at the sky.

There's little hard data in this chapter, and Haidt admits it is more an argument than a finding. But it names something many readers feel: the problem isn't only that depression scores went up; it's that children are spending their days in a way that makes them smaller.

7. The collective action trap, and the four norms

If phones are this bad, why don't parents simply say no? Because it's a textbook collective action problem: for any single family, holding out is costly — the child is shut out of the class group chat, misses the social life, gets marked as odd. But if every family held out together, every child would be better off. Each parent makes the rational choice for their own child, and the sum is a worse outcome for everyone. Plenty of parents privately wish they could wait; nobody wants to go first.

The standard fix for a collective action problem is to change the rules so everyone moves together. Hence the four norms:

NormWhat it meansWhy
No smartphones before high schoolUntil about 14, only a basic phone for calls and textsKeep the most sensitive early-adolescent years for the real world
No social media before 16Open accounts after the most vulnerable stretch is overSocial comparison and relational aggression do the most damage in early adolescence
Phone-free schoolsBell to bell, phones locked in lockers or pouchesBreaks are when kids talk face to face; "no phones in class" isn't enough
Far more independence and free playLet kids walk to school, run errands, play freely; lengthen recessPut kids back in discover mode and restore the antifragile training

Table: Haidt's four norms. The first three subtract; the fourth adds. He keeps insisting that subtraction without giving real life back just lets other screens fill the freed-up time.

Note the detail in the third norm: Haidt rejects half-measures like "phones stay in backpacks during class," because as long as the phone is on the child, breaks get spent on it and in-school socializing is still hollowed out. On the fourth, he practices what he preaches: he co-founded Let Grow, a nonprofit that promotes childhood independence, with Lenore Skenazy, who launched the "free-range kids" movement.

The program has moved policy. Many US states have passed laws restricting phones in schools, and Australia passed a law in late 2024 barring under-16s from major social-media platforms, which took effect in late 2025.

This changes how you think about self-control: some problems aren't willpower problems but coordination problems. Telling one child to use their phone less, or one parent to hold out, achieves little; change the default rules for the group, and individuals finally get room to choose differently.

The Skeleton of the Argument

The book compresses into one line: in the early 2010s, adolescent mental-health indicators deteriorated in step across many developed countries, girls worst → the timing matches the spread of the always-on social phone → before that, overprotection had already eroded kids' chances to grow up in the real world (the decline of play-based childhood) → the phone-based childhood does damage through four routes — social deprivation, sleep deprivation, attention fragmentation, addiction — trapping girls in comparison and drawing boys into virtual worlds → this happens in the brain's most sensitive developmental window and holds kids in defend mode → because it's a collective action problem, it takes four collective norms to fix.

What does he rest it on? Four kinds of evidence stacked together: time series and cross-national comparisons (the synchronized turning point); correlational studies showing heavy users have higher rates of depression and anxiety; a handful of experiments — a 2018 Penn study that limited college students to 30 minutes of social media a day and found lower loneliness and depression a few weeks later, and a 2020 study that paid people to deactivate Facebook for four weeks and found a modest rise in well-being; and natural experiments, such as a 2022 paper in the American Economic Review that used Facebook's staggered arrival on US campuses between 2004 and 2006 and found its arrival was followed by more reported depression and anxiety among students. On top of that sits the platforms' own leaked research. His defense is that each kind of evidence is imperfect on its own, but they all point the same way. That is exactly where the critics aim.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: Haidt is anti-technology, anti-screen. No. His target is mainly two things: a personal smartphone in early adolescence, and social media built on algorithmic feeds and like-based comparison. He has no quarrel with kids using a computer at home, watching TV or video-chatting with friends.

Misreading two: limiting "screen time" is enough. He's explicit that subtraction is only half; the other half is giving back free play and real-world independence. Confiscate the phone but keep the child indoors and in tutoring all week, and nothing is solved.

Misreading three: it blames kids or parents. The opposite. The book's core is that this is a collective trap, not an individual failing — it asks for new rules, not more guilt.

Criticism one: correlation isn't causation, and the effects are small. This is the strongest objection. The developmental psychologist Candice Odgers reviewed the book in Nature in March 2024, under a headline asking, in substance, whether social media is really behind an epidemic of teenage mental illness. She argues Haidt turns a coincidence in time into a cause: across many studies, the link between social-media use and mental health is weak and its direction unclear — maybe depressed kids scroll more, rather than scrolling making kids depressed (reverse causation). In 2019, Oxford's Amy Orben and Andrew Przybylski ran a "specification curve analysis" — running every reasonable way of analyzing the same data to see how robust the conclusion is — across three large datasets, and found that digital technology use explained only about 0.4 percent of the variation in adolescent well-being, roughly on par with regularly eating potatoes or wearing glasses. Haidt replies that averages hide large effects on heavy users and on teenage girls, and that social media harms through network effects: even if you don't use it, when the whole class does, your social life changes too — a group-level harm that individual-level studies simply can't detect.

Criticism two: the experimental evidence is weaker and messier than the book suggests. Most deactivation experiments find small effects, some find none, and they measure short-term changes in adults or college students, which are hard to extrapolate to the long-term development of twelve-year-olds.

Criticism three: there are other suspects on the timeline. Critics point out that the 2010s also brought the aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis, routine school-shooting drills, intensifying academic competition and the destigmatizing of mental illness — more kids reporting anxiety may partly reflect that saying so became easier and screening became more common, not only a real rise in illness. Nor are the curves equally tidy in every country.

Criticism four: the old moral-panic script. Every new medium has been accused of ruining the young: in the 1950s the psychiatrist Fredric Wertham blamed comic books for juvenile delinquency; later came television, rock music and video games. Odgers and others worry that blaming everything on phones will distract society from harder problems — poverty, racism, underfunded mental-health care — and overlook the support the internet gives marginalized kids, such as LGBTQ teens. Haidt's answer is that this time is different: the dose is unprecedented, and the platforms are deliberately engineered to hook.

An honest verdict: there is no scholarly consensus yet. A fair reading is: the evidence that social media is the main cause is insufficient; the evidence that it does real harm to some kids, especially heavy-using teenage girls, is stronger; and Haidt's four norms are cheap with few side effects even if he has overstated the causation — which is why many critics still back parts of his program, such as phone-free schools and more free play.

Crossovers

  • Neuroscience · Hubel and Wiesel's kittens: In the 1960s they sewed one eye of young kittens shut for a few weeks; during the critical period, the visual cortex permanently handed its territory to the other eye, while doing the same to adult cats had almost no effect — the hardest evidence for experience-expectant development. Alike: in both, the environment during a window sets the wiring. Unlike: the visual critical period is narrow and rigid; the adolescent social window is far wider and more reversible.
  • Game theory · Thomas Schelling's hockey helmets: In Micromotives and Macrobehavior, Schelling noted that hockey players privately preferred helmets but wouldn't wear one alone — it looked timid and cost them vision; only a league rule got everyone to put them on. That is Haidt's phone trap exactly: rational individual choices add up to a bad collective outcome, and the fix is a rule change, not braver individuals. Unlike: a league can impose a helmet rule at a stroke; phone norms have no natural rule-maker.
  • Buddhism · guarding the sense doors (indriya-saṃvara): Early Buddhist practice asks monks to guard the six senses — not to stop seeing, but to avoid being dragged along by what the eye and ear meet, so that craving and distress don't rush in behind. The attention economy installs a hook at every one of those doors. Unlike: Buddhist restraint is an adult practitioner's inner skill, while Haidt assumes children don't have it yet and must change the environment from outside.
  • Distributed systems · receive livelock: In the 1990s Mogul and Ramakrishnan described what happens when packets arrive faster than a kernel can handle: every packet fires a CPU interrupt, the system spends all its time servicing interrupts, real work never finishes and throughput collapses to zero. Their fix was to switch off interrupts under heavy load and poll in batches. It's a near-perfect picture of a notification-bombarded brain, and "checking your phone in batches" is the human version of polling. Unlike: a brain's cost of getting back into focus after an interruption is far higher than a CPU's context switch.

① Between 2010 and 2015 childhood was rewired: the play-based childhood gave way to the phone-based one, and adolescent anxiety, depression and self-harm turned upward almost simultaneously across many countries.

② We overprotected children in the real world and underprotected them online — a death grip where we should let go, an open door where we should stand guard (paraphrased).

③ Children are antifragile: falls, fights, getting lost and being rejected are the vaccines from which they grow the ability to handle fear.

④ Free play is natural exposure therapy; remove it, fill the hours with a machine that pumps out threat signals, and kids get stuck in defend mode.

⑤ Adolescence is a sensitive period for social learning: the brain wires itself for the world it lives in, and if that world is a screen, it wires for the screen.

⑥ Four foundational harms — social deprivation, sleep deprivation, attention fragmentation, addiction; the phone's biggest harm may be not what it gives but what it takes away.

⑦ Girls get trapped in an endless contest of comparison and relational aggression; boys get pulled into a virtual world that is simply easier.

⑧ This is a collective action problem, not a willpower problem: every parent's rational choice adds up to every child being worse off.

⑨ Four norms — no smartphone before high school, no social media before 16, phone-free schools, far more independence and free play; the first three subtract, the fourth adds, and without the addition the rest is wasted.
