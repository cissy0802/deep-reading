The Social Animal

The Social Animal · Elliot Aronson · first edition 1972, many times revised

中文 →

In One Sentence

Fifty years of experiments driving one nail: you think what a person does is explained by what sort of person they are, when nearly always it is explained by what situation they were put in. Conformity, obedience, prejudice, walking past an emergency, even cruelty — none of it requires bad people; ordinary people plus a specific configuration of pressure will do. And the sharper half of the book is this: once someone has done a thing, they go back and rewrite their beliefs to match it, because what we refuse hardest to surrender is not an opinion but the picture of ourselves as decent, competent and not the kind of person who does something stupid.

Where It Sits

One disambiguation first: two well-known books are called The Social Animal. This is not David Brooks's 2011 bestseller of the same title. This is Elliot Aronson's (b. 1932), first published in 1972 — the most widely used introduction to social psychology ever written, revised through many editions (recent ones co-authored with his son Joshua Aronson), and routinely called the field's bible. The title comes from Aristotle's remark that man is a social animal.

Aronson's own position matters: he was Leon Festinger's student — Festinger created cognitive dissonance theory — and became one of that theory's most important revisers. He is also the only person in the history of the American Psychological Association to have won its awards for distinguished writing, distinguished teaching and distinguished research. The book does not read like a textbook because it was never written as one: it is an argumentative essay with opinions, a temper, and serious moral commitments (racial prejudice, war, school violence) — with an experiment underneath every claim.

The Central Claims

  • The power of the situation is systematically underrated; the power of personality systematically overrated. "He's just that kind of person" is nearly always a lazy explanation; change the situation and the same people behave very differently.
  • Attitudes are often not the cause of behaviour but its consequence. People act, then supply themselves with a reason, then genuinely believe the reason. That is how dissonance works.
  • Therefore prejudice and hostility are not argued away; they are engineered away. Put people inside a structure where they must depend on one another to succeed and prejudice falls by itself — Aronson's own most useful contribution.

The Core Ideas, One by One

The fundamental attribution error: character for them, circumstance for me

The foundation of the book. The fundamental attribution error names a stable bias: in explaining other people's behaviour we overweight disposition and underweight situation; in explaining our own we do exactly the reverse.

Examples are everywhere. Someone cuts the queue: what a boor. You cut the queue: you were late, it was a special case. A colleague says nothing in the meeting: he lacks the nerve. You say nothing: it wasn't the right room for it. The asymmetry has an obvious mechanism: when you look at someone else, the most visually salient thing is the person and the situation is background; when you look at yourself, you cannot see your own face, and the most salient thing is precisely the situation.

How it changes what you ask: once installed, this makes you suspicious of every character-based explanation. After an incident at work, "who was careless?" almost never reaches the cause; the useful question is "what process makes a normal person do that?" A child lies — rather than judging their honesty, look at the punishment structure they are standing inside. The whole book performs this single move over and over: replace "what kind of person is he" with "what position was he put in."

Conformity: two entirely different pressures, not to be confused

Two classic experiments, and they are not about the same thing.

Sherif's autokinetic study (1935). Stare at a stationary pinpoint of light in a completely dark room and, because of small involuntary eye movements, it appears to drift — the autokinetic effect. Asked alone how far it moved, people give wildly different answers. Put several together taking turns aloud, and within a few rounds their estimates converge on a shared value. The crucial step is the last one: tested alone again afterwards, they keep using the group's number. They were not pretending to fit in; the group's answer had become their own judgement.

Asch's line study (1951). A standard line, three comparison lines, and the match is obvious. Everyone else in the room is a confederate, and on certain trials they unanimously give a plainly wrong answer. The result: about three-quarters of participants conform at least once, and across all critical trials roughly a third of answers go along with the error. These people are not confused — in interviews many said they knew the answer was wrong and simply did not want to be the only one out of step.

The difference between the two studies is the book's most usable pair of concepts:

  • Informational influence — I don't know the truth, and other people's judgements are genuine evidence. In Sherif's study there is no correct answer, so conforming is rational and it gets internalised.
  • Normative influence — I do know the truth, but I don't want to be excluded. In Asch's study the lines are unmistakable, so conformity buys social safety only; it changes what people say, usually not what they privately believe.

Asch also isolated a variable that matters enormously: a single ally — even one whose answer is also wrong, merely different from the majority's — collapses the conformity rate. The implication is concrete: breaking unanimity is far easier and far more effective than persuading a majority. The first person in a meeting to say "I see this differently" changes nothing about the content; they change the structure, by deleting the fact that everyone agrees — and only then can anyone else's dissent surface.

Obedience: Milgram, and what the study actually shows

From 1961, Stanley Milgram at Yale had participants play "teacher," instructed by an experimenter in a grey coat to deliver escalating shocks to a "learner" (an actor) next door for wrong answers, on a panel labelled from 15 volts up to 450 ("Danger: Severe Shock"). The learner protests, complains of his heart, and finally goes silent. In the baseline condition about 65% of participants went all the way to 450 volts. Psychiatrists asked to predict beforehand had estimated one or two per cent.

The study is usually cited to show that people are innately obedient, which is precisely not its point. Milgram ran well over a dozen variations, and obedience ranged from near zero to over ninety per cent. What the study establishes is which variables move it:

How it changes what you do: do not read this as proof that people are evil. Read it as an actionable checklist. To get fewer harmful acts out of an organisation, don't try to screen for virtuous individuals; do these four things instead: make consequences visible, stop authority from issuing orders at a distance without accountability, weaken the automatic force of institutional prestige, and make sure someone can always be the first to refuse in public. This is Aronson's method throughout — every experiment has to land on "so what should we change about the environment."

The controversies must be stated too (see the objections below): the ethics, whether all participants believed the shocks were real, and the fact that the experimenter repeatedly went off-script to apply pressure. These damage the cleanliness of the 65% figure without overturning the core finding that situational variables dominate dispositional ones.

Cognitive dissonance: act first, then rewrite the belief to fit

Aronson's home ground. Cognitive dissonance, introduced by Festinger, says: holding two conflicting cognitions at once — especially "I did X" and "X was stupid or wrong" — produces real discomfort, which people actively reduce; and since "I did it" cannot be undone, the belief is what gives way.

The founding study (Festinger & Carlsmith, 1959). Participants spent an hour on a crushingly dull task (loading spools onto a tray, turning pegs a quarter-turn, over and over), then were asked to tell the next participant it had been interesting. One group was paid $1, another $20. The counter-intuitive result: the $1 group afterwards believed the task really had been interesting. Why? The $20 group had a ready external justification — I lied for money — which absorbed the dissonance. The $1 group had no adequate reason, leaving only one route: change the belief. "Actually, it was sort of interesting."

Aronson's key revision: he argued that dissonance bites hardest not when any two cognitions conflict but when behaviour threatens the self-concept — specifically the beliefs that I am competent, that I am decent, and that I can predict and control my own conduct. What actually stings is not "I told a lie" but "I, an honest person, told a lie." That revision turns the theory from a problem in logic into a problem in self-esteem, and explains why people with higher self-regard experience more dissonance after acting badly.

That last one deserves its own paragraph; it is the darkest and most important idea in the book. Common sense says "I hated him, so I hurt him." Dissonance theory says causation also runs backwards: because I hurt him, I must now despise him. The mechanism: "I am a good person" and "I just harmed someone innocent" cannot coexist, and the harm is already done and unchangeable — so the only movable term is "innocent." He wasn't innocent. He had it coming. People like that always do. And once that repair is complete, the threshold for the next harm is lower. This explains a great deal: the ratcheting of wartime atrocity, workplace exclusion getting steadily nastier, the self-reinforcement of domestic coldness, and why perpetrators so often resent their victims more than the reverse.

Aronson later built this into a whole book with Carol Tavris, Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me), using a fine image — the pyramid: two people start at the apex with nearly identical views and make one small opposite choice; each subsequent act of self-justification slides them a little further down their own face of the pyramid, until years later they stand at opposite corners of the base, each certain the other was always a different kind of person. Divergence does not begin with disagreement; it begins with a small choice followed by a long run of self-justification.

Prejudice and the jigsaw classroom: change the structure, not the sermon

The most valuable practical result in the book. Two prior findings set it up.

First, how cheaply prejudice can be manufactured: Henri Tajfel's minimal group paradigm. Sort strangers by a meaningless criterion — a coin flip, or which of two painters they prefer — with no shared history, no conflict of interest, and no acquaintance between members. People immediately begin favouring their own group and disparaging the other. The conclusion is cold: the us/them partition alone is sufficient to produce prejudice; no substantive reason is required.

Second, how to dissolve it: Sherif's Robbers Cave study (1954). At a summer camp, boys were split into two teams and real hostility manufactured through competition (it escalated to raiding each other's cabins within days). Researchers then tried simply bringing the groups together for meals and films — which failed, and merely supplied new venues for conflict. What worked was the superordinate goal: a task both groups urgently needed and neither could accomplish alone (the water supply broke and had to be repaired jointly; a truck stalled and everyone had to haul it). After several of these the hostility largely dissolved. The operative word is not contact but interdependence.

This matches Gordon Allport's contact hypothesis: intergroup contact reduces prejudice only under conditions — equal status, shared goals, cooperation rather than competition, and institutional support. Merely putting people in the same room does nothing.

Then comes Aronson's contribution. In 1971, schools in Austin, Texas had just desegregated and classroom hostility was getting worse, not better. Called in to help, Aronson took the conditions above and engineered them into a technique a teacher can actually run every day: the jigsaw classroom.

  • Students are put in groups of six and the day's material is cut into six parts — each child receives only one part.
  • The exam covers all of it. So every child must listen to the other five to pass, and any child who is mocked, interrupted or frozen out directly damages the grade of whoever mocked them.
  • Children holding the same section first meet across groups (an "expert group") to master it, then return to teach their own group.

Change the structure and behaviour follows immediately: the children discover that interrupting and belittling costs them personally, and start asking questions, waiting, and coaching the slower speaker. Reported outcomes included improved liking across ethnic lines, higher self-esteem and better performance among minority students, and reduced absenteeism. How it changes your approach: the least efficient way to reduce hostility in a group is a meeting about unity; the most efficient is to redesign the task until "I want a good outcome" and "I need you to succeed" are the same sentence. This transfers directly to teams, families and cross-functional work.

The bystander effect: not coldness, diluted responsibility

In 1968 John Darley and Bibb Latané ran a series of studies in which participants believed someone nearby was having a seizure. Believing they were the only listener, about 85% went for help; believing four others also heard it, the figure fell to about 31%, and those who did help took markedly longer. In another study smoke was pumped into a room: alone, most people reported it; with two impassive confederates sitting still, the great majority sat there until the room filled.

Two mechanisms, neither of them "people are callous." First, diffusion of responsibility — the more bystanders, the smaller each person's share, and surely someone else is handling it. Second, pluralistic ignorance — emergencies are usually ambiguous, so everyone glances at everyone else to decide whether this counts as one, while those others are glancing back. All parties read calm off each other and collectively infer a conclusion no individual actually believes.

The countermeasure is precise and worth memorising: if you are the one who needs help, do not shout at a crowd — designate an individual. "You, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance." That single act dissolves both the diffusion of responsibility and the ambiguity at once. It is the most life-saving practical line in the book.

One honesty note: this research has long been packaged with the story of the 38 witnesses who supposedly watched Kitty Genovese's murder without calling police, and that narrative has been substantially discredited — the New York Times published its own correction in 2016; the number of witnesses, what they could see, and whether anyone called were all overstated or misreported. The experiments stand on their own: the story was wrong, the effect is not.

Persuasion, esteem and expectation: several smaller but very portable mechanisms

  • The pratfall effect (Aronson et al., 1966) — a highly competent person who spills coffee becomes more likeable; the identical blunder by a mediocre person makes them less so. A small failure adds only to someone already clearly capable, because it removes distance.
  • The gain-loss effect — someone initially reserved about you who warms up wins more of your regard than someone who praised you from the start; and going from praise to criticism wounds far more than steady criticism. People are far more sensitive to the change in an evaluation than to its level.
  • The self-fulfilling prophecy — in Rosenthal and Jacobson's classroom study, teachers were told at random that certain pupils were about to bloom, and by year's end those pupils had improved, because the teachers unknowingly gave them more attention, harder questions and longer to answer. (To be accurate: the effect size in that study is now considered much smaller than originally reported and its replication is contested, though the underlying mechanism — expectations shaping behaviour that shapes outcomes — has support across settings.)
  • Self-persuasion beats external persuasion, and by a wide margin, because a reason a person generated themselves is not one they will argue against. This is the insufficient-punishment finding applied: to make a behaviour last, use just enough external pressure and no more.

The Argument in Outline

Compressed, the book does three things:

  • One: dismantle dispositional explanation. Asch, Milgram, Darley and Latané, Tajfel — ordinary people, given the right setup, will conform, shock a stranger, walk past an emergency, and discriminate against a group defined by a coin flip. The conclusion is not "people are bad" but "the variance in behaviour lives mostly in situations, not in personalities."
  • Two: supply the psychological engine that lets situations change people — dissonance. Nobody can hold "I am a good person" alongside "I just did a bad thing," and the act cannot be revoked, so the belief is rewritten to fit the act. This is why situational effects persist: the situation doesn't just make you do it once, it makes you into someone who thinks that way.
  • Three: turn it around and use it. If structure drives behaviour and behaviour rewrites belief, then the leverage is in the structure: the jigsaw classroom, superordinate goals, designating a helper, breaking unanimity, applying just enough pressure. That is the book's moral position — it is not out to leave you pessimistic about human nature, but to hand you the few handles that actually move.

Misreadings & Serious Objections

Misreading one: hearing situationism as an excuse. Aronson blocks this repeatedly: to explain is not to exonerate. Showing that ordinary people commit cruelty under certain pressures does not absolve them; it tells you that prevention belongs in the pressure structure rather than in the post-hoc hunt for villains — and, uncomfortably, that you are not an exception.

Misreading two: remembering Asch as "everyone conforms." The accurate number is more interesting the other way round: across all critical trials, roughly two-thirds of answers were independent and correct. Asch himself set out to demonstrate independence. The right statement is that conformity is disturbingly high, not that it is the rule.

Misreading three: treating the 38 witnesses as fact. As above, the opening anecdote has been heavily corrected. That textbooks carried it for decades is a lesson social psychology learned about itself: a story that is too useful outlives the data it was recruited to support.

Objection one: the replication crisis. This is the pair of glasses to wear while reading the book today. Since the 2010s, psychology — social psychology first and hardest — has been through large-scale replication failures: priming with age-related words to slow people's walking, ego depletion (willpower as a muscle that runs out), power posing raising testosterone, the facial feedback hypothesis and other widely cited effects failed to reproduce, or shrank drastically, in large pre-registered replications. The causes include underpowered samples, publication bias (only significant results get printed) and excessive analytic flexibility (p-hacking — adjusting the analysis until something turns significant). The honest assessment: the book's load-bearing structure — conformity, obedience, dissonance, intergroup prejudice, the bystander effect — has broadly held up and mostly survives in meta-analysis; but the scattering of small, charming one-shot effects should be read at a discount.

Objection two: the collapse of the Stanford Prison Experiment. Zimbardo's 1971 study, so often cited alongside Milgram's, is now regarded as seriously compromised: Thibault Le Texier's 2019 archival work showed the researchers gave the "guards" explicit behavioural coaching rather than letting conduct emerge, and some participants later said they were performing. It works better today as a cautionary tale than as evidence. (Distinguish carefully: its collapse does not implicate Milgram, whose work had systematic variations and independent partial replications, including Jerry Burger's 2009 study within modern ethical limits, which found results close to the original.)

Objection three: Milgram's own study has real defects. Gina Perry's work in the Yale archives showed the experimenter frequently exceeded the script (which permitted only four prods) to keep pressing; that a substantial share of participants afterwards said they had doubted the shocks were real — and that this group obeyed at higher rates; and that the individual variation conditions had small samples. These damage the precision of "65%" without overturning the direction of the finding. Add the ethical problem — participants underwent genuine extreme distress — and no such study would clear a review board today.

Objection four: the WEIRD sample problem. Joseph Henrich and colleagues pointed out that the overwhelming majority of psychology's conclusions come from WEIRD populations — Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich and Democratic — and very often from undergraduates specifically. This bears directly on this book: conformity rates vary substantially across cultures (cross-cultural meta-analysis finds markedly higher conformity in collectivist settings), and the fundamental attribution error is notably weaker in East Asian samples. So the accurate reading is: the book describes mechanisms that are genuinely general, but the strength and triggering conditions of those mechanisms carry a heavy cultural coefficient.

Ten Sentences

1. We explain others by their character and ourselves by our circumstances — that asymmetry is the fundamental attribution error, and dismantling it is the first thing the book does.

2. There are two kinds of conformity and they are not the same: following others when you don't know the answer (informational) becomes real belief; following them when you do know but don't want to stand out (normative) usually changes only what you say.

3. Asch's real gift is not "people conform" but "one person breaking ranks first collapses the rate" — breaking unanimity is far easier than persuading a majority.

4. Milgram's 65% is not proof that people are evil: across his variations obedience ran from near zero to over ninety per cent, and the four handles are visibility of the victim, presence of the authority, institutional prestige, and whether anyone refuses first.

5. Dissonance is not about logical contradiction but about threat to the self-image (Aronson's key revision): what stings is not "I lied" but "I, a decent person, lied."

6. The less external justification, the more sincerely people come to believe what they did ($1 beats $20 at making a dull task interesting) — and punishment that is only just sufficient is what gets internalised as "I don't want to."

7. The darkest idea: causation runs backwards too. Not "I hated him so I hurt him" but "I hurt him, so I must now demean him," because otherwise "I am a good person" will not hold. Atrocity accelerates itself this way.

8. Divergence doesn't start with disagreement: two people who begin almost identically make one small opposite choice, and every subsequent self-justification slides them further down opposite faces of the pyramid (the image from Mistakes Were Made).

9. Prejudice yields to interdependence, not contact: cut the material into six parts, give each child one, examine all of it — the jigsaw classroom works because it makes "I want a good grade" and "I need you to succeed" the same sentence.

10. When you need help, never shout at a crowd — designate a person: "you, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance" dissolves diffused responsibility and ambiguity at once. (And read the whole book wearing the replication-crisis glasses: the load-bearing findings hold, the charming small effects should be discounted.)
