The Wisdom of Life

Aphorismen zur Lebensweisheit · Arthur Schopenhauer · 1851

中文 →

In One Sentence

The book answers a single question — what actually determines whether a life goes well? — and the answer is close to cruel: the decisive factor is what you are (health, temperament, mind), not what you have or how you appear to others, even though the last two consume almost everyone's entire supply of effort for the worst return. And beneath that sits the sharper move: happiness is negative. It is not a thing you can acquire; it is the gap left when pain is taken away. So the wise person does not maximise pleasure — they minimise pain, friction and self-expenditure.

Where It Sits

Schopenhauer (1788–1860) is best known for The World as Will and Representation (1818), a book that on publication was almost completely ignored. He was proud, caustic, and — in a famous piece of self-sabotage — scheduled his Berlin lectures against Hegel's, drawing almost no students. For roughly thirty years the profession treated him as though he did not exist. Then, in 1851, at sixty-three, he published a two-volume collection of essays called Parerga and Paralipomena, and became famous almost overnight — and The Wisdom of Life is the longest and most readable piece in it. Keep one fact in view throughout: this is not his system; it is a practical handbook written in the system's peripheral light. He says as much at the outset — the essay proceeds from an everyday, empirical standpoint, provisionally setting aside the higher standpoint from which human existence is a mistake that should not have happened. Read it without that caveat and the tone will mislead you.

The Central Claims

  • Most of your happiness is fixed by what you are, and that item is largely not acquirable. Property and reputation are periphery; spending your life on the periphery is the lowest-yield investment available.
  • Pleasure is negative, pain is positive. What we feel directly is always lack, hurt or disturbance; what we call happiness is only the quiet while those are suspended. So the correct target is less suffering, not more pleasure.
  • Life swings between pain and boredom. Want drives the poor into suffering; sufficiency drives the comfortable into emptiness. The only thing that blocks both ends is a person's own inner resources.

The Core Ideas, One by One

Three sources: what you are, what you have, what you represent

The book's skeleton is a threefold division that Schopenhauer credits openly to Aristotle, who in the Nicomachean Ethics sorted goods into goods of the soul, goods of the body, and external goods. Schopenhauer sharpens it into an audit:

Everything else in the book argues one point: the first row outweighs the other two by an enormous margin.
SourceWhat it coversSchopenhauer's verdict
What you are (personality)Health, strength, temperament, moral character, intelligence, and the capacity for feeling and taste that follows from itThe decisive item. It sets how much you can extract from any circumstance whatever
What you have (property)Possessions, wealth, resources at your disposalRemoves the pain of want; cannot manufacture happiness — and appetite inflates in step with it
What you represent (standing)Rank, honour, reputation, fameThe most overvalued of the three: it is not located in you at all, but inside other people's heads

Why is the first item so overwhelming? Because happiness is not what happens to you but what a particular consciousness makes of what happens. The same dinner party is noise and falsity to a melancholic and warmth and goodwill to a cheerful man; the same free afternoon is a punishment to an empty head and a luxury to a furnished one. Schopenhauer puts it plainly: what a man gets out of the world is settled in advance by what he is able to hold; he cannot draw out more than his own capacity (paraphrased).

The practical consequence is unforgiving. It means that changing city, job or partner shifts far less happiness than people expect — because the machine that converts events into feeling has not been changed. This has a surprisingly hard modern echo: hedonic adaptation, the finding that emotional reactions to new circumstances decay back toward a baseline within months, and the observed stability and substantial heritability of subjective well-being. Schopenhauer had no data, but he guessed the direction right.

Happiness is negative: you never taste "good," only "not hurting"

This is the least intuitive and most valuable idea in the book. What does the negativity of happiness mean? That only lack, pain and desire are experienced as positive contents; satisfaction is merely their disappearance and has no substance of its own.

Test it. Right now you cannot feel that your ten fingers are healthy, that your stomach does not hurt, that you owe nobody money — every one of those goods is silent. Let any of them fail and it instantly fills your entire consciousness. Health is the thing you never thank and first see on the day you lose it. A prize wanted for six months sinks into the background within days of arriving, while any single thorn can occupy an entire afternoon. From this Schopenhauer draws a rule of action, and cites Aristotle for it: the prudent man aims not at pleasure but at the absence of pain (paraphrased from the Nicomachean Ethics).

How this changes your decisions: you stop asking "which option is better?" and start asking "which recurring miseries can I delete?" Judge a job by its guaranteed daily costs — the commute, the humiliations, the lost sleep, the person you cannot stand — before its promised highlights. Judge a relationship by its ordinary friction rather than its peaks. On this accounting, peaks decay and friction does not. He adds a harder corollary: never risk suffering in order to buy a pleasure — the trade is nearly always a loss, because you pay in something positive and are paid in something negative.

The pendulum: pain at one end, boredom at the other

So what remains once pain has been removed? Not happiness, says Schopenhauer, but boredom — his most famous image: life swings like a pendulum between suffering and tedium.

Schematic: unmet desire is pain, met desire is emptiness — the bob never rests at the centre.

The mechanism runs like this. The will — Schopenhauer's term for the blind, reasonless urge that drives a creature — is by nature insatiable. A desire unmet is pain; a desire met extinguishes itself and leaves an interval of aimless idling, which is boredom. Hence his line that the life of the poor is a struggle against want and the life of the rich a struggle against tedium (paraphrased) — and the second is not obviously the lighter fight. Socialising, gambling, drink, restless travel, thrill-seeking: on his reading these are all flights from boredom, temporary loads bolted onto an idling engine.

The diagnostic this yields is genuinely useful: how much a person dreads their own company is roughly how impoverished their interior is. Boredom is not a property of the situation but of the capacity — the same wet Sunday afternoon is a blank sentence to one person and a luxury to another. Schopenhauer pushes this to a nearly spiteful conclusion: a person's need for company stands in inverse proportion to their intellectual worth (paraphrased). People crowd together not so much out of love for others as out of an inability to tolerate themselves.

Wealth is seawater: desire tracks ratios, not absolutes

Schopenhauer is not piously anti-money — quite the opposite. He treats property as a real good: a sea wall that keeps out an entire class of low, grinding pains. He even defends inherited wealth openly, on the grounds that it lets a man of ability decline to sell his life for a living. (He himself lived off his father's business estate and never worked for a salary — we will return to that honestly.)

What he is dismantling is something else: money removes pain but cannot manufacture happiness, and appetite grows alongside it. His image is the most quoted line in the book: wealth is like seawater — the more you drink, the thirstier you become (paraphrased).

Why should that be? Because satisfaction with wealth depends not on the absolute sum but on the ratio of what you have to what you claim: dissatisfaction equals demands minus possessions. A man who never conceived of owning a yacht suffers nothing from not owning one; a man who has just been aboard one does. Every rise in income quietly raises the denominator too, so the gap holds steady. From this he draws a rule better than most modern personal-finance advice: the real use of money is to buy out what you no longer have to do, not to buy in what you still want. Convert money into unowned time and unforced options rather than into new entry points for desire — which is close to what the evidence now says, since the marginal contribution of income to well-being falls away sharply with level while the contribution of autonomy does not.

The you inside other people's heads: rank, honour, fame, and a losing trade

This is the chapter where he attacks hardest, and he starts from an observation that is almost brutal in its simplicity: "what I am in the eyes of others" is not physically located in me at all — it sits in other people's heads. It is a second-hand, indirect existence. And for this thing, which is not even on our own premises, people will spend health, money, leisure, peace of mind, and occasionally their lives. He calls it a universally prevalent folly.

He separates three things that are usually mixed, and the definitions are worth stating exactly:

  • Rank — a conventional value, a system of artificial signs. It works, but only inside the circle that honours the signs; outside it, nothing.
  • Honour — defined precisely: not the belief that I possess some special merit, but the absence of any reason to think I lack the ordinary qualities a person is supposed to have (honesty, keeping one's word, not betraying). Honour is therefore losable but not gainable: you cannot accumulate it by keeping your word a hundred times, but one breach empties the account. Grasp that asymmetry and you understand why the reputational ledger has only a debit column.
  • Fame — the mirror image: it is the claim to have what others lack, so it is gainable and hard to lose, and it must be earned. He adds a very Schopenhauerian rider: fame won by deeds spreads fast and dies fast; fame won by works grows slowly and lives long (paraphrased). He wrote that after thirty years of being ignored.

His most persuasive stroke is exposing the cost structure of shame: why does one cutting remark in public keep you awake longer than a real material loss? Because our sensitivity to what others think is an alarm system installed by evolution and society, tuned far more sensitively than its actual value warrants. His advice is not the empty "stop caring what people think," but an executable valuation exercise: look concretely at what is inside those heads — mostly judgements you would not respect — and then ask why you are handing them the pricing of your own peace.

Solitude: not a punishment, but the natural state of the inwardly rich

Schopenhauer defends solitude not by calling it noble but by pricing it. Company almost always requires subtraction. In a group you must pitch what you say low enough for the dullest person present, suppress your sharpest judgements, and maintain an amiability that is not real. The entry fee for society is the best part of your own mind. For an inwardly poor person the fee is a bargain — he had little to lose and gets a defence against boredom in return. For an inwardly rich person it is a pure loss. So the widely quoted line — "either solitude or vulgarity" (paraphrased) — is not a pose; it is the bottom line of that calculation.

He has a finer image for the same problem, from elsewhere in the same collection: the porcupines. A group of porcupines huddles together against the winter cold; pressed close, they prick each other; separated, they freeze. They shuffle back and forth until they settle on a middle distance — not warm, but not painful. That middle distance is politeness and good manners. The conclusion follows: whoever has enough heat of his own can simply stay outside the huddle, neither pricking nor pricked.

But state the position fully: Schopenhauer's solitude is conditional, and the condition is that you have sufficient inner resources — his template is the scholar, the artist, the thinker. He concedes that the young must first learn to bear a degree of solitude precisely because those resources have not yet grown. This chapter is also where the book is most vulnerable, which the criticism section takes up.

The technique of living: draw the boundary small, finish today

The later part of the essay is a set of maxims, and behind them lies a single axis: if happiness is negative, then the art of living is not expansion but limitation and loss-prevention. The most valuable of them:

  • Limiting your horizon is the nearest thing to happiness. The fewer things you must look after, the smaller the surface available to be struck. This is not passivity; it is pulling finite attention back from an outside world that can always worsen, to an inside one that is yours.
  • Never trade health for anything. His formulation, in paraphrase: the greatest of all follies is to sacrifice health for any other thing whatever. The reason is systemic, not sentimental: health is the largest single supplier of "negative" happiness — without it nothing else registers as good; with it, the whole background is silently well.
  • Envy is the most direct killer of contentment, because it pegs your feeling to a denominator someone else controls. His antidote is to look downward rather than upward — not moral advice but the ratio mechanism run in reverse.
  • Live today, because only today is real. He is repeatedly scathing about two figures: the man who sacrifices the present to the future, and the man who spends it regretting the past. Both feed the one genuinely existing stretch of life to something that does not exist.

His chapter on the ages of life ends with a fine summary: the first half of life is characterised by a longing for happiness, the second by a dread of misfortune (paraphrased) — because by then one has understood that happiness is a chimera and only suffering is real. The world seems inexhaustible when young because you have not yet seen its repetitions; it shrinks with age, but something is returned in exchange: the fire of the will burns lower, and the amplitude between pain and boredom narrows with it. That is the book's gentlest sentence about old age: not an increase in happiness, but a decrease in torment.

The Argument in Outline

Compressed to a single line, the book is a three-step inference:

  • Step one (metaphysics): a human being contains an insatiable will. Desire unmet is pain; desire met is boredom. Therefore sustained happiness does not exist structurally.
  • Step two (psychology): given that, the only thing directly experienced is pain, and happiness is an interval between pains. So the whole ledger must be recomputed: chasing pleasure is chasing a negative number, and the correct objective is to lower suffering and avoid waste.
  • Step three (practice): what lowers suffering most? Back to the three sources. Health and mind (what you are) supply both a painless background and the ability not to be bored; property removes a class of low pains but inflates appetite in step; other people's opinion is literally located elsewhere and has the worst return of all. Hence: keep the body, furnish the mind, convert enough money into free time, cut the exposure to judgement, draw the boundary small, and finish today.

The book does not persuade by rigour. It persuades by using an ice-cold accountant's voice to invert the default ranking of values: you think you are chasing happiness, but you are avoiding pain; you think money buys pleasure, but it only buys the absence of hurt; you think you live for yourself, while most of your effort goes into an image of you kept in someone else's head.

Misreadings & Serious Objections

Misreading one: taking it for the whole of Schopenhauer. This is a eudaimonology — a treatise deliberately lowered to the everyday plane, as he says himself. On his real position, the highest wisdom is not to arrange a good life but to deny the will, to stop desiring at all. The book is, in a sense, his second-best plan for people who do not intend to take that road. Read as self-help, it loses the irony.

Misreading two: hearing "happiness is negative" as nihilism. It is not "nothing matters." It is a claim about accounting convention: book happiness under losses avoided rather than gains acquired. That convention in fact generates a great deal of positive, actionable behaviour — protect health, guard leisure, cut envy, limit exposure.

Objection one: his prescription requires a ticket he had already been handed. This is the strongest case against the book. Schopenhauer lived on an inheritance and never needed a salary. Advice to "buy out what you need not do," "decline the demands of society," "keep your leisure inviolate" is substantially unexecutable for someone who must show up daily and depend on colleagues. He wrote a way of life underwritten by financial independence and unusual intellect, and presented it as universal wisdom. That does not invalidate the psychological observations, but it does load the prescriptions with heavy prerequisites of class and temperament.

Objection two: the gap between doctrine and conduct is documented. He preached composure and moderation while being famously irascible, arrogant and litigious. The best-known episode: a quarrel on his landing with a seamstress named Caroline Marquet, whom he pushed and injured; after years of litigation the court ordered him to pay her a quarterly sum for the rest of her life. On the notice of her death he wrote a Latin pun — Obit anus, abit onus ("the old woman dies, the burden departs"). The same collection contains an essay on women whose contents are, by any standard, plain prejudice. The honest way to read him is as an exceptionally sharp instrument, not as a model of character.

Objection three: the solitude chapter is where modern evidence pushes back hardest. He treats company as a net cost and solitude as the default condition of the inwardly rich. The most robust findings in well-being research point the other way: close, sustained relationships are among the strongest single predictors of subjective well-being and of health in later life — a result the long-running Harvard Study of Adult Development and similar cohorts keep recovering — while prolonged isolation tracks with significant health risk. One defence is available to him: what he attacks is mostly obligatory, depleting sociability rather than deep friendship, and the book does endorse few but real friends. Still, executed literally, his advice costs something he never entered in the ledger.

Objection four: the assumption that the first item is unchangeable is too strong. He holds temperament, character and intellect to be innate and fixed for life, and this is one of the load-bearing supports of his pessimism. The better modern statement is that the baseline is indeed fairly stable and partly heritable, but it is not immovable — sleep, exercise, treatment of chronic pain, treatment of depression and anxiety all move exactly the thing he called "what you are." Here he turned a statistical regularity into an iron law.

Schopenhauer Beyond This Book

Without this section you would leave thinking Schopenhauer was an aphorist. He was not: under every piece of advice here sits a complete metaphysics, set out in The World as Will and Representation. Three things are worth having.

First, the world has two sides: representation and will. He starts from Kant, who argued we can know only appearances and never the thing-in-itself (what a thing is independently of anyone's perception). Schopenhauer objects that there is one interior route: when you experience your own body from the inside, what you feel is the will itself. Raising your arm is not a thought followed by a movement; the raising is the will made visible. He generalises: the inner nature of the whole world is that same blind, aimless, ceaseless striving, and everything else is its appearance at some level of organisation. That is what "will" means in his vocabulary — not willpower, but a cosmic push with no reason behind it and no destination in front of it.

Second, the pessimism is therefore derived, not a mood. If the substance of the world is unappeasable striving, suffering is not an accident of life but its structure. That is where "happiness is negative" actually comes from.

Third, he offers two exits, and this book mentions neither. One is aesthetic suspension: absorbed completely in a work of art or a landscape, you briefly stop willing — you stop asking what the thing is for and become, in his phrase, a pure will-less subject of knowing. Music ranks highest in his system because the other arts reproduce the will's appearances while music is a copy of the will itself. It is the one place in the whole edifice where a person can breathe. The other exit is denial of the will, reached through compassion — which he makes the sole real foundation of morality, because in pitying another you momentarily see through the illusion that you and they are separate beings — and running on to asceticism and renunciation. He states openly that he learned this step from India: he was the first European philosopher to take the Upanishads and Buddhism seriously enough to build them into the main line of Western philosophy, kept a Buddha on his desk, and called a Latin translation of the Upanishads the consolation of his life. Once you see that Indian thread, The Wisdom of Life reads for what it is — a much more radical road, walked a short distance and then halted.

Ten Sentences

1. What decides your happiness is mainly not what you own or how you are seen, but what kind of person you are — the world is felt through that machine, and you cannot draw out more than its capacity.

2. Pleasure is negative and pain is positive: you never feel your teeth not aching, your debts cleared, your body sound — but let one fail and it fills your whole mind. Happiness is not won; it is vacated.

3. So the prudent aim not at pleasure but at the absence of pain (after Aristotle, paraphrased) — judge any choice first by its guaranteed daily costs, then by its promised highlights.

4. Life is a pendulum between pain and boredom: wanting hurts, having empties. Only one thing blocks both ends — your own inner resources.

5. "Wealth is like seawater: the more you drink, the thirstier you get" (paraphrased). Dissatisfaction equals demands minus possessions, and every raise quietly raises the demands too.

6. The real use of money is to buy out what you no longer have to do, not to buy in what you still want — convert it into unowned time rather than new entry points for desire.

7. "What I am in others' eyes" is not in me at all; it is in their heads. Handing the pricing of your own peace to people whose judgement you do not respect is the most common of follies.

8. Honour can be lost but not gained (it is only the absence of reason to think you lack ordinary decency); fame can be gained and is hard to lose. Separate those two ledgers and you stop spending a life accumulating something that only debits.

9. The entry fee for company is the sharpest part of your mind, and the dread of one's own company is roughly a measure of inner poverty. (Discount this one: the modern evidence makes deep, steady relationships a main pillar of well-being.)

10. Sacrificing health for anything else is the greatest folly (paraphrased); and the art of living is limitation, not expansion — fewer things to guard, envy cut off, and today actually finished, because today is the only part that exists.
