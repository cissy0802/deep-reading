The Master and Margarita

Master i Margarita · Mikhail Bulgakov · written 1928–1940, unpublished in the author's lifetime; a censored text ran in the Soviet magazine Moskva in 1966–67

中文 →

In One Sentence

The devil turns up in 1930s Moscow with a talking black cat the size of a hog and runs rings around a city that has officially abolished God, the devil and chance; meanwhile a novel by a hounded writer tells how, two thousand years earlier, a Roman official sent an innocent man to his death because he was afraid. The book's claim: the worst sin isn't malice but cowardice, and the truth once written, like love once given, cannot be burned.

Where It Sits

Bulgakov (1891–1940) trained as a doctor before turning to plays and fiction; under Stalin he was banned from print, savaged in the press, and kept alive by a job at the Moscow Art Theatre. He worked on this novel for twelve years and was still dictating revisions on his deathbed. It is widely counted among the summits of twentieth-century Russian literature, and the most beloved piece of Soviet "writing for the drawer": work that could not be published while its author lived.

The Core Argument

  • Cowardice is the worst of vices. The real sinner here isn't Satan. It's Pontius Pilate, who knows the prisoner is innocent and signs the death sentence anyway to save his career. Behind him stands an entire city of 1930s Muscovites who have learned, out of fear, to say what they don't believe.
  • Evil serves a larger order. Woland, Bulgakov's devil, doesn't lure anyone to damnation. He's a cold-eyed auditor who punishes the greedy, the two-faced and the time-servers and rewards love and nerve. That's the sense of the epigraph, taken from Mephistopheles in Goethe's Faust: "I am part of that power which eternally wills evil and eternally works good."
  • Art and love outlast tyranny. "Manuscripts don't burn." A writer who was censored, mocked and driven to madness is rescued, along with the woman who turned witch for him, and the novel he threw in the stove outlives everyone who attacked it.

Put together: in a society that claims to have done away with God, the devil and accident alike, Bulgakov brings the devil back as judge, and uses a trial held two thousand years ago to try his own age, and the nerve of every reader.

Three layers: a Moscow farce, a Jerusalem tragedy and a devil's tribunal, with one theme played in three registers.

1. Woland: the auditor who "wills evil and works good"

The novel opens at Patriarch's Ponds. Berlioz, editor of a literary journal, is lecturing the young poet Ivan Bezdomny (the pen name means "Homeless") on why Jesus never existed and the whole story is myth. A stranger, claiming to be a foreign professor of black magic, joins in. If there's no God, he asks, who runs a man's life? Man himself, says Berlioz. The stranger laughs: a man can't even vouch for his own evening, because "yes, man is mortal, but that would be only half the trouble. The worst of it is that he's sometimes suddenly mortal" (paraphrased). Then he predicts that Berlioz will have his head cut off, because "Annushka has already bought the sunflower oil, and not only bought it, she's spilled it." Minutes later Berlioz slips in the oil on the tram tracks and a tram takes off his head.

The stranger is Satan, going by Woland, a name borrowed from one of the devil's aliases in Goethe's Faust. He travels with a retinue: the lanky Koroviev in his cracked pince-nez, the fanged enforcer Azazello, the naked vampire Hella, and the readers' favourite, Behemoth, a giant black tomcat who walks upright, drinks vodka and carries a pistol (his name comes from the monstrous beast in the Book of Job).

The key point is that this devil hardly tempts anyone into sin at all. What he does is expose the sin already there. At a "black magic séance" in the Variety Theatre his crew makes banknotes rain from the ceiling and the audience brawls for them. Then they open a free Paris boutique on stage, and the women of Moscow strip off their old clothes for new gowns. Afterwards the money turns into mineral-water labels and the gowns vanish in the street, leaving crowds of women standing on the pavement in their underwear. A grasping house-committee chairman pockets a bribe in roubles, the roubles turn into dollars, and he's hauled off for hoarding foreign currency. Woland simply gives each person room to act on their own nature.

Why does this matter? It flips a familiar moral setup. Traditionally the devil tempts and the human is his victim. In Bulgakov the devil is a mirror with a penalty notice attached: he doesn't manufacture evil, he leaves it nowhere to hide. That's how the epigraph cashes out. In a society where every normal channel of judgement has broken down, the only one left who can drag the hypocrites into daylight is the devil.

How it changes the way you see: a lot of bad behaviour isn't taught by a corrupter; a situation simply lets out what was already there. The next time you watch respectable people lose their composure en masse under some incentive (panic-buying, taking sides, informing), don't hunt first for whoever "led them astray". Ask what the situation was that pushed them onto the stage.

2. Cowardice, the worst vice: Pilate's moment

In chapter two the novel jumps back to first-century Jerusalem, and the register drops from farce into cold realism. Pontius Pilate, procurator of Judea, is half-blind with a migraine as he interrogates a wandering philosopher named Yeshua Ha-Notsri. This is Bulgakov's Jesus, deliberately stripped of miracle: no crowd of disciples, only one, Levi Matvei, a former tax collector who follows him around getting his words wrong; no Son of God, just a frail young man who believes "there are no evil people in the world."

Mid-interrogation Yeshua looks at Pilate and says, in effect, that the truth is that your head hurts so badly you're thinking about death. With that, the headache lifts. Pilate is drawn to the man and wants to save him. Then a second charge surfaces. Yeshua had said that all authority is violence against people, and that a time will come when there is no power of the Caesars, nor any other power (paraphrased). That is lèse-majesté. Pilate knows the man is innocent, and he has the authority to free him. But release a man who insulted Caesar and denunciations will fly to the Emperor Tiberius in Rome, and his career, maybe his life, is finished. In his heart he has already acquitted the man; out loud he pronounces death.

Later Pilate is told that Yeshua, before he died, named cowardice as one of the chief human vices (paraphrased). In a dream Pilate argues back: no, philosopher, it is the worst. He has Judas, the man who betrayed Yeshua, quietly murdered by way of amends, and it amends nothing. By the end of the novel he has been sitting on a stone with only his dog for company for close to two thousand years, dreaming the same dream at every full moon and longing to go back to that morning and do it over.

Why put cowardice at the moral centre? Because it was the disease Bulgakov watched spread around him. Most people in 1930s Moscow weren't villains. They were intelligent people who knew the truth and chose to stay quiet or play along: the colleague who raised his hand at the denunciation meeting, the editor who knew a manuscript was good and didn't dare run it, the tenant who watched a neighbour taken away and said nothing. Pilate isn't a tyrant. He's cultivated, perceptive, even compassionate, and that's exactly what makes his sin glare. What beats him isn't evil; it's self-preservation.

How it changes the way you see: we tend to picture evil as something active (hurting, lying, hitting) and rarely count "could have, and didn't" as evil at all. In Bulgakov's moral ledger, the people nodding along in the meeting room sit on the same page as the man with the axe. Yet the book also gives Pilate its deepest pity. Cowardice isn't a monster's sin; it's one any ordinary person can commit on any given day.

3. "Manuscripts don't burn": art, truth and memory

The Master of the title has no name. A historian at a museum, he wins a lottery, quits his job and writes a novel about Pilate in a basement flat. Editors reject it and a pack of critics savage it in the papers, branding him a peddler of "Pilatism". Terrified, he breaks down, throws the manuscript into the stove and retreats into a psychiatric clinic, giving up even his name. There he meets Ivan, the poet locked up for claiming he'd seen the devil.

Later, at Margarita's request, Woland asks to read the novel. The Master says he burned it. Woland answers with the book's most famous line: "Manuscripts don't burn." Behemoth hops off his chair, and the cat has been sitting on a stack of manuscript, intact.

The line's weight comes from Bulgakov's own life. In 1930 he wrote to the Soviet government that he had, with his own hands, thrown the draft of a novel about the devil into the stove; that was an early version of this book. Then he wrote it all over again. It appeared, cut, more than twenty-five years after his death, and before that, like so much banned literature, it lived on in typescripts passed hand to hand. Bulgakov proved the line with a novel that was burned and rewritten.

The claim isn't about magic; it's a judgement about truth. Power can destroy paper and destroy authors, but it can't destroy a truth once it has been clearly thought and written down. It regrows in other people's memories, in carbon copies, in the next generation of readers. That's why the novel nests its stories: the Master writes the Pilate story, Ivan later dreams its continuation, and Woland claims he was there in person. The same truth surfaces from different mouths, and no censor can stop all of them.

How it changes the way you see: writing something down is itself an act of resistance. Thinking a thing through and putting it clearly on paper isn't wasted effort. It outlives both the applause and the denunciations of the moment.

4. "Second-grade fresh" sturgeon: a society pretending all is well

The Moscow strand is an extended satire, and its targets are specific. Members of the writers' union, here called MASSOLIT, eat and drink at the Griboyedov House restaurant and talk about who will get a dacha or a voucher to a sanatorium, never about literature. The theatre directors, the treasurers and the house managers all lie and wangle. What they share is this: everyone knows things are wrong, and everyone pretends things are fine.

The sharpest single image is Woland's exchange with the theatre's buffet manager. The man has come to complain that the magic money turned to paper and cost him; Woland turns instead to the sturgeon he sells. Your fish isn't fresh, he says. The manager protests that it's "second-grade fresh." Woland: "There's only one grade of freshness, the first, and it is also the last. If sturgeon is second-grade fresh, that means it's rotten!" (paraphrased)

The joke is the key to the whole satire. A society that keeps up appearances through euphemism invents phrases like "second-grade fresh", "temporary difficulties" and "isolated incidents" to pass off rot as a lesser kind of freshness. Watching the theatre crowd scramble for banknotes, Woland sums them up: people like any other, they love money, but that has always been so; sometimes mercy knocks at their hearts... "only the housing question has spoiled them" (paraphrased). Housing in Moscow was desperately short, and people denounced neighbours to get a room. In the novel the Master's "friend" Aloysius Mogarych informs on him precisely to take over his basement flat.

How it changes the way you see: when you hear a "second-grade fresh" sort of phrase, that's the moment to get suspicious. Verbal compromise is often a sign that reality has already gone off, and a person's or an organisation's capacity for truth comes down to whether they'll say "this fish stinks."

5. Margarita: courage in the name of love

The second half belongs to Margarita. Married to a decent, wealthy, kind specialist, she leads a life others envy and feels hollow inside. One day she walks down the street carrying a bunch of yellow flowers and meets the Master. In his words, love leapt out in front of them like a murderer jumping out of the ground in an alley and struck them both at once (paraphrased). After the Master disappears she spends a winter as a sleepwalker. Then Azazello finds her: agree to one task and you'll see him again.

She agrees. She rubs on the devil's cream, becomes a witch, flies naked on a broom over Moscow and smashes everything in the flat of the critic who destroyed the Master. Then she serves as hostess at Satan's Great Ball of the spring full moon, standing all night at the top of a staircase while history's poisoners, murderers and informers file past to kneel and kiss her knee. She does all of it for one thing: to get the Master back.

The most moving moment comes after the ball, when Woland lets her make a wish. She doesn't ask for the Master first. She thinks of a woman from the ball named Frieda, who once smothered her baby with a handkerchief and every morning since has woken to find that handkerchief by her bed. Margarita had carelessly given her hope during the ball, so now she asks that Frieda no longer be given the handkerchief; having raised someone's hopes, she won't break her word. Woland says mercy isn't his department and has her announce it herself, so she tells Frieda that she is forgiven. Only afterwards, granted another wish, does she get the Master back. And just before she spoke, Woland had told her: "Never ask for anything, especially from those who are stronger than you. They'll make the offer themselves, and give you everything themselves." (paraphrased)

Why does this matter? Margarita is Pilate's inverse. Pilate has power and judgement and, at the crucial moment, chooses to save himself. Margarita has nothing, yet risks everything for love, and before she gets what she wants most, she spends her wish on a stranger. Courage and mercy are the cure for cowardice.

How it changes the way you see: courage doesn't always look heroic. It might be a woman who refuses to accept that her lover has simply vanished, or someone who keeps a promise to another person before claiming her own reward.

6. Light and peace: a ladder of mercy

The ending is a carefully built theological puzzle. Levi Matvei, Yeshua's disciple, comes to Woland with a request: Yeshua has read the Master's novel and asks Woland to take the Master and grant him peace. Then why not take him into the light, Woland asks. Levi replies: "He has not earned the light. He has earned peace."

That line divides the cosmos into three tiers. There's light, Yeshua's realm, for those who held to the truth without reserve. There's peace, an eternal little home with cherry trees, Schubert in the evenings and the beloved beside you, for those who made something true and yet gave up out of fear. And there's the darkness that Woland governs. The Master burned his manuscript and hid in an asylum; he was a coward once, so he can't enter the light. But he wrote the truth, so he has earned peace.

In the same exchange Woland turns on Levi: what would your good be doing if there were no evil, and what would the earth look like if all the shadows disappeared from it? (paraphrased) Light and shadow aren't simply enemies here. Each depends on the other.

In the last scene the Master is given a privilege: he can end the suffering of the character he created. He shouts to Pilate, who has sat on his stone for two thousand years: "Free! He's waiting for you!" Pilate walks up a road made of moonlight to resume the conversation he never finished with Yeshua. A novel about cowardice ends in forgiveness, granted by an author who was himself once a coward.

How it changes the way you see: morality isn't two boxes, black and white; it's a staircase. A person can be flawed, can have been afraid at a decisive moment, and still deserve some kind of peace if they also did one true thing. And real forgiveness often has to come from someone who has failed in the same way.

The Skeleton

The whole book on one line: a society that claims to have everything under control, with no God and no devil, is in fact held together by fear and lies. The devil arrives and shakes out its greed and hypocrisy (the Moscow strand). The root of that fear was laid down two thousand years ago in one official's moment of flinching (the Jerusalem strand). Only two things can stand against it: art that writes the truth, and love that counts no cost (the Master-and-Margarita strand). And after the judgement comes mercy.

How does it make its case? Through structure. The three strands mirror one another. Berlioz denies Jesus existed while the Master's novel tells Jesus's story. Pilate kills Yeshua for fear of informers, and Muscovites inform on neighbours for a room. The Master burns his novel and the devil hands it back intact. One moral question recurs across three times and places until readers reach the conclusion on their own: cowardice isn't the disease of one era but of humanity, and in every era someone has taken the other road.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

  • Misreading 1: "It's an anti-Soviet allegory, and Woland is Stalin." Some critics have read it that way: Woland's absolute power and his crew's talent for making people vanish do evoke the secret police of the time. But that flattens a many-layered novel into a decoding game. The satire is aimed at Soviet society, yes, but the core questions (cowardice, art, mercy) outlast any regime, and the Jerusalem chapters carry far more weight than "using the past to mock the present" can account for.
  • Misreading 2: "Bulgakov is siding with the devil." Woland is magnetic, and fairer than most of the humans in the book, but he never takes Yeshua's place. It's Yeshua who decides the Master's fate; Woland only carries it out. The order of the book puts light on top, with darkness as the necessary shadow. Calling it a Satanist book means reading only the fireworks.
  • Misreading 3: "The Jerusalem chapters are Bulgakov's gospel." Strictly speaking they are a novel written by a character, the Master, delivered to us through Woland's telling and Ivan's dreams. Their departures from the canonical Gospels (no resurrection, a single disciple, a Yeshua who says he doesn't remember his parents) are deliberate, and the nesting keeps the author at one remove.
  • The religious objection: Some Orthodox readers hold that the book turns Christ into a weak mortal and makes Satan likeable, which is theologically dangerous. Others in the Church, notably the deacon Andrei Kuraev, have defended it from the opposite angle: precisely because the Jerusalem chapters are the Master's work, and the Master does not enter the light, the novel itself does not endorse that "gospel told from the devil's side", and readers should separate Bulgakov from his fictional author. The argument is still going.
  • It's an unfinished book. Bulgakov was still revising when he died in 1940, and the second half has loose ends and contradictions. The standard text was assembled by his widow, Elena Sergeevna, widely taken to be the model for Margarita, and editions differ.
  • A serious criticism: the satire punches down. Woland punishes theatre managers, house committees and petty editors; the state machinery that actually manufactured the fear appears only as hints (people who "disappear", plainclothes men at the door) and is never put on trial. Some see this as the only way to write under censorship; others think it blunts the satire. Some readers also find the ending's "peace" a retreat: the Master doesn't fight but withdraws into a private cottage forever.

Crossovers

  • Physics · the black hole information paradox: Quantum unitarity says information can't truly be destroyed. Leonard Susskind's favourite example is a burning book, whose information in principle survives in the ash and smoke. Hawking argued black holes erase information and publicly conceded in 2004. Like "manuscripts don't burn"; unlike it, physics promises only recovery in principle, scrambled beyond reading, while Woland hands back a clean copy.
  • Psychology · Milgram's obedience experiments: In the 1960s most ordinary volunteers, prodded by a man in a lab coat, kept raising the "shocks" to the top of the scale. Like Pilate, conscience was present and lost to pressure from above. Unlike Pilate, they faced a direct order; he held power himself and folded at merely imagining a higher power's reaction.
  • Religious studies · the Satan of the Book of Job: In Hebrew, ha-satan means "the accuser", an officer of God's court who tests and prosecutes, not God's rival. Woland plays exactly this part, an auditor working inside a higher order. Unlike Job's dutiful prosecutor, though, Woland has taste, irony and mercy.
  • Jungian psychology · the shadow: Jung called the repressed, disowned part of the personality the shadow and warned that if you don't integrate it, it erupts elsewhere. Woland's "what would the earth look like without shadows" shares that structure. The difference: Jung's shadow lives inside the psyche, while Woland's is an order of the cosmos.
  • Distributed systems · replication: Data stored in one place dies with a single failure; keep several replicas and losing any one machine doesn't matter. Soviet samizdat, banned texts retyped and passed hand to hand, kept forbidden books alive the same way. Unlike machine replicas, hand copies drift a little with every pass.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. The worst of vices is cowardice. Pilate knew Yeshua was innocent and signed the sentence to save himself.

2. The devil doesn't make people bad; he gives each of them room to show what they already are.

3. "Part of that power which eternally wills evil and eternally works good": where judgement has failed, it takes the devil to unmask the hypocrites.

4. Man is mortal, which is only half the trouble; the worst of it is that he's sometimes suddenly mortal. So don't claim everything is under control.

5. Manuscripts don't burn: a truth clearly thought and written down outlives those who condemn it.

6. There's only one grade of freshness; "second-grade fresh" sturgeon is rotten sturgeon.

7. They're people like any other; only the housing question has spoiled them.

8. Margarita is Pilate's inverse: she has nothing, risks everything for love, and before she gets her wish she spends it on a stranger.

9. He has not earned the light; he has earned peace. Morality isn't two boxes but a staircase.

10. After judgement comes mercy: an author who was once a coward sets free the cowardly procurator he created.

Deep Reading Daily · You now have the bones of The Master and Margarita: its three-storey structure, a Woland who wills evil and works good, Pilate's cowardice, the manuscript that won't burn, Moscow's "second-grade fresh" society, Margarita's courage, and the ladder of mercy between light and peace. The rest of the magic belongs to a black cat who mends a primus stove with a pistol at his side, and he's worth meeting in person.
