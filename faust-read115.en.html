Faust

Johann Wolfgang von Goethe · Part One 1808 / Part Two 1832

中文 →

In One Sentence

A scholar who has mastered every branch of learning and still feels empty makes a bet with the devil: the moment I say to any passing instant, "Stay a while — you are so beautiful," my soul is yours. He then spends a lifetime proving he will never be satisfied. Out of that wager Goethe built a portrait of the modern condition: ceaseless striving is both our dignity and the source of our worst damage; we go astray because we strive, and only striving can save us.

Where It Sits

The historical Faust was a disreputable sixteenth-century German conjurer who, after his death, became the folk-tale doctor who sold his soul to the devil; Christopher Marlowe's English version ends with him dragged off to hell. Goethe worked on the material for nearly sixty years, from his twenties until months before his death at eighty-two, turning a cautionary tale into a verse drama about whether modern people can find meaning for desire that has no natural limit. Its place in German letters is roughly Dante's place in Italian.

The Core Argument

  • Human beings are defined by striving (Streben), and striving entails error. The Lord sets the tone in the Prologue in Heaven: "Man errs as long as he strives" (paraphrased). Error is not proof that striving has failed; it is the price of striving at all.
  • The real stake is not pleasure but satisfaction. Faust doesn't trade his soul for a bag of goodies. He bets that nothing the devil gives him will ever make him stop. What he dreads is not hell but "enough." It is a new, distinctly modern curse: always being on the way.
  • The power of negation gets conscripted into creation. Mephistopheles describes himself as "part of that power which always wills evil and always produces good" (paraphrased). In Goethe's world, destruction and appetite are folded into a larger movement — but the price of that "dialectic" is the innocent people crushed along the way: Gretchen, her child, the old couple Philemon and Baucis.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Two wagers: one in heaven, one in the study

What it is. The play actually contains two bets. The first is in the Prologue in Heaven. Mephistopheles tells the Lord that humans are pitiful creatures who use their reason only to become "more bestial than any beast," and asks permission to tempt Faust. The Lord agrees: as long as a man strives he will err, but a good man, even in his dark impulses, remains aware of the right path. The structure is lifted straight from the Book of Job, where Satan wagers that Job will abandon his faith.

The second bet is the one Faust himself makes in his study. Mephistopheles will serve him on earth and grant every wish, on one condition:

"If ever I say to the passing moment, 'Stay a while — you are so beautiful!' (Verweile doch! du bist so schön!), then you may put me in chains, and I will gladly perish." (paraphrased)

An analogy. The ordinary soul-selling contract is a loan: you get twenty-four years of glory up front and repay it in hell when the term expires. That's Marlowe's Faustus. Goethe's Faust signs something closer to a performance clause. The devil doesn't win on a deadline; he wins on a state of mind — the day I'm fed, content, and ready to lie down, you've got me. Faust is effectively saying: nothing you offer will work, because I have no "enough" switch.

Why it matters. That one change moves the moral center of the story from "desire is sin" to "satisfaction is death." In the medieval picture, a person has a proper harbor — God, vocation, station — and going beyond it is transgression. In Faust's wager, dropping anchor is the failure. That is the psychological architecture of modernity: growth, progress, self-realization, always a next thing.

How it changes your view. You start to notice that a lot of modern anxiety isn't about lacking something; it's about refusing to let yourself stay a while. Promoted, and already eyeing the next rung; back from a trip, and already planning the next. Faust shows that this restlessness is both an engine (it drives a lifetime of ventures) and a curse (it means he is never fully present anywhere).

2. "Two souls dwell in my breast": the Faustian split

What it is. On an Easter walk outside the city gate, Faust delivers one of the play's most famous lines: "Two souls, alas, dwell in my breast, each longing to break away from the other: one clings to the world with lusty, clutching organs; the other rises from the dust toward the realm of lofty ancestors" (paraphrased). One wants the here and now — body, pleasure, people. The other wants the beyond — truth, spirit, the infinite.

An analogy. Picture someone tied to two ropes pulling in opposite directions; one rope is called living, the other understanding. The opening monologue is the moment the second rope snaps. He has studied philosophy, law, medicine, "and theology too, alas" (paraphrased), only to conclude that we can know nothing. He raises a cup of poison to his lips, and it is the Easter bells and memories of childhood that pull him back. Then the devil arrives offering the other rope: stop trying to know; go and experience.

Why it matters. This is what later came to be called the "Faustian" temperament: refusing human limits, wanting both knowledge and experience, both infinity and this very moment. The German philosopher of history Oswald Spengler, in The Decline of the West, went so far as to label all of Western civilization "Faustian" — a culture driven toward infinite space and endless expansion — in contrast to the "Apollonian" culture of ancient Greece, which prized the bounded, perfected form. The thesis is contested, but it shows how readily the play became the West's self-portrait.

How it changes your view. Wanting it all is not a character flaw; it is the basic modern predicament. You don't have to pick a side, but you should know both pulls are always there — most of life's worst mistakes happen when one rope suddenly overpowers the other, as when Faust lets appetite swamp everything with Gretchen.

3. Mephistopheles: "the spirit that always negates"

What it is. When Mephistopheles first appears he introduces himself as "part of that power which always wills evil and always works good," and adds, "I am the spirit that always negates!" (paraphrased). He is not Christianity's terrifying Satan but a witty, cutting, worldly realist, and his lines are often the funniest and most clear-eyed in the play. The most quoted comes while he poses as Faust to advise a student: "Gray, dear friend, is all theory, and green the golden tree of life." (paraphrased)

An analogy. Think of him as a professional contrarian, a machine that only knows how to say no. He builds nothing, but he demolishes every reason you have for stopping: you call love sacred, he calls it lust; you call scholarship noble, he calls it gray paper. He works like gunpowder — meant for destruction, and turned to production the moment someone uses it to blast a mine.

Why it matters. This is Goethe's deepest move about evil. Evil is not a rival substance set against the good; it is negativity — the force that dismantles, doubts, mocks. The Lord says so in the Prologue: human activity slackens too easily, people love unconditional rest, so I'm glad to give them a companion who "stirs and works" them (paraphrased). The devil is a component in God's economy: without negation, nothing moves. Hegel would later make "the negative" the motor of history, a line of thought very close to this one.

How it changes your view. You learn to tell two kinds of "bad" apart: pure destruction, and the negation that forces you out of your comfort. Criticism, competition, crisis, failure often play Mephistopheles' role. But there is a shadow here: "evil ends up producing good" is a perfect alibi for evildoers — and Mephistopheles loves that line more than anyone. Goethe's achievement is to show you both its truth and its cold-bloodedness at once.

4. "In the beginning was the Deed": from Word to action

What it is. In his study, Faust sets out to translate the first line of John's Gospel, "In the beginning was the Word" (Im Anfang war das Wort). "Word" won't do. He tries "Mind" (Sinn), then "Force" (Kraft), and finally writes: "In the beginning was the Deed!" (Im Anfang war die Tat). At that very moment the black poodle that followed him home grows restless and turns into Mephistopheles.

An analogy. It's like a scholar swapping the motto "I think, therefore I am" for "I act, therefore I am." The four renderings form a ladder — language, meaning, energy, action — each rung farther from the book and closer to the world. The devil appears precisely as the word "Deed" hits the page, and that's no accident: action is the road to Faust's salvation and the road to his crimes.

Why it matters. The scene compresses the play's whole turn from the contemplative life to the active one. All of Part Two — issuing paper money at court, waging war, reclaiming land from the sea — is "the Deed" unfolding. Goethe is rehearsing modern society's value system: a person is measured by what he gets done.

How it changes your view. Next time someone says "stop overthinking and just ship it," remember this scene. Action really can break the paralysis of thought, but action carries no direction of its own — every one of Faust's later crimes is also a "deed."

5. The Gretchen tragedy: the cost in the small world

What it is. The second half of Part One is a heartbreaking love story. Mephistopheles takes Faust to a witch's kitchen for a rejuvenating potion, and Faust falls for Gretchen (formally Margarete), a devout, unworldly small-town girl. He wins her with jewels and the devil's arrangements. To meet him at night, she gives her mother a sleeping draught Faust supplies — and the mother dies of it. Her brother Valentin challenges Faust over the family's honor and is run through by Faust (with Mephistopheles' help), cursing his sister as a whore in the street as he dies. Gretchen, pregnant and shunned, drowns her newborn in despair and is condemned to death. Faust breaks into her cell to rescue her; she refuses and gives herself to God's judgment. "She is judged!" says Mephistopheles. A voice from above answers: "She is saved!" (paraphrased)

An analogy. Think of Faust as a founder who has just been handed unlimited resources and decides to try his hand in a small town. He only wants a lovely romance; he has no intention of hurting anyone. But his power is wildly out of scale with the town's fragile structures — every "gift" he brings (jewels, sleeping draught, the devil's sword) detonates in a world too small to absorb it. His striving is infinite, her world is finite, and the entire cost falls on the finite side.

Why it matters. This is the most human part of the play and the harshest verdict on Faustian striving: infinite desire, once it lands on a particular person, becomes plunder. Goethe was also writing about a real eighteenth-century social problem. In 1772 Frankfurt tried and executed an unmarried servant, Susanna Margaretha Brandt, for infanticide, and the young Goethe very likely knew the case files. Gretchen is not an allegory; she is the casualty of a real legal and moral order.

How it changes your view. You grow warier of the self-justification "I'm only pursuing what I want." The more power you have, the more your pursuits land on other people. Faust never meant to kill anyone, yet Gretchen's mother, brother, and child lie along his path.

6. The "great world" of Part Two: paper money, Helen, and the reclaimed coast

What it is. Part One is the "small world" (one man, a love affair, a town); Part Two is the "great world" (state, economy, history, classical myth). A few pivotal episodes:

  • Paper money. The Holy Roman Emperor's treasury is empty, so Mephistopheles proposes issuing paper notes backed by treasure still buried underground. Overnight the realm feels "rich," and everyone goes on a spending spree. The Swiss economist Hans Christoph Binswanger, in Money and Magic, reads the scene as a parable of modern credit money: money is no longer gold dug from the earth but a promise about the future that can simply be printed — the alchemy at the heart of the modern economy.
  • Helen. Faust descends to "the Mothers," a mysterious realm of archetypes, to summon Helen of Troy, later unites with her, and fathers Euphorion, a boy who keeps leaping higher until he crashes to his death — a figure Goethe openly linked to Lord Byron, who died in 1824 in the Greek War of Independence. The episode says that the modern Faustian spirit longs to embrace classical, perfected beauty, but the union is brief and fragile.
  • The reclaimed coast. The aged Faust receives a strip of shoreline from the Emperor and sets out to dike it and drain it, opening free land for millions. In the way stand an old couple, Philemon and Baucis (named after the kindly, hospitable pair in Ovid's Metamorphoses), living on a dune. Faust tells Mephistopheles to relocate them. Mephistopheles and his men burn the cottage, killing the couple and a guest.

An analogy. The American critic Marshall Berman, in All That Is Solid Melts Into Air (1982), gave the famous reading: Faust is the first "Developer." He evolves from dreamer (the opening) to lover (Gretchen) to the moderniser of mega-projects — building dikes, developing land, clearing whatever stands in the way in the name of progress. Philemon and Baucis are the holdouts bulldozed for the project.

Why it matters. Part Two scales "Faustian" up from private psychology to the pattern of modern civilization: an economy floated on printed promises, politics run on engineering and mobilization, a progress that demands the destruction of the old world in its path. Faust never kills anyone with his own hands; he gives an order and then regrets the outcome — the most common form of crime under modern power.

How it changes your view. When you hear about a project, a growth plan, or a reform "for the greater good," you'll ask one more question: who are this time's Philemon and Baucis?

7. "Stay a while": Faust's death and the riddle of his salvation

What it is. At the end, the hundred-year-old Faust is blinded by Care (Sorge), who appears as a gray woman. He hears spades and assumes the laborers are carrying out his land project; in fact they are Lemures, spectral gravediggers summoned by Mephistopheles to dig his grave. Rapt in a vision of the future — standing on free soil among a free people — he says: "Then to the moment I might say: Stay a while, you are so beautiful!" (paraphrased). Note the subjunctive: "then I might say." And he falls dead.

Mephistopheles thinks he has won and moves to collect the soul. But angels arrive scattering roses and singing, and while he is distracted — smitten by the angels' beauty — they carry Faust's soul upward. Their song contains one of the play's keys: "Whoever strives with ceaseless effort, him we can redeem." (Wer immer strebend sich bemüht, den können wir erlösen; paraphrased). In the final scene, among saints and penitent women — Gretchen among them, interceding for him — a Chorus Mysticus closes the play: "The Eternal Feminine draws us upward." (Das Ewig-Weibliche zieht uns hinan)

An analogy. It's like a disputed call at the end of a match. The devil says, "He said the words, I win." The angels say, "He said 'someday,' and he never stopped striving." The letter of the wager may have been met, but in spirit Faust was never content with the present — his only satisfaction is with a future that doesn't yet exist, which is exactly the proof that he was striving to the last breath.

Why it matters. The ending refuses both easy answers. It doesn't punish the transgressor as Marlowe did, and it doesn't declare Faust a good man. He is saved not for doing right but because striving itself is made a condition of redemption, joined by "love from above" (the other half of what the angels sing). Salvation is grace, not reward.

How it changes your view. It delivers a verdict on modern people that is comforting and unsettling at once: you will err, perhaps gravely; your worth lies not in never erring but in never surrendering to "enough." That same forgiveness has enraged many readers — see below.

The Skeleton

Compressed into one line of argument, the twelve-thousand-plus verses run like this:

(1) Premise: human nature is striving, and striving errs (Prologue in Heaven). (2) Crisis: the pursuit of pure knowledge dead-ends in nothingness (the study). (3) Wager: Faust turns to experience and bets he will never be satisfied. (4) Small world: infinite desire destroys a finite person (Gretchen). (5) Great world: the same desire scales up into economics (paper money), aesthetics (Helen), and engineering (the coast), and the costs scale with it. (6) Ending: Faust dies imagining the future and is saved by "ceaseless striving" plus "love from above."

What the play establishes is not a moral lesson but a diagnosis of modern people: our dignity comes from never resting, and so do our crimes. Goethe offers no prescription to "just stop"; he makes you see both truths in a single life. He does it not by argument but through a "world drama" that uses every form at hand — lyric, myth, farce, opera, allegory — to stage one contradiction across a lifetime.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "Faust sells his soul for pleasure." That's Marlowe's and the chapbook's Faust. Goethe's turns on a bet about never being satisfied; pleasure is precisely what he scorns — he tells Mephistopheles that if pleasure can ever fool him, that day will be his last (paraphrased).

Misreading 2: "He's saved at the end, so he's the hero." No. He's saved by grace and by striving, not by merit. Goethe never makes him repent, and never erases his crimes. The play's ethics remain unresolved.

Misreading 3: "Only Part One is worth reading; Part Two is impenetrable." Part Two is genuinely hard, dense with classical myth and allegory; Goethe sealed the manuscript and left instructions that it be published only after his death. But without Part Two, Faust is just a man who ruined a girl. Part Two is what turns a private sin into a structural problem of modern civilization, and the major readings by Berman, Binswanger, and others rest on it.

Criticism 1: The salvation is too cheap. Many readers, and one strand of German criticism, refuse to accept it: why should a man who brought about the deaths of Gretchen's family and ordered the old couple "relocated" be carried off by angels? Some argue Goethe papered over the ethical problem with religious pageantry. Defenders reply that because salvation is grace rather than payment, it does not justify Faust's crimes. The argument has never been settled.

Criticism 2: Gretchen is fuel for the hero's ascent. Feminist critics point out that her tragedy ultimately serves Faust's "rising," and that "the Eternal Feminine" abstracts women into a spiritual force for redeeming men rather than people with their own fate. The critique bites: she bears the entire social penalty, and he loses nothing.

Criticism 3: "The Faustian spirit" was conscripted by nationalism. From the late nineteenth century through the Nazi era, "Faustian German spirit" was promoted as a symbol of national expansion and the supremacy of will. Many postwar German scholars called this a gross misreading — the play is deeply critical of Faust's expansion and violence. But the history shows how a work that celebrates never resting can easily be recruited to endorse any means necessary.

Goethe Beyond This Book

The Sorrows of Young Werther (1774): Faust's mirror image. This novel made the twenty-five-year-old Goethe famous overnight. Werther falls for Lotte, who is engaged to another man; his feelings have nowhere to go, and he kills himself. It is the signature work of Sturm und Drang ("Storm and Stress"), a movement that rebelled against Enlightenment cool-headedness in favor of intense personal feeling and untamed genius. Werther is the man swallowed by feeling until he stops; Faust is the man driven by striving until he can't stop — together they are Goethe's lifelong meditation on the two dangers of the self.

Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship (1795–96): growing up as self-formation. This novel set the template for the Bildungsroman, the "novel of formation," in which a young person becomes whole through mistakes and experience. It shares Faust's conviction that people are shaped by their errors, but gives a gentler answer: not infinite expansion but finding a place in society and accepting limits.

Goethe the scientist: morphology and color. Goethe poured years into natural inquiry. He argued that humans, too, have an intermaxillary bone (then considered a marker separating humans from apes), and proposed that the parts of a plant are transformations of the leaf — the "metamorphosis of plants," an approach of tracing variations from an archetype that influenced later comparative morphology. His Theory of Colours (1810) rejected Newton's account of white light as a spectrum of rays and insisted on studying color through human perception; as physics it was wrong, but its observations of color's psychological and physiological effects are still cited by artists and psychologists. This sharpens Faust: Goethe himself refused to stay with books and needed to touch the world — yet he trusted, more than Faust, that observation and form could let a person rest within limits.

The Book in Ten Sentences

1. Man errs as long as he strives (Prologue in Heaven, paraphrased) — error is the cost of striving, not proof of failure.

2. Faust's bet is not about pleasure but satisfaction: the day he tells a moment "Stay a while, you are so beautiful," he loses.

3. Two souls dwell in my breast: one clings to the earth, one longs for the heights (paraphrased).

4. Mephistopheles is part of the power that "always wills evil and always works good" — negation is the engine of movement and the evildoer's favorite alibi.

5. Gray is all theory, and green the golden tree of life (paraphrased — and spoken by the devil).

6. In the beginning was the Deed: the road from Word to action is Faust's road to salvation and to crime alike.

7. Gretchen's tragedy shows that infinite desire, landing on a finite person, becomes plunder.

8. Paper money, Helen, the reclaimed coast: Part Two scales one man's desire into modern civilization — credit, beauty, development — and each has its Philemon and Baucis.

9. Faust speaks the fatal words only about an imagined future: his one satisfaction is with what does not yet exist.

10. Whoever strives with ceaseless effort, him we can redeem; the Eternal Feminine draws us upward (paraphrased) — salvation is grace, not reward, and whether that grace is just, Goethe leaves to you.
