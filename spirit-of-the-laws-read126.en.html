The Spirit of the Laws

De l'esprit des lois · Montesquieu (Charles-Louis de Secondat, baron de Montesquieu) · 1748

中文 →

In One Sentence

Laws are not universal truths handed down by a sage; they are a web of relations that has to fit a people's form of government, climate, soil, customs, religion and livelihood — and the one relation that holds everywhere is that anyone with power will abuse it until they hit a limit, so power can only be stopped by power. Montesquieu spent some twenty years laying the world's known legal systems side by side to answer two questions: why laws differ from place to place, and what actually keeps people free.

Where It Sits

Montesquieu was a presiding judge in the Bordeaux parlement and a first-generation figure of the French Enlightenment, already famous for the satirical novel Persian Letters (1721) and his Considerations on the Causes of the Greatness of the Romans and Their Decline (1734). The Spirit of the Laws appeared anonymously in Geneva in 1748, in thirty-one books, and went onto the Catholic Index in 1751. It founded comparative politics and the sociology of law, and America's framers treated it as the oracle on separating powers — Madison calls Montesquieu exactly that in Federalist No. 47 (see Read 125).

The Core Claims

  • Laws are "the necessary relations deriving from the nature of things." Nations have different laws not because some are foolish but because their situations differ. Good laws are tailored laws: it would be a great coincidence if the laws of one nation suited another (Book 1, ch. 3, paraphrased).
  • Every regime has a "nature" and a "principle." Republics run on virtue, monarchies on honor, despotisms on fear. Laws must fit the regime's principle, and a regime's corruption always starts with the corruption of its principle.
  • Political liberty exists only under moderate government, and moderation is guaranteed only by structure: legislative, executive and judicial power must never sit in the same hands. Power has to be checked by another power.

Put together: there is no single best law for every country, but there is one rule that is bad in every country — concentrating power in one place.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. The "spirit" of the laws: a set of relations, not a list of commands

What it is. The book opens strangely: "Laws, in their most general signification, are the necessary relations arising from the nature of things." In this sense God has laws, the material world has laws, animals have laws, and so do humans. Montesquieu is deliberately recasting law from command into relation. Just as Newton's law of gravitation describes a necessary relation between two bodies, human law sits in a web: its relation to the form of government; to climate, soil and the size of the territory; to how people make a living (hunting, herding, farming, trade); to religion, population, mores and commerce. The sum of all these relations is what he calls the "spirit of the laws" — hence the title. He is not studying the text of any code but the invisible web behind it.

An analogy. A law is like a coat. Natural-law philosophers — who held that reason alone could discover one standard of justice valid everywhere — asked what the perfect coat looks like. Montesquieu asks who will wear it, in what weather, and for what work. A northern fur coat is a torture device in the tropics; tropical linen is a death sentence in Siberia. You can't judge the coat apart from the person wearing it.

Why it matters. This move pulled politics out of theology and moralizing and turned it into an empirical inquiry where you compare cases and look for causes. In the preface he writes that he first examined human beings and came to believe that, amid the endless diversity of laws and customs, they were not led by whim alone; once he had laid down his principles, he saw particular cases bend to them as if of their own accord (paraphrased). That is the basic stance of social science: regularities behind diversity. Durkheim later wrote his Latin doctoral thesis on Montesquieu, treating him as a forerunner of sociology.

How it changes the way you see. It hands you a useful reflex. Whenever someone says "that country's system works — just copy it," you ask: which relations does the system depend on back home, and do they exist here? The same statute is a shield in a society with a tradition of independent courts and just a piece of paper in one without.

2. A regime's "nature" and "principle": what makes the machine run

What it is. Montesquieu sorts regimes into three kinds, cutting differently from Aristotle: republics (a democracy when the whole people holds sovereign power, an aristocracy when only part of it does), monarchy (one person rules, but by fixed, established laws), and despotism (one person rules with no law or rule, by sheer will and caprice). What separates monarchy from despotism isn't the number of rulers but whether laws and "intermediate powers" stand between the ruler and the people.

Then comes the deeper distinction: a regime's nature is what makes it that kind of regime — who holds power and how; its principle is what sets it in motion — the human passion that drives it. The nature is the structure; the principle is the fuel.

Mechanism and examples. Why does democracy need virtue? In a monarchy, the law is enforced by the force of the prince. In a democracy, the people enforcing the law are themselves subject to it and bear its consequences; once citizens stop loving the community, there is no outside force to keep it going. In later editions Montesquieu added a note insisting that by "virtue" he meant political virtue — love of country and of equality — not Christian moral virtue, because too many readers took him to be saying that subjects of kings had no morals.

Monarchy runs on honor, and this is one of his sharpest insights. Honor is, philosophically, a "false" passion: nobles crave preferment, titles and distinctions — vanity, by any moral standard. Yet that vanity makes each person advance the public business while chasing private advantage, and it draws a line the king cannot cross: a nobleman will die for his king but refuse an order that would dishonor him. Honor is a soft constraint on royal power.

Despotism gets a single image: "When the savages of Louisiana want fruit, they cut the tree at the root and gather the fruit. That is despotic government." (Book 5, ch. 13). The despot grabs what he can today and destroys the source of tomorrow's yield; under the rule of fear, nobody dares plan ahead.

Why it matters / how it changes the way you see. The same legal text, driven by a different passion, becomes a different thing. A constitution that provides for elections, a legislature and courts is, if what actually moves people is fear, a despotism. Hence Book 8: "The corruption of every government almost always begins with that of its principles." When the principle is rotten, even good laws turn bad; while it holds, even bad laws can do good. When you diagnose a country, a company or a team, don't stop at the org chart. Ask: what is really pushing these people to act?

3. "Power must check power": political liberty and the separation of powers (Book 11)

First, what liberty is. No word, Montesquieu says, has been given more meanings than "liberty." His own definition is restrained: political liberty is not doing whatever you want but "the right to do everything the laws permit", because if one citizen could do what the laws forbid, everyone else could too, and nobody would be free. Going further, a citizen's political liberty is "that tranquility of mind which arises from the opinion each person has of his safety" (Book 11, ch. 6, paraphrased). The sign of freedom isn't how many votes you cast; it's whether you sleep without fearing a knock on the door.

The central claim. Then comes the book's most famous passage: "It is an eternal experience that every man who has power is inclined to abuse it; he goes on until he finds limits." Note: not "bad men," but every man who has power. Even virtue, he adds, needs limits. Therefore, "so that power cannot be abused, power must check power by the arrangement of things." The phrase that matters is "the arrangement of things" — not the ruler's goodwill, education or oath, but structure.

The three powers. Discussing England's constitution, he divides state power into the legislative, the executive (in matters of war and diplomacy), and the "power of judging." "When legislative power is united with executive power in a single person or a single body of magistracy, there is no liberty," because people will fear that the same monarch or senate will make tyrannical laws and then execute them tyrannically. Nor is there liberty if the power of judging is not separated: joined to the legislature, the judge becomes legislator and power over citizens' lives becomes arbitrary; joined to the executive, the judge gains the force of an oppressor. Put all three in one set of hands and "all would be lost."

Two details people miss. First, the judging power is "in some fashion, null": judges should be no more than "the mouth that pronounces the words of the law," with no will of their own. He even wants trials by people drawn temporarily from the citizenry, so that "one fears the office, and not the magistrates." Second, separation doesn't mean three sealed-off domains; it means mutual checks. The executive holds a "faculty of preventing" (faculté d'empêcher — a veto) over legislation, the legislature can examine how its laws are executed, and of the two chambers one represents commoners and the other nobles. The three powers ought to settle into stillness, but because things must move, they are forced to move in concert (paraphrased).

Why it matters / how it changes the way you see. It turns liberty from a philosophical ideal into an engineering problem. The question stops being "who should rule?" and becomes "whoever rules, where will they be stopped?" Splitting the board chair from the CEO and keeping auditors independent are the same idea. It also gives you a clear warning light: when one person or body writes the rules, enforces them and settles disputes about them, however enlightened they are today, your freedom depends on their mood.

4. Intermediate powers and moderate government: why nobles, courts and town charters guard freedom

What it is. In his account of monarchy Montesquieu introduces an often-overlooked idea: "intermediate, subordinate and dependent powers" — the nobility, the clergy, the privileges of towns, and above all "depositories of the laws" like the parlements. Wedged between king and people, they work like channels through which royal power must flow along fixed courses. "No monarch, no nobility; no nobility, no monarch," he writes — otherwise you have a despot. The parlements preserve the laws, remonstrate when new ones are issued, and remind the king when old ones are forgotten.

The mechanism. Why should things that look like privilege and feudal leftovers protect freedom? Because the essence of despotism is that nothing stands between the ruler and the isolated individual. When everyone is a grain of sand, the ruler can mold them at will. When society is layered with bodies that have their own interests and charters, every step the ruler takes runs into organized resistance. Abolish the prerogatives of lords, clergy, nobility and towns in a monarchy, he warns, and you will soon have either a popular state or a despotic one.

Moderation is his highest value. The good regime is not a particular type but a moderate one — republic or monarchy — in which power is dispersed, restrained, and bound by law and intermediate bodies. Near the end of the book he writes: "I say it, and it seems to me that I have written this work only to prove it: the spirit of moderation should be that of the legislator." (Book 29, ch. 1). And: "Political good, like moral good, is always found between two limits."

How it changes the way you see. Tocqueville picked this up and turned it into his praise of American associations; later it became the core of civil-society theory. A society reduced to "the state" and atomized individuals is the one most likely to slide into despotism, even when it starts from equality. Guilds, associations, local self-government and independent media are slow, often conservative, sometimes self-serving — and they are the riverbanks that keep power in its channel.

5. Climate and the "general spirit": laws should follow the land, but legislators should fight its vices

What it is. This is the book's most famous and most mocked part (Books 14–18). Montesquieu holds that climate acts on the body and through it on temperament: cold tightens the body's fibers, making people stronger, braver and less sensitive to pleasure; heat relaxes them, making people more sensitive, more indolent, more ruled by passion. He even ran an experiment: he examined a sheep's tongue under a microscope, froze it and watched the little papillae retract, then thawed it and watched them reappear — and concluded that people in cold countries feel pleasure less keenly (Book 14, ch. 2). From there he argues that hot lands lean toward despotism and slavery and cold ones toward liberty; that Asia's vast plains breed great empires and despotism while Europe, cut by mountains and rivers into mid-sized states, lets the rule of law take root; that islanders are freer than continental peoples.

But the next sentence is the key. He doesn't counsel resignation. "Bad legislators are those who have favored the vices of the climate, and good ones are those who have opposed them" (Book 14, ch. 5). In a hot country that inclines people to idleness, he argues, an ideal of contemplative repose — his example is Buddhism — feeds the idleness, and good legislators should use law to encourage labor. Climate is a headwind, not a verdict.

The "general spirit" is where he really lands. In Book 19 he widens the lens: "Many things govern men: climate, religion, laws, the maxims of government, examples of past things, mores, and manners; from all these a general spirit is formed." Where one cause acts strongly, the others weaken: nature and climate rule savages almost alone, manners govern the Chinese, laws tyrannize Japan, mores once set the tone in Sparta, maxims of government and ancient customs did so in Rome. From this follows a very practical rule: what should be changed by manners must not be changed by laws, and vice versa. When Peter the Great used decrees to make Russians cut their beards and shorten their coats, that was using law to change manners — which is tyranny; setting an example and introducing other manners would have worked better (paraphrased).

Why it matters / how it changes the way you see. Strip away the dated physiology of the sheep's tongue and what's left is a very modern idea: institutions are embedded in culture, culture in ecology, and each shapes the others. The reformer's classic mistake is treating something deep and custom-held as a surface parameter that a single decree can reset.

6. "Gentle commerce": trade softens manners and forces power to behave

What it is. Book 20 opens with a line that would be quoted for centuries: "Commerce cures destructive prejudices; and it is almost a general rule that wherever there are gentle mores there is commerce, and wherever there is commerce there are gentle mores." And: "The natural effect of commerce is to lead to peace." Two nations that trade become mutually dependent — one has an interest in buying, the other in selling — and every union rests on mutual need.

The better part is a historical mechanism (Book 21). Montesquieu tells how medieval Jews, expelled and extorted, invented — or at least spread — the bill of exchange: a slip of paper standing in for a cartload of gold, making wealth invisible and portable. That changed the ruler's arithmetic. If a prince tried to seize wealth at will, it would simply leave for another country; rulers were forced to govern more predictably and more gently, and people "began to be cured of Machiavellianism" (paraphrased). Historians today may doubt the detail about who invented the bill, but the mechanism is clear: when wealth can move, power has to negotiate with it.

He wasn't naïve. He concedes that commerce corrupts pure mores (Plato had complained of it), and that the commercial spirit puts a price on every action and every virtue (paraphrased). It unites nations but not necessarily individuals, and it breeds a precise, calculating kind of justice.

How it changes the way you see. This doux commerce thesis is one root of Kant's "perpetual peace" and of postwar Europe's bet that a coal and steel community would make war unthinkable; the economist Albert Hirschman traced its history in The Passions and the Interests. It adds a dimension to how you see trade: mobile wealth is an invisible constraint on arbitrary power. Flip it, and the warning follows — when interdependence is weaponized, gentleness turns into leverage.

The Skeleton

Compress thirty-one books into one line of argument, and this is what Montesquieu establishes:

Step 1: Laws are neither arbitrary nor universal; they are products of relations, so they can be compared and explained the way nature is. (Book 1)

Step 2: The main relation is to the form of government. Each regime has a structure (nature) and a driving force (principle); education, civil and criminal law, sumptuary law, the status of women all have to fit the principle. When the principle rots, the regime is finished. (Books 2–8)

Step 3: Under any regime, liberty exists only where power is restrained, and restraint comes only from "the arrangement of things" — separated powers, mutual vetoes, intermediate bodies, mild punishments. (Books 11–12)

Step 4: Beyond the regime, climate, terrain, mores, religion, commerce and population form a "general spirit." Good legislators work with it while counteracting its vices. (Books 14–25)

Conclusion: There is no best law, only the most fitting one; but every law that fits liberty shares two traits — moderation, and leaving no power unchecked. (Book 29)

How does he prove it? Through comparison on an unprecedented scale, from Greece and Rome and Frankish law to China, Persia and England. The method was new (finding causes in diversity); the evidence was old (much of it secondhand, some of it unreliable) — which is exactly why later readers both revere and fault him.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "Montesquieu invented the separation of powers." He didn't. Locke's Second Treatise already distinguished legislative, executive and federative powers, and the classical theory of "mixed government" goes back further (Polybius used it to explain Rome's strength). Montesquieu's contribution was to pull the judiciary out as its own power and tie separation directly to liberty, in language sharp enough to be written straight into a constitution.

Misreading 2: "Separation means the branches never interfere with each other." As shown above, he wanted mutual checks and vetoes, not isolation. Madison used exactly this point in Federalist No. 47 against the Anti-Federalists: Montesquieu never said the branches could have no overlap, only that the whole power of one department must not be held by those who hold the whole power of another.

Criticism 1: The England he described didn't exist. Montesquieu lived in England from 1729 to 1731, but his England of three separate powers was idealized. The real trend was a cabinet governing through a parliamentary majority, with executive and legislature increasingly fused. Walter Bagehot argued in The English Constitution (1867) that the "efficient secret" of the system was precisely the close union of the two. The theory's great success was in America, not in the England it claimed to describe.

Criticism 2: The climate theory is crude, and easily turned into a defense of hierarchy and empire. The sheep's tongue and claims like "people in hot countries are naturally indolent" are pseudo-physiology with an obvious Eurocentric tilt, and geographical determinism was later used to argue that some peoples were "made" to be ruled. His defenders point out that he repeatedly urges legislators to fight climate's vices, and that in Book 15 he mocks the case for enslaving Africans with savage irony (the passage beginning "these creatures are black from head to toe…" is satire, often quoted as if it were his sincere view). Misreading that irony is one of the great injustices done to Montesquieu — but the climate theory itself doesn't survive scrutiny, and there's no need to pretend otherwise.

Criticism 3: The case for intermediate powers has a class interest behind it. He was a nobleman who inherited, and later sold, a presiding judgeship in the Bordeaux parlement. He defended noble privilege and the sale of judicial offices, and critics like Voltaire mocked him for speaking up for the robe nobility. On the eve of the Revolution the parlements were exactly the privileged bastions blocking reform. He saw how intermediate bodies resist despotism, but underrated how readily they become oppressors themselves.

Criticism 4: Shaky sources, loose structure. He leaned heavily on missionaries' and merchants' travel accounts, and his China, Japan and Persia are often wrong or hearsay (he files China under despotism, then has to concede that its rites restrain it powerfully, and tangles himself up). Madame du Deffand is supposed to have quipped that the book was not De l'esprit des lois but de l'esprit sur les lois — "wit about the laws," playing on esprit as both "spirit" and "wit" (the attribution is anecdotal). The thirty-one books jump around, and the last ones plunge into Frankish feudal law, where many readers give up.

Criticism 5: Rousseau's opposite turn. In The Social Contract Rousseau insists sovereignty is a single, indivisible general will; dividing it, he says, is like a conjurer cutting up a child and reassembling it (paraphrased). This is a fault line running through modern politics: does liberty come from dispersing power (Montesquieu, Madison) or from unifying the people's will (Rousseau, the Jacobins)? One side fears the tyranny of the majority, the other the privileges of the few — and both have blood on the record.

Montesquieu Beyond This Book

Read only The Spirit of the Laws and you'd think of him as a cool comparative jurist. But his theory of despotism was first told as a novel. Persian Letters (1721) purports to be letters home from two Persian noblemen in Paris, using foreign eyes to satirize France's king, church and salons — making your own society strange is itself comparative politics in embryo. The darker thread is the plot: Usbek lectures on reason in Paris while, back in Isfahan, eunuchs rule his harem by fear. The longer he's away, the worse the disorder and the crueler his orders, until his favorite wife, Roxane, writes before taking poison that though enslaved she was always free — she had "reformed your laws by those of nature" (paraphrased). The harem is despotism in miniature: held together by fear, forced to answer every absence of the master with more fear, and finally collapsing from within.

The Considerations on the Romans (1734) was the first trial of his method of looking for causes: Rome rose on civic virtue and the competitive turbulence of republican institutions; once the empire grew, distant legions obeyed their generals rather than the republic, citizens became subjects, and when virtue went the institutions could not hold. "It is not Fortune that rules the world" (paraphrased). Taken together, the three books show the whole man: by satire, then history, then comparative law, he proves one thing over and over — absolute power destroys itself.

Crossovers

  • Evolutionary biology · adaptation and niches: Evolution has no "best organism," only fit to a given environment — a polar bear at the equator is a poor design. "The best laws are simply the ones that fit this people" has the same structure, a century before Darwin. The difference: natural selection works by elimination and has no legislator; Montesquieu wants a legislator who actively fights the environment's vices.
  • Cultural psychology · the rice theory: In a 2014 Science paper, Thomas Talhelm and colleagues found that people from China's rice-growing regions, where irrigation demands cooperation, think more holistically and collectively than those from wheat regions. It's nearly a modern version of "the land shapes the general spirit," with livelihood replacing body temperature as the cause. Unlike Montesquieu, they test it with data — and the effect size is still debated.
  • Physiology · antagonistic regulation: The body never hands a variable to a single hormone: insulin lowers blood sugar, glucagon raises it; flexor and extensor muscles work in pairs. Homeostasis is literally power checking power. The difference: evolution tuned the body's antagonists, while the balance of three branches has to be written down — and ambition slowly pushes it out of true.
  • Distributed systems · separation of duties and the two-person rule: Security engineering insists that whoever writes the code, approves the release and holds the keys must not be the same person; launching a nuclear weapon takes two officers turning keys at once. The core idea is the same — no one has to be trusted. The difference: system designers can assume a bounded fraction of attackers, while political design faces the fact that any officeholder may become one.

Ten Lines to Keep

① Laws are the necessary relations arising from the nature of things — not commands, but a web that has to fit a people's government, land, customs and livelihood.

② It would be a great coincidence if the laws made for one nation suited another.

③ Republics run on virtue, monarchies on honor, despotisms on fear: don't just look at a system's structure, look at the passion that drives it.

④ The corruption of every government almost always begins with the corruption of its principle.

⑤ When the savages of Louisiana want fruit, they cut down the tree — that is despotism.

⑥ Everyone with power tends to abuse it until they meet a limit; so power must check power, by the arrangement of things.

⑦ Liberty isn't doing what you like but doing all the law permits; its mark is each person's quiet confidence in their own safety.

⑧ If one person or body holds legislative, executive and judicial power, all is lost — and separation means mutual checks, not mutual isolation.

⑨ Bad legislators indulge the vices of the climate, good ones resist them; don't use laws to change what should be changed by manners.

⑩ The spirit of moderation should be the legislator's spirit: political good always lies between two extremes.

Deep Read · You now have the through-line of The Spirit of the Laws: law as relation, the nature and principle of regimes, power checking power and the separation of powers, intermediate bodies and moderate government, climate and the general spirit, gentle commerce — along with its idealized England, its crude climate theory, its defense of noble privilege, and the still-open quarrel with Rousseau's indivisible sovereignty.
