Notes on Being a Man

Notes on Being a Man · Scott Galloway · 2025

中文 →

In One Sentence

Young men are falling behind — in school, at work, in friendship and intimacy — and the vacuum is being filled by influencers selling a tough-guy persona and a steady drip of grievance. Galloway wants to offer a different answer: being a man is not an identity but a net contribution — you give more than you take. Protect, provide, procreate, the three oldest male jobs, get redefined as three ways of being useful to other people.

Where It Sits

Galloway is a marketing professor at NYU's Stern School of Business, a serial entrepreneur, and the co-host of Pivot and host of The Prof G Pod, known for acid-tongued business commentary. This 2025 book is his most personal since The Algebra of Happiness (2019): half memoir (raised by a single mother, a first half of life marked by selfishness and failure), half data-driven polemic, plus a father's operating manual for his sons. Among books on the "boys and men" crisis, it pairs naturally with Richard Reeves's more policy-oriented Of Boys and Men (2022): Reeves writes for policymakers; Galloway writes for the boys themselves and their parents.

The Core Claims

  • The state of young men is an underrated social crisis. They are the cohort in Western democracies that has fallen furthest and fastest — and a lonely, broke, directionless young man is the most dangerous person in a society, to himself and to others.
  • The essence of masculinity is "surplus value": a man becomes a man at the moment he reliably produces more than he consumes and gives more than he takes. The threshold has nothing to do with age, muscle, or income as such.
  • Protect, provide, procreate should not be discarded but expanded. Instead of calling traditional masculinity "toxic," name the specific behaviors that do harm — cruelty, bullying, abuse of power — and reinterpret the three ancient duties as forms of service any man can practice, including men who never marry or have children.

The Key Concepts, One by One

1. The crisis: where the boys fell behind

Galloway starts from a set of numbers and a judgment. The numbers' broad direction is well supported: in the U.S., women now earn roughly 60 percent of bachelor's degrees, a gender gap wider than the one that disadvantaged women in the early 1970s; men die by suicide at about four times the rate of women; and a 2021 survey by the Survey Center on American Life found that about 15 percent of men said they had no close friends, up from roughly 3 percent in 1990. Add large numbers of young men living with their parents, without relationships, and spending long hours on screens — and these lines all bend downward together after the 2010s.

The judgment is the line he keeps returning to (paraphrased): there is nothing more dangerous than a lonely, broke young man. It isn't demonizing men; it describes a structure. A person with no economic prospects and no relationships tethering him has nothing to lose and no one who'll be disappointed if he fails. At that point anger, radicalization, and capture by online "mentors" become the path of least resistance.

An analogy: a ship that loses both its anchor (relationships) and its engine (work and purpose) doesn't sit still — it drifts with whatever current is strongest. In Galloway's view, today's strongest current is algorithmically amplified anger and a narrative of male victimhood.

How it changes your view: many problems that look political or cultural — extremism, online gender wars, young people "checking out" — can be reread as a much plainer question: does this person have a job, friends, and someone who needs him? When the answer is three noes, any ideology is just the first tenant to move into the vacancy.

2. Man = surplus value: the turn from taking to giving

This is the book's central concept and the one most easily skimmed past. The definition Galloway gave again and again while promoting the book: to be a man is to be a producer of surplus value — you bring your family, colleagues, and community more than you take from them. "Value" here isn't just money: time, attention, emotional support, a promise people can count on all count.

He uses himself as the cautionary tale. He says he didn't qualify as a man until his forties, because before that he treated nearly every relationship as a transaction — marriage, friendship, partnership, all scored by "what am I getting?" Only after divorce, entrepreneurial ups and downs, and fatherhood did he slowly switch ledgers. The confession isn't decoration; it's evidence for the concept: the threshold isn't how old, strong, or rich you are, but whether your account runs positive or negative. A twenty-year-old stocking supermarket shelves who visits his grandmother every week may already be over the line; a forty-year-old executive earning seven figures and looking out only for himself may not be.

The analogy is pure business school: a company's health isn't its revenue but whether its cash flow is positive. Galloway moves that language onto character: are you a net inflow or a net outflow?

Why it matters: it turns masculinity from an identity label into a state you can act on, measure, and lose. You aren't a man because you were born male, nor because you lift or earn; you keep re-earning it through daily giving. That knocks down two stories at once — that masculinity is inherently toxic, and that a man strong enough is entitled to take.

3. The three-legged stool: protect, provide, procreate

The book's frame is what Galloway calls the "three Ps": protect, provide, procreate — three legs of a stool; lose one and it wobbles. It sounds like a throwback to old gender roles, but he deliberately widens each:

DutyTraditional versionGalloway's expanded version
ProtectUse strength to fend off enemies and guard the familySide with the vulnerable; be the guy who breaks up the fight rather than joins it; be an emotional and physical source of safety for those around you
ProvideThe man as sole breadwinnerBe financially reliable, including backing a partner's career; count time, attention, and emotional labor as provision too
ProcreateHave children, continue the lineInvest in the next generation: as a father, but also as an uncle, coach, or mentor — younger people are better off because of you

Table: the "old" and "expanded" versions of the three ancient duties — Galloway keeps the frame and removes its exclusivity.

The stool's cleverness: it keeps the heroic vocabulary boys are instinctively drawn to, and swaps its contents for service. A sixteen-year-old may light up at "be a protector" and feel nothing at "examine your privilege"; Galloway's strategy is to come in through the first door and walk him to a place the second camp would also endorse — caring for others, taking responsibility, giving up some convenience for someone else.

How it changes your view: in debates over what men should be, you notice the real disagreement is often not the frame but its contents. "Protect" can be a license for violence or another word for empathy; the question isn't whether to keep the word but who defines it and whom it serves.

4. Not "toxic masculinity," but cruelty, bullying, and abuse of power

Galloway explicitly refuses the phrase "toxic masculinity." Not because what it points to doesn't exist, but because the label welds "masculinity" itself to "harm": a boy who hears it takes away the message that the male part of him is a poison to be managed — and goes looking for whoever tells him, "You're not the problem; the world is keeping you down."

His alternative is to name specific behaviors: cruelty, bullying, preying on the weak, abuse of power — bad no matter who does them — while courage, strength, competitiveness, and appetite for risk are neutral or even valuable raw materials; what matters is where they're pointed.

He is equally hard on the other side: the influencer "male mentors" who cast men as victims and sell anger and grievance. One of the book's central asks is to drop the victim mindset and the sense of grievance — not because boys have never been wronged, but because grievance is an emotion that keeps you stuck: it hands the steering wheel of your life to whomever you resent.

How it changes your view: language doesn't just describe; it recruits. The words you use for a group decide who finds it easiest to recruit the people in it. That principle reaches well beyond the question of men.

5. Follow your talent, not your passion; meet anxiety with action

On careers, Galloway pushes back on the slogan "follow your passion." His version: follow your talent — find something you do better than most people and that others will pay for, then put in years; passion usually comes from being good at something, not the other way around. The industries sold as "passions" (music, sports, fashion, media) are oversubscribed, and their rewards are wildly unequal.

Two companions come with it: take risks, especially when young — move to a big city, take the job that makes you uncomfortable, ask out the person you assume will say no — and handle anxiety with action rather than rumination. In his telling, anxiety isn't a disease to be eliminated but a prompt: "there's something you haven't done yet." Do the thing, and the anxiety usually shrinks on its own.

How it changes your view: it breaks "becoming a useful person" from a question of character into a series of executable moves: pick a lane, put in the hours, take the risk, translate the anxiety into a to-do list. For a young man who feels lost, a concrete next step is often more useful than grand meaning.

6. Synthetic substitutes: screens, porn, and frictionless rewards

Galloway has never been gentle with tech companies. Here he treats phones and social media as a major force stunting boys' emotional development and calls pornography a "masculinity killer." The logic is consistent: boys are wired to strive for status, take risks, and pursue partners — drives that would otherwise push them out of their rooms to learn skills, endure rejection, and get better — and tech products supply cheap synthetic versions of those rewards. Winning in a game, collecting likes, getting sexual stimulation from a screen: none requires the friction of the real world, so none produces real growth. The sports-betting apps he has long attacked in his newsletter share the same structure, turning the urge to take risks into a precisely harvested funnel.

The analogy is sugar instead of a meal: the hunger signal shuts off for a while, but the nutrition never arrives. The danger isn't that these things corrupt boys; it's that they make them feel "enough," so they stop going out to get the real thing.

How it changes your view: you start auditing your own pastimes with one question: is this helping me practice what real life demands, or is it switching off the signal that would make me go practice?

7. Relationships and manners: a small, reliable circle, and "a good father is good to the mother"

The back half of the book comes down to relationships. A few pieces of advice readers remember most: build a small, reliable circle of friends and maintain it on purpose — male friendship often depends on doing things together (sports, work, fraternities) and vanishes when those settings do, so you have to organize the dinner and make the call; being a good father largely means being good to your children's mother — kids learn far more from how their father treats their mother than from anything he lectures; and manners — one chapter is titled "Man…ners" — which he treats as the foundation of success, a continuous, low-cost form of respect for others.

The final chapter, "Life Is So Rich," is about presence and mortality: precisely because time is finite, the ordinary moments with people you love are what you actually own. The book closes with a letter to his two sons.

How it changes your view: "surplus value" lands here at its smallest unit — a phone call, showing up on time, a thank-you. Whether someone is a net contributor is settled not in the big moments but in countless small ones nobody sees.

The Spine of the Argument

① Problem: young men are slipping on several dimensions at once; a lonely man with no prospects is both suffering and dangerous.

② Diagnosis: the old male script has stopped working, and the empty slot has been taken by two bad answers — one tells boys "you are the problem," the other "you are the victim; get angry."

③ Redefinition: a man is a producer of surplus value; protect, provide, procreate are widened into three ways of serving others.

④ Path: follow talent, take risks, step away from synthetic on-screen rewards, turn anxiety into action.

⑤ Landing: small reliable relationships, manners, presence — net contribution accumulating day by day.

What it really establishes isn't "how men should be" but that a story for boys more attractive than anger is possible. It rests on three things: the outline of a crisis drawn from public data, the author's own turn from selfishness to giving, and a business professor's instinct for translating everything into something executable.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "It's another men's-rights book." Galloway's position is fairly clearly center-left: he doesn't want anything to slow women's continued entry into college, and he openly criticizes the misogyny of male influencers. His point is that helping boys doesn't require girls to go backward — this isn't a zero-sum game.

Misreading 2: "The three Ps are just a return to traditional gender roles." The frame is old, but its contents are deliberately widened (see the table). Critics fairly ask: if it's widened that far, why insist on calling these male duties at all? That's the doorway to the first criticism below.

Criticism 1: lots of maxims, little structural analysis. The Guardian's review said his solutions to men's problems often boil down to a set of pithy codes and maxims, some sensible, some old-fashioned; another reviewer found the three commandments tidy enough for a PowerPoint slide. The deeper criticism: the book pushes a structural problem — industrial change, schools ill-suited to boys, housing and marriage markets — largely back onto individual choices, and no amount of individual effort changes those structures.

Criticism 2: the data is padded, and the author's life undercuts the pitch. Some reviews note he tends to cite the most dramatic version of a statistic. Others find a wealthy, successful business professor who admits to years of transactional relationships a limited role model, and note that his template for success — high-achieving, heterosexual, capitalist upward mobility — doesn't fit all men.

Criticism 3: pragmatic, but without an ethical foundation. A review from a Christian perspective credited the book's practical wisdom but argued it lacks a moral ground beyond usefulness — he calls porn a masculinity killer, for example, without offering a deeper reason. From the clinical side, one therapist's review said the book "isn't bad, but it isn't exactly therapy": it can point a direction but can't treat deeper wounds.

And in fairness: in a field where talk about boys is routinely hijacked by the extremes, the book manages something hard — it uses language boys will actually listen to and steers them toward care and responsibility. Its value lies more in who will actually read it than in theoretical originality.

Crossovers

  • Anthropology · Gilmore's manhood triad: In Manhood in the Making (1990), anthropologist David Gilmore compared cultures from the Mediterranean to New Guinea and found manhood almost everywhere to be a status that must be earned and can be lost — and that it keeps landing on the same duties: "Man-the-Impregnator-Protector-Provider." That's nearly Galloway's three Ps; a 2025 self-help book independently retraces a cross-cultural finding over three decades old. The difference: Gilmore stresses the script as a social demand forced by harsh environments, not an ideal to celebrate.
  • Political economy · Marx's "surplus value": same phrase, opposite direction. Marx's surplus value (Mehrwert) is value workers create and capitalists appropriate; Galloway's is the extra a person chooses to give away. One is about extraction, the other about gift.
  • Neuroscience · Cacioppo on loneliness: John Cacioppo's research treats loneliness as a biological alarm, like hunger, that makes the brain more vigilant for social threat and more prone to hostile readings — one mechanism behind "a lonely young man is dangerous." The difference: Cacioppo emphasizes the harm and withdrawal the lonely person suffers, not the danger to others.
  • Evolutionary psychology · Dunbar's layers: Robin Dunbar proposes that social networks are layered — an inner circle of about 5, then about 15, out to a limit near 150 — and the innermost layer needs steady time to sustain. The "small, reliable circle" is those five; and because male friendship rides on shared activities, that layer is the first to empty when the activities stop.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① Young men are among the fastest-falling groups of our time, and there is nothing more dangerous than a lonely, broke young man (paraphrased).

② To be a man is to produce surplus value: you give more than you take.

③ The line has nothing to do with age, muscle, or income — a rich forty-year-old may not have crossed it; a twenty-year-old stocking shelves may have crossed it long ago.

④ Protect, provide, procreate are the stool's three legs; breaking up a fight, backing a partner's career, and being a good mentor all count.

⑤ Don't say "toxic masculinity"; name cruelty, bullying, and abuse of power — the label you give a group decides who gets to recruit it.

⑥ Grievance keeps you stuck: it hands the steering wheel of your life to whomever you resent.

⑦ Follow your talent, not your passion; passion usually comes after you get good.

⑧ Screens, porn, and betting apps offer synthetic rewards — their danger isn't that they corrupt you but that they make you feel "enough," so you stop going out for the real thing.

⑨ Being a good father largely means being good to your kids' mother; children learn what you do, not what you say.

⑩ Net contribution is settled not in grand moments but in a phone call, showing up on time, a thank-you — because life is finite, those ordinary moments are what you actually own.
