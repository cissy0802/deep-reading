A Short History of Chinese Philosophy

A Short History of Chinese Philosophy · Fung Yu-lan (Feng Youlan) · 1948, edited by Derk Bodde

中文 →

In One Sentence

For more than two thousand years, Chinese philosophy kept circling a single question: how can a person reach the highest state of being while still eating, working and living an ordinary life, without leaving the world or waiting for heaven? Fung calls the answer "sublime yet following the common way," and he threads the early schools, Neo-Taoism, Chinese Buddhism and Neo-Confucianism onto that one line.

Where It Sits

Fung Yu-lan (1895–1990) earned his PhD in philosophy at Columbia, and in the 1930s wrote a two-volume History of Chinese Philosophy, one of the first systematic accounts of the field using modern philosophical method. This shorter book grew out of lectures he gave at the University of Pennsylvania in 1946–47 for Western readers who knew nothing about China, so it constantly holds Chinese ideas up against Western ones and recasts them as questions an outsider can follow.

Core Arguments

  • Philosophy is systematic reflection on life, and its job is not to add knowledge but to raise the mind to a higher state. In Chinese culture philosophy plays the role religion plays elsewhere: the Chinese longing for something beyond the here and now has been met mainly by philosophy, not by a church.
  • The main spirit of Chinese philosophy is "sublime yet following the common way" and "sageliness within, kingliness without." The highest state is not found outside daily life but inside it, and the inner work of becoming a sage and the outer work of ordering the world are two sides of one task.
  • Chinese philosophy prefers suggestion and the "negative method." It speaks in aphorisms, metaphors and dialogues, and approaches the unsayable by saying what it is not. That is not muddled thinking: what it wants to reach simply lies beyond where clear concepts can go.

Put together: Chinese philosophy is not a body of knowledge but a discipline of becoming a certain kind of person. Grasp that and two millennia of arguments start to point in one direction.

The Key Concepts, Unpacked

1. Spheres of living: one act, four ways of living it

What it is. In the final chapter, where he sketches his own philosophy, Fung argues that what separates people is not what they do but how deeply they understand and are aware of what they do. Different understanding gives the same act a different meaning, and that meaning makes up a person's sphere of living. He names four:

SphereHow the person actsHow they understand themselves
InnocentFollows instinct and customRarely asks why; could not say what the act means
UtilitarianActs for personal gain, even if others benefitKnows they are working for "me"
MoralActs from righteousness, doing their part for societyKnows they are a member of society, complete only within the whole
TranscendentDoes the same things, but as service to HeavenKnows they are a member of the universe, one with all things

Fung's four spheres. The first two are "gifts of nature" that everyone is born into; the last two are "creations of the spirit," reached only through philosophical understanding.

Analogy / example. Four people sweep a street. The first does it because everyone does. The second does it for the wage and the review. The third thinks a clean street is what he owes his city. The fourth feels that each stroke of the broom is part of the working of the cosmos. Same motion, entirely different spheres. The person in the transcendent sphere does nothing odd; he simply understands the most.

Why it matters. This scheme explains why Chinese philosophy never needed a religious "other world": the transcendence you want is available in this one, by changing your understanding. It is also Fung's yardstick for the schools: Yang Zhu and the Legalists think broadly within the utilitarian sphere, the Confucians mainly in the moral sphere, the Taoists and Chan Buddhists aim at the transcendent, and Neo-Confucianism tries to fuse the last two.

How it changes the way you see. It moves the question "am I living well?" from "should I change jobs or move?" to "should I change how I understand?" Fung's point: first ask how well you understand what you are already doing.

2. Sublime yet common: the summit is inside everyday life

What it is. The phrase comes from the Doctrine of the Mean, and Fung makes it the keynote of the whole book. "Sublime" names the summit of the spirit; "following the common way" means walking an ordinary path. The world's philosophies and religions, he says, tend to be either this-worldly (concerned with family, society and achievement, as Confucianism is often seen) or other-worldly (seeking escape, as early Buddhism seeks nirvana and release from rebirth). The mainstream of Chinese philosophy takes neither side and tries to unite them: in the world and beyond it at once.

Its companion phrase is "sageliness within, kingliness without" (from the closing chapter of the Zhuangzi, later borrowed by nearly every school). The inner sage and the outer ruler cannot be separated, so a Chinese philosopher is by nature also a political philosopher: once you become a sage, the natural next step is to govern and to teach. By this standard, Fung notes, Plato's philosopher-king comes closest to the Chinese ideal.

Analogy / example. Fung likes to quote a line by the Tang-dynasty lay Buddhist Pang Yun: "Supernatural power and marvelous activity: drawing water and carrying firewood." Buddhism began as the most other-worldly of religions; in China, through Chan, it ended up locating enlightenment in chores. That is the evidence of "sublime yet common" reshaping a foreign religion. The Neo-Confucians then turned the conclusion back on the Buddhists: if the marvelous Way is present in carrying firewood, it is present in serving one's father and one's ruler, so why leave home to become a monk?

Why it matters. Many Western readers take Confucianism for a secular ethics with no transcendence. Fung's reply: transcendence is not outside ethics but in the depth of understanding with which ethics is lived out. When Confucius says that at seventy he could follow his heart's desire without overstepping the bounds, he describes a perfect freedom inside ordinary rules.

How it changes the way you see. You stop treating "renounce the world" and "live in it" as a binary. You don't have to leave life to find meaning, nor give up meaning to get on with life: the real question is whether you can stand in the middle of life while holding a view higher than it.

3. Aphorisms and the negative method: why Chinese philosophy won't spell things out

What it is. Western readers meeting the Analects or the Laozi often find them fragmentary and unargued. Fung concedes they lack articulateness but says they are rich in suggestiveness: through aphorisms, metaphors and parables they stop short and let the reader feel the unspoken meaning.

In the last chapter he turns this into a methodology. The positive method uses logical analysis to say clearly what something is, and is the strength of Western philosophy. The negative method says what it is not, negating step by step until, with everything sayable said, what cannot be said shows itself. Taoism and Chan are masters of it: the Laozi opens "The Tao that can be spoken of is not the constant Tao," and Chan holds that the "first principle" cannot be spoken, since opening your mouth is already a mistake. Fung's conclusion (paraphrased): a complete metaphysics should begin with the positive method and end with the negative. Without first passing through logical clarity, the negative method becomes mere confusion; without finally falling silent, the positive method never reaches the top.

Analogy / example. Zhuangzi offers the image: the fish trap exists for the fish, and once you have the fish you can forget the trap; words exist for meaning, and once you have the meaning you can forget the words. Language is a tool, not a destination.

Why it matters. It names a structural feature of Chinese thought: its core is often written in the white space. Read the Laozi like a textbook and it contradicts itself; read it like a poem and it begins to speak.

How it changes the way you see. Faced with an old saying that seems empty, don't rush to call it unargued; ask where it is trying to lead you that words can't. And don't use "ineffable" as an excuse for laziness either: Fung demands that you say everything you can until you can say no more, and only then fall silent.

4. Three stages of Taoism: three ways to avoid harm

What it is. Fung presents early Taoism not as a few thinkers talking past each other but as a progression. It starts from a plain question: how does one preserve life and avoid harm? The three stages are three increasingly clever answers.

  • Stage one, Yang Zhu: run. Yang Zhu put preserving life above everything. Mencius says he would not pluck out a single hair to benefit the whole world; the Han Feizi says he would not trade one hair of his shin for all the world's profit. The point is not stinginess but that no external good is worth your life, so stay out of office and live in seclusion. But the world is complex, and some dangers can't be dodged.
  • Stage two, Laozi: understand the laws. Hiding has no end, so better to see how things change and act in accord. The central law is "reversal is the movement of the Tao": anything pushed to an extreme turns into its opposite. Hence: be soft, know when you have enough, keep low. Fung links this to agrarian life: farmers watch the seasons turn and fullness give way to decline every year, so cyclical change feels self-evident. But laws sometimes fail, and understanding them still guarantees no safety.
  • Stage three, Zhuangzi: see from higher up. Zhuangzi's move is the most radical: since harm cannot be avoided, change how you see it. From a human standpoint there is big and small, right and wrong, life and death; but "seen from the Tao, nothing is noble or base." Once you truly see these distinctions as relative and feel yourself one with all things, "harm" loses its grip. When his wife died, Zhuangzi drummed on a pot and sang, seeing death as one more turn of the seasons.

Analogy / example. Three people facing storms: one moves away from the coast (running); one learns to read clouds and reinforces the house in time (understanding laws); one realizes that storms and he alike are part of nature, and meets the storm without fear (changing perspective). By Zhuangzi, the problem is not solved but dissolved.

Why it matters. The progression reveals Taoism's inner logic and its division of labor with Confucianism: Confucians teach how to do your part in society, Taoists how to rise above the distinctions society hands you. For two thousand years Chinese scholars were, as the saying goes, Confucian in success and Taoist in failure, because together the two made a whole life.

How it changes the way you see. In a setback, ask which move you're making: running, studying the pattern, or stepping back to see the thing at a larger scale. The third move is not numbness; it is enlarging the scale until the thing shrinks back to its real size.

5. Righteousness and fate: how a Confucian acts without counting the odds

What it is. Fung reads Confucius through two words. Righteousness (yi) is the "ought": if something should be done, you do it because it is right, not because it pays. "The superior man understands righteousness; the petty man understands profit." Fate (ming) is the whole set of conditions beyond your control, the part that decides success or failure. Confucius says that one who does not know fate cannot be a superior man.

Together they yield what Fung, borrowing a later Confucian phrase, calls "doing for nothing": you do what you ought without making the outcome your reason. Outcomes belong to fate; righteousness belongs to you. A gatekeeper once described Confucius as "the one who knows it can't be done and does it anyway," and Fung thinks no line captures Confucianism better.

Analogy / example. A doctor fights for a patient who is almost certainly lost. She does it not because she ran the odds but because it is what a doctor does. If the patient dies she grieves, but she doesn't feel the effort was wasted, because she never staked its meaning on the result. Hence Confucius: "the humane are free from worry."

Mencius pushes further. Human nature is good: everyone has four "sprouts" of virtue, such as the heart that cannot bear another's suffering, and by extending them one cultivates a "flood-like qi," a moral energy that fills heaven and earth. For Fung, Mencius' flood-like qi already reaches from the moral sphere into the transcendent, exactly where Neo-Confucianism would later pick up. On the other side Xunzi held human nature to be bad, with goodness the product of deliberate effort and ritual training, representing the more realist, institution-minded branch.

Why it matters. It gives Confucianism a moral drive that needs neither gods nor rewards in an afterlife. Righteousness resembles Western deontology (judging acts by duty rather than consequences), and knowing fate adds a layer of psychological peace: do what you should and leave the rest to fate.

How it changes the way you see. It splits every task in two: what is my duty to perform, and what is fate I can't control. Answer only for the first, and you'll dare harder things with less anxiety.

6. From "beyond shapes" to principle: how Chinese philosophy learned to think the abstract

What it is. Fung traces an easily missed thread: Chinese philosophy learning to think about what lies "beyond shapes and features," things that can't be seen or touched and don't sit in space and time. The first step was taken by the School of Names, Warring States thinkers who argued about the relation between names (concepts) and actualities (things). Often dismissed as sophists, they get Fung's respect:

  • Hui Shi stressed that all distinctions are relative ("the unity of similarity and difference"): from a large enough scale, "heaven is as low as earth, mountains as level as marshes." This line fed Zhuangzi.
  • Gongsun Long argued that "a white horse is not a horse": "horse" names a shape, "white" a color, and "white horse" a combination, so the concept "white horse" is not identical to the concept "horse." Fung reads this as Gongsun Long discovering universals, abstract concepts that exist independently of any particular horse.

The thread blossomed more than a thousand years later in Neo-Confucianism. Zhu Xi divided the world into principle (li) and material force (qi): principle is what makes each kind of thing the kind it is, akin to a universal, invisible; qi is the stuff concrete things are made of. "Before there are boats, there is already the principle of boats." Every person and thing has its principle, and all principles together are the one Supreme Ultimate: "principle is one, its manifestations many." Zhu used the image of the moon reflected in ten thousand rivers: one moon in the sky, a whole moon in every river, not a moon broken into pieces.

Against him stood the School of Mind of Lu Jiuyuan and Wang Yangming. Zhu said "nature is principle," principle lies in things outside the mind, so one must investigate things one by one; Lu and Wang said "mind is principle," so seeking outward misses what is near. As a young man Wang tried Zhu's method by "investigating" bamboo for seven days and got only an illness for it; later, in exile in Guizhou, he realized that "my own nature is sufficient for sagehood." Fung's verdict: the split between the two schools comes down to whether there is a world of principle independent of the mind.

Why it matters. It corrects the impression that Chinese philosophy only does ethics and never thinks abstractly: it grew its own reflection on universals and a metaphysical realm, only to bring it back to life in the end. One studies principle in order to become a sage.

How it changes the way you see. "Principle is one, manifestations many" is a useful template: a single principle looks different in countless concrete situations, yet each fully embodies it. To understand a principle is to be able to recognize it in any particular case.

The Spine of the Argument

1. Background. China is a continental, agrarian country whose social skeleton is the family. That produced a soil in which human relationships matter most, change is cyclical, and the unity of heaven and humanity feels like common sense.

2. The age of the philosophers (pre-Qin). As the old Zhou order collapsed, specialists once attached to aristocratic houses scattered and began teaching on their own, giving rise to the "hundred schools." Fung keeps the Han historian Sima Tan's six-school scheme (Yin-Yang, Confucian, Mohist, Names, Legalist, Taoist) and adds his own hypothesis about origins: Confucians came from teachers and ritual experts, Mohists from knights-errant, Taoists from recluses, the School of Names from debaters, the Yin-Yang school from diviners, and Legalists from practical statesmen. Every basic option for a philosophy of life is on the table by now.

3. The age of the classics (from the Han). After the Qin unified the empire, thought had to be unified too. Under Emperor Wu of Han, Dong Zhongshu helped make Confucianism the state orthodoxy, welding Yin-Yang and Five Phases cosmology onto Confucian ethics; the mutual response of heaven and humanity became official ideology. From then on, thought mostly advanced as commentary on the classics.

4. Taoism returns, Buddhism becomes Chinese. When Han scholasticism ossified, Wei–Jin literati turned to the Laozi, Zhuangzi and Book of Changes, creating what Fung calls Neo-Taoism: Guo Xiang's commentary on Zhuangzi argued that social norms and the natural Way don't conflict, while the "romantic" ideal celebrated a spontaneous, detached yet feeling personality. Meanwhile Indian Buddhism arrived and, via thinkers like Daosheng (who taught that all beings have Buddha-nature and can awaken suddenly), finally produced the purely Chinese Chan school: no reliance on scriptures, the ordinary mind is the Way.

5. The great synthesis of Neo-Confucianism. Confucianism absorbed Taoist cosmology and Buddhist theories of mind, rebuilding a system that covers the cosmos, self-cultivation and government at once: Zhou Dunyi and Zhang Zai on cosmology and "all people are my siblings, all things my companions," Cheng Yi and Zhu Xi on principle, Lu and Wang on mind. Here "sublime yet common" finally gets a complete theoretical form: without leaving home, one becomes, within ordinary relationships, a sage at one with heaven, earth and all things.

6. The modern age. Western philosophy arrives with the positive method of logical analysis. Fung's hope is that Chinese philosophy will use it to remedy its lack of clarity while keeping the sphere that the negative method opens.

What the book establishes is one claim: the history of Chinese philosophy is the history of a single question, how to become a sage within the world, rewritten again and again and answered more completely each time. It rests on one yardstick (spheres of living), one keynote (sublime yet common), and a constant question for each school: what problem it solved for its predecessors, and what it left open.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: Taoism the philosophy equals Taoism the religion. Fung insists on separating them: philosophical Taoism is a philosophy; religious Taoism is a religion of elixirs, longevity and immortality. In his paraphrased formula, the philosophy teaches following nature, while the religion teaches working against nature to defeat death. Conflating Zhuangzi's calm about death with the alchemist's quest to escape it gets the meaning exactly backwards.

Misreading 2: "the mean" equals splitting the difference. "Following the common way" means walking an ordinary path, not having both sides give a little. The figure Confucius scorned most was the "village worthy," the smooth people-pleaser who offends no one, whom he called "the thief of virtue."

Misreading 3: a "short history" is a neutral primer. It reads easily, but it carries strong personal judgments: who matters, how the schools rank, how Neo-Confucianism should be interpreted. You are reading Chinese philosophy as Fung Yu-lan sees it, not a neutral catalogue.

Criticism 1: cutting Chinese thought to a Western pattern. The most common and most forceful objection. Fung's Western training was in New Realism (an early twentieth-century Anglo-American school holding that universals are real independently of minds), and he used the universal/particular framework to explain Gongsun Long's white horse and Zhu Xi's principle. Many scholars think this forced Chinese concepts into Western boxes. When his two-volume history appeared, the philosopher Jin Yuelin's review already asked the sharp question: is this a history of Chinese philosophy, or a history of philosophy found in China, i.e. Western standards used to go hunting for Chinese material?

Criticism 2: the New Confucians say he missed the dimension of mind and nature. Modern New Confucians led by Mou Zongsan argued that Fung turned Neo-Confucianism into a static metaphysics of principle, almost a logic, and missed its core of moral practice and inner realization: the Neo-Confucians talked about principle in order to live it out, not to build a conceptual system. In their view his grasp of the School of Mind was especially distant.

Criticism 3: selection and proportion. The book centers on Confucianism, Taoism, Buddhism and Neo-Confucianism, and gives little space to later Mohist logic and science, Qing-dynasty evidential scholarship and philosophers like Dai Zhen, popular religion, or post-Han political thought. To draw a clear spiritual through-line, Fung sacrifices the diversity of intellectual history. The clearer the main line, the more branches get pruned.

Criticism 4: does China even have "philosophy"? Fung's project presupposes that Chinese thought can be called philosophy. The premise keeps being challenged: on his 2001 visit to China, Jacques Derrida said (paraphrased) that China has thought but not philosophy in the Western sense, setting off a long debate about the "legitimacy of Chinese philosophy." Fung's answer is in his first chapter: his definition of philosophy was broader than the Western academic one from the start.

Disputed specifics. He dated the Laozi after Confucius, which drew a famous dispute with Hu Shi; after partial Warring States manuscripts of the Laozi were excavated at Guodian in 1993, the question of how the text took shape remains open. His theory of the schools' social origins is likewise a suggestive hypothesis, not an established fact.

Fung Yu-lan Beyond This Book

Read only this book and Fung looks like a gifted historian. He was in fact a system-builder, and his life took several sharp turns.

1. The "Six Books" and the New Rational Philosophy. During the war, at the National Southwestern Associated University, he wrote six books meant to mark winter's end and the nation's revival. New Rational Philosophy (1939) is the core; A New Treatise on the Nature of Man (1943) introduced the four spheres. He described his work as "continuing" the Cheng–Zhu school rather than merely "repeating" it: he took principle and material force and, by logical analysis, refashioned them into four purely formal concepts: principle (what makes a thing what it is), material force (pure matter without any qualities), the Tao-in-operation (the whole process from material force to principle), and the Great Whole (the totality of all that is). They assert almost nothing about the actual world, yet they give a person the view of the Great Whole that opens onto the transcendent sphere.

2. The turn after 1949, and the late return. After 1949 Fung publicly repudiated his earlier philosophy and took up Marxism. In 1957 he proposed the "method of abstract inheritance": an ancient proposition has a concrete meaning, stamped by its era and class, which can be discarded, and an abstract meaning, which can be inherited. "To learn and to review it often," for example: what you learn may change, but "review what you have learned" still holds. He was criticized for it; late in the Cultural Revolution he was drawn into the campaign against Lin Biao and Confucius, which he later reflected on with regret. In his eighties he rewrote the field as a seven-volume New History of Chinese Philosophy, finished in 1990 shortly before his death; its last volume reaffirmed Zhang Zai's line that "opposition must end in reconciliation." A couplet he wrote for himself late in life ends "sublime yet following the common way," the very keynote of this book.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · Kohlberg's stages of moral development: Lawrence Kohlberg ranked moral reasoning as pre-conventional (reward and punishment), conventional (norms and others' expectations) and post-conventional (universal principles), which echoes the climb from the utilitarian to the moral sphere. The mismatch: Kohlberg measures only how people reason about right and wrong, with nothing like the cosmic transcendent sphere; late in life he speculated about a similar "seventh stage," but only as conjecture.
  • Neuroscience · the default mode network: When we daydream or think about ourselves, a set of regions called the default mode network is most active, and it is closely tied to the narrative self; Judson Brewer and colleagues found in 2011 that experienced meditators show less activity there while meditating. That seems to match Zhuangzi's "I have lost myself" and Cheng Hao's "one body with all things": the self's boundary fades. The mismatch: Fung's transcendent sphere comes from understanding, not from a state of consciousness that needs special training or drugs.
  • Quantum physics · Bohr's complementarity: Niels Bohr held that light's wave and particle aspects are mutually exclusive yet both necessary descriptions; when knighted into the Danish Order of the Elephant in 1947, he put the yin-yang symbol on his coat of arms with the motto contraria sunt complementa, opposites are complementary. The likeness is opposites completing each other; the difference is that complementarity concerns which aspect an experimental setup lets us see, while yin and yang describe the generation and cycling of the cosmos itself.
  • Complexity science · negative feedback and population cycles: "Reversal is the movement of the Tao" reads like negative feedback, where the farther a system strays, the harder it is pulled back. In the Lotka–Volterra model, more prey breed more predators, who eat the prey down, and the two rise and fall in turn. The difference: real systems also have positive feedback that runs away without return, so "extremes reverse" is no universal law.

The Book in Ten Lines

1. Philosophy is systematic reflection on life; its use is not to add knowledge but to raise the mind.

2. In China philosophy holds the place religion holds elsewhere: the longing for transcendence finds its home there.

3. The same act, understood differently, belongs to different spheres: innocent, utilitarian, moral, transcendent people can all be doing the same thing.

4. Sublime yet common: the highest state is not outside daily life but in drawing water and carrying firewood.

5. Sageliness within, kingliness without: perfecting yourself and ordering the world are one task, so Chinese philosophers are born political philosophers.

6. Chinese philosophy lacks articulateness but abounds in suggestion; words exist for meaning, and once you have the meaning you can forget the words.

7. A complete metaphysics begins with the positive method and ends with the negative: say all you can, then fall silent (paraphrased).

8. Taoism in three steps: Yang Zhu flees harm, Laozi sees that reversal is the movement of the Tao, Zhuangzi views all from the Tao and harm loses its grip.

9. Confucian strength lies in "knowing it can't be done and doing it anyway": duty is yours, outcome is fate's.

10. One principle, many manifestations, the moon in ten thousand rivers: grasp a principle and you should recognize it everywhere.
