Secrets to Winning at Office Politics

How to Achieve Your Goals and Increase Your Influence at Work · Marie G. McIntyre · 2005

中文 →

In One Sentence

Office politics isn't the private sport of a few schemers; it is what inevitably happens whenever people with different goals, interests and personalities try to work together. Opting out doesn't make it go away — it just lets other people decide for you. So the question is never whether to play, but for what ends and by what means: lining up your own goals with the organization's is both the cleanest and the most effective politics there is.

Where It Sits

McIntyre is an organizational psychologist who worked in corporate HR and management consulting and for years wrote a syndicated advice column, "Your Office Coach," answering readers' workplace dilemmas. This book is a standard-bearer for "ethical politics": it doesn't teach scheming like Robert Greene's The 48 Laws of Power, and it doesn't pretend that keeping your head down will get you noticed. It stands in between — admitting the power game is real, then teaching you to win it without selling your soul.

The Core Argument

  • Politics itself is neutral. Wherever there are human organizations, different goals compete for limited resources and attention. Whether a political act is good or bad depends on the goal you pursue and the means you use, not on the fact of "playing politics."
  • Forget fairness; look for leverage. Organizations aren't democracies, power is unevenly spread, and virtually every decision is subjective. In that reality, complaining that something "isn't fair" is the least effective use of your energy. Spend it where it increases your influence — results, knowledge, attitude, relationships — and outcomes start to move.
  • Winners win on both fronts. She sorts political behavior into a 2×2: helping both the organization and yourself makes you a Winner; helping the organization at your own expense, a Martyr; helping yourself while harming the organization, a Sociopath; harming both, a Dimwit. The heart of political intelligence is making your success also the organization's success.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. What politics is: not a dirty word, but a by-product of working together

What it is. The book starts by de-stigmatizing the word. Her definition, roughly: politics is what naturally happens whenever people with different goals, interests and personalities try to work together. Notice what the definition leaves out — plotting, flattery, power struggles. Those are just some of the ways politics gets played. Which project gets the budget, who gets promoted, whose proposal wins, who takes the blame for a failure: whenever the answer isn't produced by an objective formula, it passes through human judgment, preference and bargaining.

The analogy. Picture a family ordering dinner. There's one budget, everyone wants something different, someone is loud, someone holds the wallet, and someone gets along well with whoever holds the wallet. You can announce that you won't take part in such a vulgar argument — and then you'll eat what other people ordered. Stepping out of politics isn't neutrality; it's handing your vote to someone else.

Why it matters. Plenty of highly capable people stall at work precisely because they've written off "politics" wholesale as immoral, and so refuse to learn how power works or to invest in relationships. That moral fastidiousness is what she wants to dismantle: the test of a political act is its purpose and its methods, not whether it is "political." Fighting for resources for your team, building support for a good proposal, making sure your boss knows what you've delivered — all of that is politics, and good politics.

How it changes your view. You start unpacking the phrase "she's very political." Is she using influence to push things that help the organization, or trading other people's interests for her own? The first is worth learning from and the second worth guarding against — but both run on the same skills.

2. The organizational facts of life: read the table before you play the hand

The book lays out what she calls "organizational facts of life," to be accepted before any strategy is discussed:

  • Organizations are not democracies. No one hands you an equal vote; decision rights are distributed by hierarchy and function. Expecting things to be settled by a show of hands guarantees disappointment.
  • Some people have more power than others. It isn't always justified, but it's the terrain. Setting out without a map means walking into walls.
  • Virtually all decisions are subjective. Who gets promoted, whose performance is better, which plan is stronger — there may be criteria on paper, but in the end someone makes a judgment call. Since decisions are subjective, how the decision-makers see you matters as much as how well you actually perform.
  • Your boss controls much of your life. Your assignments, reviews, promotion prospects, even your daily mood depend heavily on this one person. So "managing up" isn't sucking up; it's what she treats as an essential component of political intelligence.
  • Fairness is an impossible goal. The harshest item and the most important — it gets its own section next.

How it changes your view. Together, these swap "how work ought to be" for "how work actually is." Most workplace misery lives in the gap between the two. Accepting reality isn't surrender; it's ending your argument with the terrain and starting to study it.

3. Forget fairness, look for leverage

Why fairness is impossible. Because everyone's idea of fair is different, and each one tilts toward its owner. Social psychology has a classic account, equity theory (proposed by J. Stacy Adams in the 1960s): people compare their own ratio of inputs to rewards with other people's, and feel aggrieved when they seem to come out behind. The catch is that everyone knows in detail how much they themselves put in — the late nights, the swallowed frustrations — but sees only what others get out. That's self-serving bias. On a team of ten, it's entirely possible for all ten to feel they're the one being shortchanged. A split that strikes everyone as fair simply doesn't exist.

So change the question. Don't ask "is this fair?" Ask "how much leverage do I have here?" Leverage is your ability to affect the outcome. She contrasts two kinds of people. Low-leverage people pour their energy into complaining, worrying, blaming, gossiping, scheming and whining — activities that burn energy and change no decisions. High-leverage people concentrate on producing results and building relationships.

Where leverage comes from (the sources the book names):

  • Results: visible performance. There's a feedback loop here: political power helps you get results, and results in turn build political power — people with resources find it easier to deliver, and people who deliver find it easier to get resources.
  • Knowledge / expertise: becoming the go-to person in some area; scarce expertise makes others need you.
  • Attitude: being friendly, helpful and cooperative, someone people want to work with. It sounds soft, but if decisions are subjective, it directly shapes the impression of you that sits in someone's head when they make the call.
  • Relationships: the network of allies covered below.

How it changes your view. The next time you catch yourself thinking "why him and not me?", skip the fairness ledger and run the leverage ledger: in the decision-maker's eyes, what does he have that I don't — more visible results, deeper knowledge of that part of the business, a closer relationship with the people deciding? Translate a grievance into a leverage gap, and the grievance becomes a to-do list.

4. The four political types: Winners, Martyrs, Sociopaths, Dimwits

This is the book's most memorable framework. Score any workplace behavior on two axes: does it help the organization (the business goals)? Does it help you (your personal goals)?

Helps meHurts me
Helps the organizationWinners
Personal and business goals aligned; both sides win		Martyrs
Serve the organization at great personal cost; burn out or turn bitter
Hurts the organizationSociopaths
Serve only themselves at the business's expense; win short-term, often crash		Dimwits
Driven by emotion and impulse; damage both

McIntyre's political types: what gets sorted is not a person but each specific behavior.

Martyrs deserve the closest look, because many principled people live in that box. They work themselves to the bone for the organization but won't let anyone know what they've done, won't invest in relationships, won't advocate for themselves. Pushed far enough, they start saying "it's not fair," or turn preachy until colleagues steer clear. The Martyr's problem isn't morality but sustainability: someone who burns themselves out ends up useless to the organization too.

Sociopaths (her word is a metaphor, not a clinical diagnosis) grab credit, shift blame and manipulate information. They often rise fast in the short run, which is exactly what infuriates the Martyrs. Her observation, though, is that they frequently crash at some point — they collect too many enemies along the way, and the organization eventually notices that their results were hollow.

Dimwits aren't necessarily unintelligent; they're hijacked by the emotion of the moment — openly defying the boss, firing off an angry email, bringing a personal feud into a meeting. Such moves help neither the business nor their own reputation.

Why it matters. The framework replaces the moral either/or of "do you play politics?" with a more useful question: which box does this particular behavior land in? The same person can be a Winner in the morning and a Dimwit after lunch. It also gives principled people a way out: you don't have to become a Sociopath to win; you need to shift left out of the Martyr box — keep doing things for the organization, and let those things work for you as well.

How it changes your view. Before you act, you start asking two questions: what does this do for the business? What does it do for me? Only when you can answer both is it a good move.

5. Allies and adversaries: decisions are subjective, so people are the key

What it is. If nearly every decision is subjective, then the impressions held by the decision-makers — and by the people whispering in their ears — are your political capital. The book sorts allies into three kinds:

  • Friends: people who genuinely like you and will speak up for you.
  • Partners: people whose results depend on yours, and vice versa. Relationships held together by shared stakes tend to be sturdier than goodwill alone.
  • Connections: people you don't deal with often but can call on for information or a small favor when you need it.

The analogy. Think of it as distributed storage for your reputation. It isn't stored in you; it's spread across the heads of dozens of colleagues, and every time someone mentions you in a meeting, that's a read. You can't control each read, but you can decide what gets written to those heads — through everyday cooperation, help and reliability.

Turning adversaries into allies. The book spends real time on difficult people and opponents. The core move is psychological: don't cast the other person as a villain; first work out their goals and pressures. Many "adversaries" are simply people whose goals collide with yours, not people with a grudge. Find where your goals overlap, and conflict can often become a trade.

Managing up. Your boss is your most important power relationship. The book's advice is practical: learn your boss's goals, priorities, preferred way of communicating and expectations of you, then meet them — get this one critical relationship running smoothly and the rest of your influence has a foundation. Some readers object that this section leans too hard on accommodating the boss and says too little about expanding your influence upward. That criticism is worth keeping in mind.

How it changes your view. You realize that relationships aren't an extra credit question on top of results; they're the channel through which results get seen and recognized. A result no one speaks up for is, when a subjective decision gets made, as good as invisible.

6. Political games: spot them, then don't take the bait

What it is. Part of the book deals with destructive "political games." She gives five signs that you're in one: a repeating pattern, an emotional payoff, an unstated true motive, a winner (and often a loser), and resistance when anyone tries to break it up. The idea is well read alongside the psychiatrist Eric Berne's "games" in Games People Play: on the surface one thing is happening, underneath an unspoken emotional transaction is being settled, and the players re-enact it again and again without quite noticing.

She groups the games into three families (as compiled from summaries of the book):

FamilyTypical gamesThe emotional payoff
Power gamesSuck-Up, Control, ShunningGetting close to power, holding territory, punishing the "different" with exclusion
Ego gamesSuperiority, Put-Down, In-GroupRaising yourself by lowering others
Escape gamesScapegoat, AvoidanceNever having to face responsibility or conflict

Three families of political games: each delivers a feeling of winning, and often makes the whole team lose.

A few examples. The Suck-Up player focuses on pleasing the powerful. The boss may enjoy it, but colleagues generally see such players as useless, so they seldom have decent peer relationships — and when the boss moves on, they have nothing. Control players insist on everything going their way, turning meetings into endless power struggles. Shunning is a group freezing out someone who is different; the Scapegoat player always offloads blame onto someone else.

How to respond. Games keep going because both sides keep unwittingly supplying the payoff. So the core response is to recognize the game and stop handing it the emotional reward it wants — don't join in putting someone down, don't get provoked, don't take part in a clique's freeze-out — and to check whether you're playing one yourself.

How it changes your view. Many of the work scenes that leave you seething turn out, from another angle, to be games: they have a fixed script, and the role you've been cast in is one you can decline.

7. Political suicide and the game plan: run influence like a project

Political suicide. One chapter is titled "How to Commit Political Suicide" and covers the reputation-wrecking blunders many people make without noticing. The 2×2 makes it clearest: political suicide is a string of behaviors in the Dimwit box — they hurt the business and hurt you, often for a moment's satisfaction. One summary boils it down to a single line: don't become the problem. Where decisions are subjective, once you're labeled "difficult," "a complainer" or "unreliable," your results get read at a discount.

Changing perceptions. The publisher's description promises techniques for changing the way you are perceived. The logic follows directly: perception isn't reality, but decisions are made on perception. Labels are hard to peel off once they stick, so they have to be overwritten — deliberately and consistently — with the opposite behavior.

The Political Game Plan. The last part of the book, on how to become a Winner, ends by having you write your own Political Game Plan: using the strategies covered, lay out the steps needed to reach your individual goals (paraphrased) — what you want, who can influence it, where your leverage lies now, which behaviors need to change. Her point is that influence, like any work project, can be planned, executed and reviewed, rather than left to luck or talent.

The Skeleton

1. Politics is unavoidable: people with different goals working together produce politics; refusing to take part means giving up your voice.

2. Read the terrain first: organizations aren't democracies, power is uneven, decisions are subjective, the boss looms large, fairness is unattainable.

3. So change the question: not "is it fair?" but "where is my leverage?" — results, knowledge, attitude, relationships.

4. Calibrate every behavior on two axes: good for the organization? good for me? Aim to stay in the Winner box.

5. Avoid the traps: recognize political games and deny them their payoff; don't commit political suicide in the Dimwit box.

6. Manage it actively: cultivate allies, manage up, reshape perceptions — and write it all into a game plan.

What does the book rest on? Not experimental data, but a great many real cases gathered by an organizational psychologist over years of consulting and answering readers' letters — story after story of political winners and political losers. That is its strength (concrete, usable) and its limit (see below).

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "Winning at politics means beating your colleagues." The opposite. Her Winners win on both fronts — for the organization and for themselves. Climbing by harming others puts you in her Sociopath box, and those people tend to crash.

Misreading 2: "It's a manual for sucking up." Sucking up is listed in the book as a political game, and one that often backfires: it buys a little favor above and costs you all your trust at peer level.

Misreading 3: "Accepting politics means abandoning principle." Her claim is that the morality of political behavior depends on ends and means. Principled people need political skill more, not less — otherwise good proposals lose to bad ones that are better sold.

Criticism 1: too deferential to power. Some readers note that the book's underlying message is close to "those with power always win, so adapt," with only a token warning about bullying and abuse. Others worry that calling everything "politics" risks laundering genuine manipulation as "influence skills." This criticism lands: when the organization itself is toxic, "learning to adapt" isn't the answer; leaving or fighting back is.

Criticism 2: individual fixes for structural problems. The book assumes the same behavior earns the same reward, and research doesn't back that up. The psychologist Laurie Rudman's 1998 studies found that women who promote themselves are more likely than men to be judged unlikable — the so-called backlash effect. In other words, "make your results visible" carries different costs for different people.

Criticism 3: anecdotal and fairly basic. It's a practitioner's handbook built on cases rather than systematic research, and some readers found it very elementary. For the evidence-based version, set two research lines beside it. The management scholar Gerald Ferris and colleagues developed the idea of political skill — four dimensions: social astuteness, interpersonal influence, networking ability and apparent sincerity — and meta-analyses link it positively to job performance and career success, which broadly supports McIntyre's direction. The Stanford professor Jeffrey Pfeffer, in Power (2010), is colder: he argues performance matters far less for advancement than people assume, and that being noticed by those above you and asking for what you want matter more — a real tension with McIntyre's treatment of results as a core source of leverage.

Criticism 4: the era. Published in 2005, the book predates remote work and instant messaging, and today the problem of making your work visible is sharper. The framework hasn't aged, but many of the specific scenes need translating.

Crossovers

  • Evolutionary biology · Hamilton's four kinds of social behavior (W. D. Hamilton, 1964): sort an act by whether it raises or lowers the fitness of the actor and of the recipient and you get mutual benefit, altruism, selfishness and spite, which line up almost exactly with Winners, Martyrs, Sociopaths and Dimwits. The difference: altruism can persist in evolution through kin selection (rB > C), while the book's Martyr is bound to burn out.
  • Philosophy · Epictetus's dichotomy of control: the Enchiridion opens by splitting things into what is up to us and what is not, and spending effort only on the first; "seek leverage, not fairness" makes the same cut. The difference: the Stoics put reputation and office on the not-up-to-us side and counsel indifference, while McIntyre wants you to move them.
  • Complexity science · Preferential attachment (Barabási and Albert, 1999): new nodes prefer to link to nodes that already have many links, so a few hubs emerge; "results bring power, power brings results" is the same rich-get-richer loop. The difference: preferential attachment counts only links, while the book's loop at least needs real results to get started.
  • Distributed systems · EigenTrust (Kamvar et al., 2003): in a peer-to-peer network, a node's reputation is computed from others' ratings, each weighted by the rater's own reputation, so who speaks for you matters more than how many do. The difference: EigenTrust aggregates the whole network, while at work it is often one boss taking one subjective reading.
  • Investing · Keynes's beauty contest (General Theory, 1936): entrants pick not the face they find prettiest but the one they think others will pick; "decisions are made on perception" is that second-order judgment. The difference: Keynes saw it as markets drifting from fundamentals, while McIntyre treats managing perception as legitimate work, provided real results sit underneath.

The Book in Ten Sentences

1. Politics is what naturally happens when people with different goals, interests and personalities work together (paraphrased) — not a dirty word, but a by-product of cooperation.

2. Stepping out of politics isn't neutrality; it's handing your vote to someone else.

3. Organizations aren't democracies, power is unevenly distributed, and your boss controls much of your life.

4. Virtually every decision is subjective — so how the decision-makers see you matters as much as how well you perform.

5. Fairness is an impossible goal: everyone's sense of fair tilts toward themselves.

6. Forget fairness, look for leverage: complaining, blaming and gossip are low leverage; results, knowledge, attitude and relationships are high leverage.

7. Winners help the organization and themselves; Martyrs burn out; Sociopaths win short-term and often crash; Dimwits hurt both.

8. Allies come in three kinds — friends, partners with shared stakes, connections you can call on; a result no one speaks up for is as good as invisible.

9. Political games have fixed scripts and emotional payoffs — spot them, then stop paying out.

10. Influence can be planned like a project: write down your goal, the key people, your leverage and the behaviors to change, and be someone who wins on both fronts.
