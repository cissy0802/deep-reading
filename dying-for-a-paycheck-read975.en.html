Dying for a Paycheck

Dying for a Paycheck: How Modern Management Harms Employee Health and Company Performance—and What We Can Do About It · Jeffrey Pfeffer · 2018

中文 →

In one sentence

The management practices we treat as normal—layoffs at the first sign of trouble, endless hours, no say over your own work, unfair treatment, a job that tears at your family, no health insurance—don't just make people unhappy; they make people sick and kill them early, and that harm can be measured like factory emissions and, like emissions, it is a choice companies make, not an act of nature.

Where it sits

Pfeffer is an organizational behavior professor at Stanford's business school who spent four decades asking how management affects company performance. Here he asks how management affects human lives, and the book's backbone is an epidemiological estimate he published in 2016 in Management Science with the operations researchers Joel Goh and Stefanos Zenios. The same Pfeffer who coolly teaches people how to climb in Power (Deep Read 972 on this site) turns the camera here on the people beneath the power—those with no control over their situation.

Core claims

  • The workplace itself is a major public-health problem. By his and his co-authors' estimate, roughly 120,000 excess deaths and about $180–190 billion in health spending each year in the U.S. can be attributed to how companies manage their workforces (approximate; see the method and the objections below). At that scale, if "work" were a cause of death it would rank around fifth in the country (Pfeffer's framing, paraphrased).
  • Most of this harm comes from management choices, so most of it is avoidable. Layoffs, schedules, hours, how much discretion people get, whether they are treated fairly, whether they have insurance—companies decide these things. They are not laws of nature.
  • Companies don't change because the costs are pushed onto others. Employees, their families, public insurers, and the next employer pick up the bill—exactly the structure of pollution. So a company's effect on its workers' health should be measured, reported, and held to account the way its environmental footprint is; and most corporate "wellness programs" are a bandage on the wound.

The key ideas, one by one

1. Workplace stressors: ten "exposures"

What it is. Pfeffer borrows the language of epidemiology. An exposure is anything you come into contact with that might cause disease—secondhand smoke, asbestos, lead. Researchers measure how much more likely exposed people are to get sick than unexposed people; that multiple is the relative risk. Pfeffer and his co-authors treated the psychosocial features of work as exposures and picked ten with a large research base:

ExposureWhat it means
Unemployment / layoffsLosing the job, and with it income, identity, and relationships
No health insuranceIn the U.S. coverage mostly comes with the job; without it, people skip care until it's serious
Shift workNight and rotating shifts that wreck sleep and the body clock
Long hoursCrowding out sleep, exercise, rest, and family
Job insecurityNot laid off yet, but always waiting for it
Work-family conflictJob demands and family duties squeezing each other
Low job controlLittle say over what you do, how, or when
High job demandsHeavy workload, fast pace, tight deadlines
Low social supportColleagues and bosses who don't help or back you
Organizational injusticeArbitrary decisions, unfair treatment, no one hears your side

Table: the ten workplace exposures in the Goh–Pfeffer–Zenios model. The first two are about having a job and a safety net, the middle three about how your time is taken, the last five about the human and power structure of the job.

An analogy. Picture a chemical plant. It doesn't have to poison anyone on purpose; it just dumps wastewater into the river, and people downstream slowly get sick. Between the plant and any one patient lie years and many intervening steps, so no single case can be pinned on it. Workplace stressors are the pollutants in that river: for any one person it is hard to say which job caused the heart attack, but across tens of millions of people the rise in risk shows up.

The mechanism. Pfeffer stresses two pathways. One is direct and physiological: chronic stress keeps the body's stress response switched on—blood pressure up, inflammation up, sleep worse—and over time that lands as cardiovascular disease, metabolic disease, and depression. The other is behavioral, often overlooked and just as lethal: stressed people "cope" by drinking, smoking, overeating, and using drugs, and have no time or energy left to exercise or see a doctor. Many illnesses filed under "lifestyle" start in how the work is arranged.

How it changes your view. "Work stress" stops being a private mood you fix with the right attitude and becomes an environmental exposure with a dose, a relative risk, and an epidemiology.

2. The ledger: 120,000 lives and well over a hundred billion dollars

How it was computed. This is the book's numerical pillar, and both its force and its weak points live in the method. Roughly three steps (details approximate):

  • Pool the risks. They ran a meta-analysis—combining the results of many independent studies with a common method to get a sturdier estimate—of more than two hundred published studies, yielding the relative risk of each exposure for four outcomes: poor self-rated physical health, poor self-rated mental health, physician-diagnosed conditions, and death.
  • Estimate how many are exposed. Using national survey data (such as the General Social Survey), they estimated how common each exposure is among U.S. workers and how the exposures go together—people without insurance, for example, are also more likely to feel insecure in their jobs.
  • Ask "what if the exposures were gone?" They compared the actual situation with a counterfactual—a what-if scenario in which these exposures were eliminated—and the gap in deaths and spending is the share "attributable" to the workplace. The model explicitly handled the overlap between exposures so as not to double-count.

The result. More than 120,000 excess deaths a year, and spending equal to about 5–8% of annual U.S. health costs, roughly $180–190 billion (approximate). On the mortality side, the biggest contributors were lack of insurance and unemployment/layoffs (approximate). Pfeffer puts the number in context: it exceeds many familiar killers such as diabetes or Alzheimer's, so if work were a disease it would be one of the top five or so causes of death in America (paraphrased).

Why it matters. "Work stress is bad for you" was a truism everyone nodded at and nobody acted on. Converting it into deaths and dollars moves it from personal feeling into the same table as smoking, traffic accidents, and environmental pollution. Companies make decisions only about what shows up in the numbers; this step is meant to put human lives into the numbers.

How it changes your view. A scheduling "efficiency" reform or a round of "restructuring" layoffs carries, besides the savings on the income statement, a health bill that appears on no statement at all—because it is charged to someone else.

3. Control: why the lower your rank, the sooner you die

The Whitehall studies. This is the physiological bedrock of Pfeffer's case. Whitehall is the London street where British government departments sit, and the name stands for the civil service. The Whitehall studies, led by the epidemiologist Michael Marmot, followed many thousands of British civil servants from the late 1960s onward. The group had a rare property: everyone had a job, everyone had Britain's universal health care, everyone lived in London and worked in an office—poverty, unemployment, lack of care, and dangerous manual labor were all ruled out as explanations. Yet a clear gradient emerged: men in the lowest grades (messengers, doorkeepers) died at roughly three times the rate of the top administrators (approximate), and it wasn't only the bottom that suffered—each step down the ladder brought somewhat worse health.

What drives it. The follow-up study, Whitehall II, found that classic risk factors—smoking, blood pressure, cholesterol—explained only part of the gradient; low control over one's work was a major contributor to the grade differences in coronary heart disease (Marmot and colleagues, The Lancet, 1997). Senior officials aren't healthier because they're less busy—they are often busier—but because they decide how to be busy.

The demand-control model. This matches the job demand-control model proposed by the sociologist Robert Karasek in 1979: how harmful a job is depends not just on how much it demands but on how much decision latitude you have. Cross the two axes and you get four kinds of jobs:

Figure: Karasek's demand-control model (schematic). The top-left "high strain" job—high demands, low control—is the most damaging; the same demands paired with high control can make for a demanding but healthy, growth-oriented job.

Later researchers added a third axis, social support. A job that is high-demand, low-control, and isolated—called "iso-strain"—carries the highest risk.

An analogy. On the same night drive, the person at the wheel stays calmer than the passenger watching someone else speed. Stress harms not because there is "a lot to do" but because there is a lot to do and I have no say.

How it changes your view. It partly rewrites health inequality from a question about money into a question about power. That is why one of Pfeffer's first prescriptions is to push decisions down—give people more say over schedules, methods, and pace. It costs almost nothing and hits one of the strongest risk factors directly.

4. Layoffs and economic insecurity: the most underrated harm

The health cost of a layoff. Pfeffer leans on a 2009 study by the economists Daniel Sullivan and Till von Wachter, who used Pennsylvania administrative records to follow long-tenured workers displaced in mass layoffs. In the year after losing their jobs, their death rates were 50–100% higher than comparable workers who kept theirs, and remained about 10–15% higher each year for the next twenty years—for someone laid off at 40, roughly a year to a year and a half of lost life expectancy (approximate). What is lost is not just income but identity, daily structure, and colleagues.

"Not laid off yet" hurts too. Job insecurity is an exposure of its own: after a round of cuts, the "survivors" also face higher health risks, because they now spend their days waiting to be next. The damage of a layoff spills over onto the people who keep their jobs.

Layoffs may not even buy performance. Citing research by the management scholar Wayne Cascio and others, Pfeffer argues that companies that downsize do not reliably outperform those that don't on profits or stock price (approximate)—layoffs drain experience, sap the commitment of those who remain, and force expensive rehiring when business recovers. So treating layoffs as the default way to cut costs trades employees' health for a gain that may not exist.

An example. One counterexample the book praises is Bob Chapman, CEO of the industrial-equipment maker Barry-Wehmiller: when orders collapsed in the 2008–2009 crisis, instead of layoffs he had everyone, executives included, take four weeks of unpaid furlough to share the pain (approximate).

How it changes your view. Employment security stops looking like a perk or a nostalgic attachment to lifetime employment and becomes a hard health variable.

5. The theft of time: long hours, shift work, and work-family conflict

Long hours and "always on." Email and phones erase the boundaries of work: evenings, weekends, and vacations can all be interrupted by one message. Pfeffer describes a culture that wears overwork as a badge—"I'm so busy" has become a status symbol, and in many industries (consulting, finance, tech, medicine) long hours are read as proof of loyalty and commitment rather than as a symptom of bad management. The price is lost sleep, skipped exercise and checkups, and the behavioral pathway above: getting through on alcohol, caffeine, and pills.

The extreme form: death from overwork. In the 1980s Japan coined the word karoshi for sudden death or suicide caused by chronic overwork, and the government built a system for recognizing and compensating such cases; Chinese has an equivalent term. Pfeffer uses them to make a point: when a society needs a dedicated word for being worked to death, the problem is no longer individual constitution.

Work-family conflict. It falls hardest on dual-earner couples and people caring for children or aging parents; schedules posted only a few days ahead make childcare impossible to arrange, and the harm reaches the children too.

How it changes your view. "Flexibility" is often one-way: employees must always respond to the company, while the company owes nothing to the employees' time. Healthy flexibility means employees control their own time—back to the core of idea 3.

6. Social pollution and "human sustainability"

What it is. This is the book's central analogy. In economics, an externality is a cost one party's actions impose on another without paying for it—factory pollution is the textbook case: the profits go to the factory, the coughing to the people downstream. Pfeffer argues that unhealthy workplaces produce a kind of social pollution: the profits go to the company, the illness, medical bills, and broken families to employees and society.

Why companies needn't care. The costs land in four places: on employees themselves (out-of-pocket care, early death); on families (caregiving, effects on children); on public budgets (public insurance, disability benefits); and on the next employer—in a labor market with constant turnover, a body worn out at one company often breaks down at the next one, or in retirement, and the original employer almost never pays.

Human sustainability. Pfeffer points out a contrast: companies work hard to report their carbon emissions and water use, yet "sustainability" almost always means the natural environment, not the people the company employs. He wants human sustainability on the same ledger: a company's effect on its employees' physical and mental health should be measured, disclosed, and compared, like emissions.

How it changes your view. The history of environmental protection shows that pollution doesn't fall because companies grow a conscience; it falls because it gets measured, made public, and regulated. Pfeffer carries that path straight into workplace health.

7. Bandages and the illusion of choice: why the problem persists

Wellness programs are bandages. Many companies respond with workplace wellness programs: gym subsidies, step challenges, mindfulness classes. Pfeffer's critique is that they redefine the problem as the employee's personal lifestyle while leaving the work arrangements that generate the stress untouched—like handing a quit-smoking pamphlet to someone who spent all day breathing secondhand smoke. The evaluations he cites show that many such programs do little to improve health or save money (approximate).

"If you don't like it, quit" is an illusion. The standard rebuttal is that employees are volunteers and can leave. Pfeffer's answer is that choice is hemmed in—mortgages, families to feed—and in the U.S. there is "job lock": insurance is tied to the job, so switching can leave a chronically ill family member uncovered. Besides, it is hard to escape the norms of an entire industry: if every investment bank expects eighty-hour weeks, changing banks changes nothing.

Why leaders don't change. Health effects are delayed and hard to attribute; managers are often the winners of this very culture and believe "I made it through, so should they"; and the measurement systems see only short-term costs.

8. The cure: measure, give control, stop treating people as disposable costs

Pfeffer's prescriptions work on three levels:

  • Companies: first measure employee health (as you would safety incidents) and hold managers accountable for it; push decisions down on schedules and methods; make layoffs a last resort rather than the default; provide insurance and sane hours. Positive examples in the book include the software company SAS, long known for low turnover, on-site health care, and reasonable hours, and the outdoor brand Patagonia, an early provider of on-site child care and other family support (approximate).
  • Policy: many problems need public policy—most obviously insurance: in countries with universal coverage, the "no insurance" exposure barely exists; add labor protections on hours and schedule notice.
  • Individuals: his blunt advice to employees is if you can, leave jobs that are damaging your health, and stop treating endurance as a virtue; and, as consumers, job seekers, and investors, factor in how employers treat their people.

His position throughout: none of this requires sacrificing performance—healthier employees with more control tend to be more engaged, quit less, and cost less in health care. That is what the "company performance" in the subtitle means.

The skeleton

The whole book compresses into one line: workplaces are full of psychosocial "exposures" → each is shown by a large body of research to raise the risk of illness and death, through chronic stress plus unhealthy coping → add them up and some 120,000 deaths and well over a hundred billion dollars in U.S. health spending a year are attributable to management practices → one of the strongest factors is control, so the health gradient is also a power gradient → the harm goes unowned because, like pollution, its costs are externalized, while wellness programs treat symptoms and the "freedom to quit" is an illusion → so govern it the way we govern pollution: measure, disclose, hold accountable, and redesign work at the level of companies, policy, and individuals.

What holds it up? A numerical pillar (the Goh–Pfeffer–Zenios estimate), a physiological foundation (Whitehall and Karasek's model), some studies with stronger causal designs (layoffs and mortality), plus many interviews and company cases. The numbers say how bad it is, the mechanisms say why, and the cases say it doesn't have to be this way.

Common misreadings & criticisms

Misreading 1: Pfeffer says work is harmful and not working is better. The opposite. Unemployment itself is among the most damaging of the ten exposures. His claim is that bad jobs are harmful, and whether a job is bad depends on how it is managed.

Misreading 2: This is about personal resilience. No. The whole book takes aim at turning structural problems into individual ones. Meditation and exercise are good for you, but offering them as the solution is exactly the "bandage" he criticizes.

Misreading 3: The 120,000 is a body count. It isn't. It is a model's estimate of "attributable" excess deaths, carrying a string of assumptions and a wide uncertainty band—an order of magnitude, not a vital-statistics report.

Criticism 1: causal inference. The weightiest objection. The meta-analysis pools mostly observational studies: people in poor health are more likely to end up in low-control, unstable, uninsured jobs to begin with (reverse causation), and low income and education shape both jobs and health (confounding). How much of each relative risk is "work causing illness" and how much is "sick people and bad jobs ending up together" the model cannot fully separate. Whitehall removes some confounders, but it isn't a randomized experiment.

Criticism 2: an unrealistic counterfactual. "What if all these exposures vanished?" is an unreachable baseline—an economy will always have some unemployment and some jobs that need night shifts (hospitals, the power grid). A number computed against that baseline measures the total size of the problem, not the number of lives a company could save by changing course. Different assumptions—the relative risks used, how exposures correlate, how reliable self-rated health is—move the result considerably.

Criticism 3: very American. "No health insurance," the largest contributor on the mortality side, is barely a workplace issue in most rich countries; it is a peculiarly American institutional arrangement. Which leads to the next point.

Criticism 4: whose responsibility? Pfeffer aims mainly at employers, but insurance coverage, unemployment protection, and limits on hours are fundamentally public-policy choices. Critics argue that in competitive markets a single firm that unilaterally treats workers better may lose out, and the more effective lever is rules, not conscience; Pfeffer doesn't deny the role of policy, but the book's center of gravity remains an appeal to business leaders.

Criticism 5: the evidence for the cure is weaker than for the disease. The evidence on harm is fairly solid; the evidence on which interventions improve health without hurting performance comes mostly from individual company stories, open to survivorship bias. Research after the book (not in it) partly supports him: a 2021 joint WHO–ILO estimate linked working 55 or more hours a week to hundreds of thousands of stroke and heart-disease deaths worldwide each year, and a randomized trial of a workplace wellness program at the University of Illinois, published in 2019, found no measurable improvement in health or spending.

Pfeffer beyond this book

Read only this book and Pfeffer looks like a health advocate; read only Power and he looks like a cynical coach in office politics. Both books come from the same person and the same thread.

The Human Equation (1998). This is the source of the "company performance" half of the argument. Pfeffer assembled evidence that a set of high-performance management practices produces lasting competitive advantage, and first on the list is employment security (only people who aren't about to be let go will commit and share the ideas that make work more efficient), along with selective hiring, self-managed teams, comparatively high pay tied to organizational performance, extensive training, reduced status distinctions, and wide information sharing. Twenty years later, Dying for a Paycheck adds the other side to that "good management pays" argument: bad management also kills.

Leadership BS (2015) and evidence-based management. Pfeffer has long attacked the leadership industry for selling inspiring stories without evidence, and with Robert Sutton in Hard Facts (2006) he championed evidence-based management: like evidence-based medicine, decide on the basis of research rather than fashionable ideas. Using epidemiology to tally the human cost of workplaces is that idea in practice.

The quiet link to Power. Power opens with the Whitehall studies to show that power is tied to lifespan, and urges readers to go get some. This book draws a second conclusion from the same study: if control is a matter of life and death, organizations owe some of it to the people who can't fight for it. One is advice to the individual, the other a demand on institutions; underneath is the same fact.

Crossovers

  • Evolution · Sapolsky's baboons: Robert Sapolsky tracked wild baboons in Kenya and found that low-ranking males had chronically elevated stress hormones. When the most aggressive males of one troop died of tuberculosis from tainted meat at a garbage dump, the troop turned gentler, low-rankers' stress fell, and the culture persisted for years. Alike: rank wears down the body in our primate cousins too. Unlike: the baboons changed by accident; an organization's culture can be designed.
  • Psychology · learned helplessness, reversed: Seligman found that dogs given inescapable shocks stopped trying to escape. In 2016 he and Steven Maier reinterpreted it: passivity is the default, and control is something the prefrontal cortex learns to detect, which then damps the stress response. Alike: control itself is the remedy. Unlike: the shocks were an experiment; low control at work comes dressed up as "process."
  • Distributed systems · queueing and utilization: queueing theory says that as a server's utilization approaches 100%, wait times blow up roughly as ρ/(1−ρ), so engineers keep slack. Alike: an organization that loads people to the limit breaks at the first fluctuation. Unlike: a saturated server recovers on reboot; people accumulate damage.
  • Buddhism · the second arrow: the Sallatha Sutta says the untrained person, struck by a first arrow (physical pain), shoots a second into himself (resistance and anxiety). Mindfulness courses address the second arrow. Alike: both separate suffering from reaction. Unlike: Pfeffer's whole point is the first arrow—and the employer fired it.
  • Philosophy · Marx on the working day: the chapter on "The Working-Day" in volume one of Capital draws on English factory inspectors' reports to show capital seizing the time workers need to stay healthy, and records the fight for the ten-hour day. Alike: working hours as a battleground of health and power. Unlike: then it was physical exhaustion; now it is mostly psychosocial strain.

Ten sentences to keep

1. The workplace is a public-health problem: "normal" practices like long hours, layoffs, low control, and unfairness are making people sick and killing them early.

2. By the Goh–Pfeffer–Zenios estimate, about 120,000 excess deaths a year in the U.S., and spending equal to 5–8% of health costs, are attributable to how companies manage people (approximate).

3. Workplace stressors can be studied as "exposures," like secondhand smoke: no single case can be pinned down, but the rise in population risk can be measured.

4. The harm travels two roads: chronic stress damaging the body directly, and the drinking, smoking, overeating, and lost sleep and care people resort to in order to cope.

5. Whitehall shows that even when everyone has a job and health care, the lower the grade the earlier the death, and lack of control over one's work is a key reason.

6. What hurts isn't "a lot to do" but "a lot to do and no say"; pushing decisions down costs almost nothing and hits the strongest risk factor.

7. Layoffs raise the death rates of those let go for years, harm those who stay, and often don't even improve performance.

8. An unhealthy workplace is social pollution: the profits go to the company, the illness and the bills to employees, families, the public purse, and the next employer.

9. Wellness programs are bandages and "just quit" is an illusion of choice; pollution has never fallen through conscience, only through measurement, disclosure, and accountability.

10. The numbers carry model assumptions and an American accent, but the core judgment holds: human health belongs on a company's sustainability ledger, right next to carbon.
