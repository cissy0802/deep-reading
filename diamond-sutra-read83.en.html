The Diamond Sutra

Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra · Chinese version by Kumārajīva, c. 402 CE · about 5,000 characters

中文 →

In One Sentence

This five-thousand-word text performs exactly one operation, over and over: it pries open, finger by finger, every grip your mind has taken — starting with the most respectable grips, the ones that don't feel like clinging at all: "I am practising," "I helped them," "I understand the teaching," "I have earned merit." It hands you no new belief. It hands you a technique for dismantling beliefs — and then tells you to go on giving, go on doing good, and simply stop keeping the ledger.

Where It Sits

The Sanskrit title, Vajracchedikā Prajñāpāramitā Sūtra, means roughly "the perfection of wisdom that cuts like a diamond" — prajñā (the insight that sees things as they actually are) working like a vajra, the adamantine weapon of Indian myth that nothing can resist and that cuts through anything. It belongs to the Perfection of Wisdom literature, the strand of Mahāyāna Buddhism devoted to emptiness (its other famous short text is the Heart Sutra). Kumārajīva (344–413) produced the Chinese version around 402 CE in Chang'an, and it has been among the most recited scriptures in East Asian Buddhism for sixteen centuries. From the Fifth Patriarch onward, Chan (Zen) adopted it as the text of transmission in place of the Laṅkāvatāra Sūtra, and Huineng — the Sixth Patriarch — is said to have broken through on hearing a single line of it, which is why you cannot really read Chinese Chan without passing through this book first. A printed copy recovered at Dunhuang, dated 868 CE, is the earliest surviving printed book bearing a definite date.

The Central Claims

  • Every "mark" — every form, concept, identity — is a dependent, provisional posit with no fixed core. Whatever you can point at, name, and set up as a thing is already not the thing: "all marks are illusory."
  • The teaching itself is also a mark, and must also be released. The dharma is a raft; the person still hauling it overland after the crossing is harder to help than the one who never boarded. "Even the teaching must be let go — how much more what is not the teaching."
  • Emptiness is not inaction; it is action without a ledger. The text is explicit: "with no self, no person, no being, no life-span, cultivate every good thing — and that is awakening." The self that scores the action is what gets cancelled, not the action.

The Core Ideas, One by One

It opens with a meal: how the question gets asked

A scripture about emptiness begins with something close to tedium: the Buddha puts on his robe, takes his bowl, walks into the city of Śrāvastī to beg, goes from door to door in order — not skipping the poor houses or favouring the rich ones — returns, eats, puts the bowl away, washes his feet, arranges his seat, and sits down.

This is not throat-clearing before the real content. It is the answer, performed before it is stated. The "non-abiding" the whole text will spend five thousand words on has already been fully demonstrated here: eating is eating, washing feet is washing feet, with no thought of "I am the World-Honoured One, displaying proper deportment" hanging off any of it. The later Zen line — when hungry, eat; when tired, sleep — begins in this paragraph.

Once he is seated, the elder Subhūti (one of the Buddha's ten chief disciples, traditionally ranked first in understanding emptiness) stands up and asks the one question the sutra contains: for someone who has resolved to awaken, "where should the mind abide, and how should it be subdued?"

Notice the shape of the question. It smuggles in two assumptions: that there exists a place to abide, and that there exists a thing that needs subduing. Everything that follows is less an answer than a slow demolition of the question's foundations. What the Buddha finally offers is not a better resting place but no resting place; not a way to pin the mind down, but the dissolution of the "I" that wanted to do the pinning.

The four marks: taking the self apart in four layers

The Buddha's first move is a spectacular promise immediately withdrawn. A bodhisattva vows to liberate every being without exception — "and having liberated countless, measureless, boundless beings, no being whatsoever has been liberated."

Why? "If a bodhisattva holds the mark of a self, of a person, of a being, or of a life-span, he is not a bodhisattva." These four marks (saṃjñā: the fixed image the mind congeals around something) are the sutra's standing target. They are not four synonyms but four strata of self-awareness, coarse to fine:

The sharp part is this: the four marks grow fastest precisely when you are doing good. Donate, rescue, teach, and all four get fed at once — the giver (self), the recipient (person), the group I served (being), the things I have done with my years (life-span). Which is why the text plants its knife in the highest vow of all: someone who has genuinely helped does not experience himself as having helped; the moment "the beings I saved" stands up in your mind, the "I" has already walked back in.

The most brutal illustration is the Buddha's account of a former life, dismembered limb by limb by a tyrant king: "at that time I had no mark of self, no mark of person, no mark of being, no mark of life-span — for had I held them while being cut apart joint by joint, anger would have arisen." He felt no rage not because his patience was heroic, but because the injured party never got constituted. The far end of patience is not gritted teeth; it is that there is nobody there doing the enduring.

"A is not A, therefore it is called A": the engine of the book

The Diamond Sutra reads like a tongue-twister because it runs the same formula again and again. The Japanese scholar D. T. Suzuki called it the logic of sokuhi — the logic of "is not":

The three-part formula turns up on nearly every page. It is not wordplay; it is a machine for levering nouns off their pedestals.
The lineUnpacked
The perfection of wisdom is not the perfection of wisdom; that is why it is called the perfection of wisdomThere is no entity named "highest wisdom" — we just need something to call it
A world is not a world; that is why it is called a world"World" isn't one solid object but a temporary convergence of conditions we gave a name to
What is called the Buddha's teaching is not the Buddha's teachingEven that label points at nothing anyone can own
Adorning a buddha-field is not adorning; that is why it is called adorningWhoever is really building holds no thought of "I am doing a thing called building"

How can a sentence like that not be simple contradiction? Because the "A" being denied and the "A" being kept are not on the same level. The first A is the entity you assumed — something with a fixed core, ownable, pointable-at. The middle clause cuts exactly that core out. The third clause returns the word to you as a working label: life continues, speech continues, you simply now know it is a name.

An analogy: a glass of water is perfectly real, but there is no thing called "glass-of-water-ness" living inside the water. You still drink it, you still call it water; you have merely stopped believing there is a nugget of water-essence at the centre. Madhyamaka — the school founded by Nāgārjuna a century or two later — systematised this as the two truths: conventional truth, where labels work perfectly well, and ultimate truth, where no intrinsic nature can be located. The sutra never uses that vocabulary, but this is what it is doing.

What it changes: you acquire a portable move. Whenever you notice a word has you by the throat — "I'm a failure," "this was a humiliation," "I have to become X" — run the three parts on the spot: What is it? There is no core there. Then why keep the word? Because you need a name to get on with the day. What binds you was never the situation; it was mistaking the name for the situation.

Give rise to a mind that dwells nowhere

This is the most quoted line in the book and the one that broke Huineng open. Describing how a bodhisattva should generate a clear mind, the text says: do not give rise to a mind that dwells on sights, on sounds, smells, tastes, touch, or mental objects — give rise to a mind that dwells nowhere.

Start with the verb. Dwell here does not mean "live in"; it means to settle on, adhere to, hang off. The six items listed are the standard Buddhist inventory of everything the mind can take as an object: what the eye sees, the ear hears, the nose smells, the tongue tastes, the body feels, and what thought itself constructs. So the whole line reads: let the mind arise without lodging on any perception or any thought.

The easy misreading is "don't let your mind move" — which would make you a stone. The text says give rise to the mind: it is supposed to arise, live, function. What is negated is not the arising but the lodging. The analogy: a mirror reflects whoever stands before it, perfectly and in full detail; when they leave, not a trace remains in the glass. The mirror isn't refusing to reflect; it is refusing to store. Or: the surface of water can rise into waves, but no wave gets stuck at one spot.

Why would one line crack open an illiterate woodcutter? Because it dissolves the practitioner's deepest knot in a single stroke. The harder you try to abide in a good state — calm, clear, focused — the more surely that state becomes the next thing you clutch. "Dwelling nowhere" is not a better place to stand; it removes the premise that you need somewhere to stand. The question stops being "where should I put my mind" and becomes "who said it has to be put anywhere?"

Giving without marks: emptiness is not inaction, it's action without a ledger

This is the most easily skipped section, and the one that keeps the whole book from sliding into nihilism. For a text about emptiness, everything lands on dāna — giving, the first of the six perfections: "a bodhisattva should practise giving without dwelling on anything."

Read the phrase in two halves: the giving is mandatory; the "without marks" is how. It means holding no thought of I gave, to whom, how much, what is owed back. The payoff is stated as a deliberate paradox: give without dwelling on marks and the merit is beyond measure — precisely because nothing is being measured.

There is an entirely unmystical mechanism here. The instant you record "I helped him," you have opened an account receivable. No thanks arrives, the return disappoints, the person later hurts you — the books go out of balance and the original kindness sours into resentment. "Without marks" is not a demand for moral loftiness; it is a way of lifting a good act out of the structure of exchange so that it cannot mature into a debt.

The text also runs a comparison repeatedly: filling a universe with treasure and giving it all away generates less merit than holding four lines of this scripture and explaining them to someone. The logic is that treasure changes circumstances, whereas seeing changes the machine that manufactures suffering. (These self-praising passages take up a substantial share of the book and are also its most criticised feature — see below.)

The decisive anti-nihilist sentence comes in the second half: "This dharma is level, with nothing higher or lower... with no self, no person, no being, no life-span, cultivate every good thing — and that is awakening." What follows selflessness is not lying down. It is cultivating every good thing, by someone who has stopped counting.

All marks are illusory: how to "see" what cannot be seen

The Buddha asks Subhūti whether the Tathāgata can be recognised by the thirty-two auspicious bodily marks a buddha is traditionally said to bear. Subhūti says no. The Buddha then delivers one of the book's four most-quoted lines: "All marks are illusory. Whoever sees that marks are not marks sees the Tathāgata."

Illusory is regularly misread as "non-existent, fake." The more accurate sense is insubstantial: phenomena genuinely appear, but without the solidity you attribute to them — the way a rainbow is unquestionably there and yet you can never reach its foot or pick up a piece of it. "Seeing that marks are not marks" does not mean going blind to appearances; it means seeing the appearance and its insubstantiality at the same time. Look at the rainbow all you like; just stop sending a crew to dig up its base.

A later verse puts it harder: whoever looks for me in form, or seeks me in sound, is walking a false path and will not see the Tathāgata. Pair it with another line — the Tathāgata comes from nowhere and goes nowhere, which is why the name means "thus come" — and the point lands: the word does not designate a person travelling between places; it names reality just as it is, and reality occupies no location, so there is nothing for it to arrive from or depart to.

The knife's sharpest stroke is the one it turns on itself. Here is a religious text taking apart the very object its devotees most want to hold: the figure of the Buddha. It even says that anyone who claims the Tathāgata taught a doctrine is slandering him. The reasoning is consistent throughout: the moment "the teaching" is set up as something sayable and ownable, it becomes one more mark.

The raft: even the teaching must be let go

"Know that my teaching is like a raft. Even the teaching must be let go — how much more what is not the teaching."

The image has to be taken all the way or it stays a pretty line. A raft's entire value lies in a function — getting across — and not in being a fine object. Between one bank and the other it is indispensable; on dry land, the person still carrying it on his shoulders has converted a tool into a burden. Buddhist teaching is teaching because it cures a specific clinging; the moment you cling to it instead, it stops being medicine and becomes the disease.

Which is why the next line is "grasp neither the teaching nor the negation of the teaching." That second half matters just as much: announcing that everything is empty so nothing need be believed is grasping the negation as a new doctrine — the same illness wearing a different face.

How this changes what you see: any system that has made you better — a therapy, a methodology, a teacher, an identity — deserves one question. Am I using this to cross, or am I ashore and unable to put it down? The test is simple: when someone attacks the system, do you feel "we can discuss that," or do you feel personally insulted? The second means the raft has grown into your shoulders.

Three minds ungraspable, and the closing verse: time gets dismantled too

Everything so far has dismantled the object side. The last stroke goes after the subject — the mind doing the grasping. "Past mind cannot be got hold of; present mind cannot be got hold of; future mind cannot be got hold of."

Try it once and it is obvious. The thought just past has ceased, so you cannot catch it. The future one hasn't arisen, so there is nothing to catch. And the present? The instant you turn to seize it, it has become the past. What you call "my mind" was never a continuous thing; it is a stream of arising and vanishing moments that memory has stitched into a line. And if no enduring mind can be found, then Subhūti's opening question — how should the mind be subdued? — has quietly lost its subject. There is no fixed mind sitting there waiting to be subdued.

A famous Chan anecdote (a later story, not part of the sutra) catches the force of the line. Deshan, renowned as a lecturer on this very text, stopped to buy a snack from an old woman by the road. She said: answer one question about the Diamond Sutra and the food is free. If all three minds are ungraspable, which mind are you planning to refresh? He had no answer.

The book closes on a verse: "All conditioned things are like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow, like dew, like lightning — regard them thus." Conditioned things means everything produced by causes, everything that arises and passes — that is, everything you will ever experience. Each simile bites a different edge: a dream is utterly convincing while it lasts and unlocatable on waking; an illusion makes your pulse jump even when you know it is a trick; a bubble bursts as it forms; a shadow has shape but no substance and moves when the light does; dew is gone by mid-morning; lightning is blinding and lasts an instant.

The operative words are the last three: regard them thus. This is not a sigh about the vanity of life, it is an instruction — look this way. Seeing impermanence clearly does not produce indifference; the opposite. If a thing is truly happening only once and is about to break, that is exactly what makes it worth being entirely present for. Which is the posture of the whole book: dwell nowhere, and let the mind arise.

The Argument in Outline

The Diamond Sutra builds no system, mounts no argument, and asserts almost nothing. It works more like a single surgical procedure repeated on different parts of the body. The through-line:

  • It begins with a wrong question. Subhūti asks where the mind should abide and how it should be subdued — two assumptions in one sentence. The rest of the book removes both.
  • First cut, the object: all marks are illusory. Bodies, worlds, beings, particles, the thirty-two marks of a buddha — anything identifiable as a thing gets levered loose by the "A is not A, therefore called A" machine: name yes, core no.
  • Second cut, the subject: the four marks. Self, person, being, life-span are four shells around one "I," and they thicken fastest during good deeds. So the knife goes into the highest vow itself: liberate every being, and no being has been liberated.
  • Third cut, the instrument: the raft. Even the teaching is not to be held — and neither is its negation, so you are denied even that handrail.
  • Fourth cut, time: the three minds. The grasping mind is not a thing either, at which point the opening question dissolves completely: there is no mind that needs subduing.
  • And after the demolition, not idling — doing. "With no self, no person, no being, no life-span, cultivate every good thing." Keep giving, keep practising, without marks and without a ledger. That single positive instruction is the whole constructive content; everything else is clearing ground.
  • What actually persuades? Honestly, not argument. It is a linguistic operation performed on the reader: each time you are about to close your hand around a conclusion, the next sentence withdraws it. What you are left with at the end is not information but a grip that has been pried open many times in a row. That is why it has lasted sixteen centuries — and also where it is most vulnerable.

Misreadings, Criticisms, Live Disputes

  • The big misreading: taking emptiness for "nothing exists." The text guards against it explicitly: one who has set out toward awakening "does not assert annihilation." Annihilationism — the view that nothing survives, that cause and effect are void — has always been treated inside Buddhism as more dangerous than clinging to existence. What emptiness denies is svabhāva, intrinsic self-existing nature, not phenomena or causation. Nāgārjuna's line makes the point: it is because things are empty that anything can work at all — change, causation, and practice are possible only because nothing has a fixed core.
  • Does the "A is not A" formula actually hold up? Read as formal logic it violates non-contradiction outright, and critics have long dismissed it as mystification or as unfalsifiable by design. The traditional repair is the level distinction of the two truths: what is denied is A-as-entity, what is kept is A-as-convention. That repair is a later Madhyamaka systematisation; the sutra itself supplies none. Stated fairly: the account is internally consistent, but to anyone who insists that claims be testable it reads as persuasion technique rather than argument — and that disagreement has never been settled.
  • "Even the teaching must be let go" is the line most often stolen as a licence for laziness. The strain of showy, undisciplined Chan that appeared in later Tang China — scorning scripture and precept as mere words while doing neither study nor practice — drew cover from exactly such sentences. Remember the sequence in the image: you drop the raft after the crossing, not instead of boarding. Someone who abandons the raft on this bank hasn't transcended anything; he is still standing where he started.
  • The merit passages read like advertising. Large stretches of the text insist that holding four lines of it outweighs universes of treasure, and that wherever the sutra is kept, a buddha is present. Modern readers find this unseemly. There is a well-known scholarly reading: Gregory Schopen built his "cult of the book" thesis partly on the line declaring that any spot where the sutra is taught should be honoured like a stupa — Perfection of Wisdom texts were shifting the object of veneration from relic-mounds to the scripture itself, a practical move for an expanding movement. In other words, those passages may be doing missionary work rather than carrying the doctrine.
  • The lines you have memorised may not be what the original said. Kumārajīva's version is famous for its literary quality: it paraphrases where it must to read well. The clearest case is the closing verse. Kumārajīva gives six similes — dream, illusion, bubble, shadow, dew, lightning — while the Sanskrit and Xuanzang's more literal seventh-century Chinese version have nine, adding a star, an eye-floater, a lamp, and a cloud. The six-simile verse is beloved partly because of Kumārajīva's cutting and cadence; it is a translator's literary choice, not the letter of the original. Likewise the division into thirty-two numbered sections with headings is traditionally credited to Prince Zhaoming of the Liang dynasty in the sixth century — editorial work, not original structure.
  • Authorship and date. The text opens "thus have I heard," formally presenting itself as a record of the historical Buddha's words. Scholarship generally holds that the Perfection of Wisdom scriptures were composed and compiled between roughly the first century BCE and the second century CE, the Diamond Sutra emerging alongside the rise of Mahāyāna, quite possibly in the Gandhāra region. Reading it as a distillation of Mahāyāna wisdom thought is safer than reading it as a transcript — which subtracts nothing from it, just as the layered composition of the Platform Sutra subtracts nothing from Chan.
  • If there's no self, who carries the consequences? This is one of Buddhism's oldest internal problems: with no enduring self, who acts, who reaps, who awakens? The Madhyamaka answer is that causation holds perfectly at the level of dependent arising and requires no substantial bearer — the way a flame travelling along a fuse is not the same flame from moment to moment and yet is genuinely a causal sequence. Whether that satisfies has been argued for eighteen centuries; but it is at least an answer and not an evasion.
  • A reservation a modern reader should keep: the position is unfalsifiable. "You haven't seen it yet because you are still dwelling on marks" is immune to every counterexample. As a path of practice this is potent; as a claim about the world it is nearly untestable. The pragmatic reading is to take what it does to clinging — a part you can verify in your own experience — and leave its metaphysics as an open question.

Ten Lines to Keep

1. The book's only question is Subhūti's — where should the mind abide, how should it be subdued? — and the answer is that both assumptions fail: there is no place, and no fixed mind needing subjection.

2. "All marks are illusory; whoever sees that marks are not marks sees the Tathāgata" — not an instruction to stop seeing, but to see the appearance and its insubstantiality at once.

3. The "A is not A, therefore called A" machine does one job: cut out the entity behind the word and hand the word back as a working label. What binds you is never the situation, only mistaking the name for the situation.

4. Self, person, being, life-span are four shells around one "I" — and they thicken fastest while you are doing good.

5. "No being whatsoever has been liberated" — whoever truly helped does not experience himself as helping; the moment "the people I saved" appears, the "I" is back.

6. "Give rise to a mind that dwells nowhere" is not deadening the mind but letting it arise without adhering: the mirror reflects fully and stores nothing.

7. Giving without marks is not moral loftiness. Record "I helped him" and you have opened an account receivable — and on the day the books fail to balance, the kindness turns to resentment.

8. "Even the teaching must be let go, how much more what is not" — a raft is for crossing, and carrying it overland is a burden; but note the order: you drop it after the crossing, not instead of boarding.

9. "Past mind, present mind, future mind — none can be got hold of": you cannot catch the present, because by the time you turn to seize it, it has become the past.

10. "All conditioned things are like a dream, an illusion, a bubble, a shadow, like dew, like lightning — regard them thus." The instruction is in those last words: not a sigh at impermanence but a way of looking. Precisely because it happens once and is about to break, it deserves all of you.
