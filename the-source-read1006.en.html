The Source

The Source: Open Your Mind, Change Your Life (US subtitle: The Secrets of the Universe, the Science of the Brain) · Tara Swart · 2019 & The Signs · 2025

中文 →

In One Sentence

The Law of Attraction says the universe delivers whatever you hold in mind; Swart's translation is that the universe isn't listening — your brain is filtering: when a goal is seen again and again and tagged with strong emotional value, the brain's attention filter starts picking out related opportunities from the world on your behalf, and neuroplasticity means repeated practice can slowly rewire your habits of mind. "Manifesting" is getting attention, emotion and action to point the same way.

Where It Sits

Swart trained in medicine and psychiatry, holds a PhD in neuroscience, has taught at MIT Sloan and has worked for years as an executive coach. The book is one of the best-known recent attempts to rewrite "think it and it will come" spirituality in the language of brain science; her 2025 book The Signs extends the same claims to intuition and the "signals" life sends you, and is folded in here.

The Core Argument

  • The Law of Attraction is not magic but a misdescription of two real brain functions: selective attention and value tagging. Wishing doesn't change the world; it changes what you see in the world and what you care about.
  • The brain is plastic, but change is slow, costly in energy, and requires repetition. One epiphany doesn't rewire anything; what lasts is the pathway practised and used again and again. Backsliding is normal, not failure.
  • A brain that runs on logic alone is half a brain. She argues for "whole-brain" thinking — emotion, motivation, bodily awareness, intuition, logic and creativity used in balance — all resting on a brain that is looked after: hydration, nutrition, rest, stress management.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Selective attention: the world you see is a filtered world

What it is: every second, far more information reaches your senses than your brain can process, so it has to choose what gets in. Selective attention is that filter: whatever bears on your current goals, identity and worries is amplified; everything else is turned down until it is nearly invisible.

An analogy: after you decide to buy a particular car, suddenly the streets are full of them — there aren't more of them; your filter has changed its settings. Two classic findings in psychology make the mechanism concrete (they aren't Swart's discoveries, but the common ground her argument stands on). One is the cocktail party effect: in a noisy room you can't follow anyone, yet the moment someone says your name, you hear it. The other is inattentional blindness: in Simons and Chabris's 1999 study, people counting basketball passes in a video failed, about half the time, to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the middle of the scene.

Swart's inference: if the filter decides which opportunities you can see, then setting a clear goal and keeping it in front of you is a way of tuning the filter's parameters. Her examples are everyday ones: want a promotion badly and you start noticing which projects nobody has claimed in meetings and which manager is looking for someone; take up a new kind of music and you find it is playing everywhere. That isn't the universe answering; the information was always there and you have finally tuned in.

Why it matters: it extracts the one defensible part of "think it and it will come" — a goal doesn't create opportunities, but it decides whether you can see them. It also marks the limit: the filter can only amplify what is already out there.

How it changes the way you see: you realise that "there are no opportunities around me" is at least partly a statement about your filter rather than the world — and you grow a little warier of the feeling that evidence is everywhere, which may be nothing but the filter at work.

2. Value tagging: the brain sticks an emotional price tag on everything

What it is: the brain doesn't merely record information; it attaches value to it — is this good or bad for me, and how much does it matter? Swart calls this value tagging. Things tagged high are noticed, remembered and pursued more readily; things tagged negative are avoided, even when you know rationally they would do you good.

Her example: if family walks at the weekend were a warm childhood memory, "let's go for a walk" appeals to you as an adult without effort; if walks were bound up with arguments, compulsion or boredom, the very same activity makes you instinctively resist. The tags were often stuck on long ago, without your noticing, and they have been making your choices ever since.

That is why her method starts with awareness: you need to dig out the old tags — the role you played in your family, its unspoken rules and secrets, the beliefs about money and success you inherited from your parents — before you can decide which to peel off and replace. She calls them limiting beliefs, such as "people like me don't make real money" or "showing vulnerability gets you looked down on."

Selective attention and value tagging together are her mechanism for manifesting: the first decides what you see; the second decides how much drive you have to act on what you see. Fix the goal without changing the tags and you will notice opportunities but lack the energy to grab them.

How it changes the way you see: when you "know you should but just don't want to," the problem may not be willpower but an old price tag on that thing you have never noticed. The way in is to re-price it — to connect it to what you actually care about.

3. Abundance versus lack: a fear-driven brain can't see far

What it is: Swart restates the Law of Attraction as a handful of principles, the most central being abundance — the belief that there are enough resources and opportunities to go round — set against a "lack" mindset: there's never enough, and anything someone else gets comes out of my share.

Her brain-science reason: the brain responds more strongly to a loss than to an equal gain, roughly twice as strongly — what behavioural economics calls loss aversion. A brain dominated by a sense of lack lives on the defensive, its attention narrowed to threats, so it sees fewer opportunities and dares fewer risks. Her prescription is to reframe failure — treat each setback as information for improvement rather than evidence of loss — and, when a problem looms, to ask how much it will matter five years from now.

Linked to this is her sorting of emotions into "survival" emotions (such as fear, anger, disgust, shame and sadness, which make you flee or fight) and "attachment" emotions (such as love and joy, which draw you toward others). Both drive behaviour, but run on the first kind for too long and you burn out and your field of view shrinks.

How it changes the way you see: "abundance" sounds like a greeting-card slogan, but its testable core is plain — people in threat mode make short-sighted decisions. You may not believe your way into wealth, but you can notice when you are deciding out of fear, and postpone the decision.

4. Neuroplasticity: you can change, but slowly

What it is: the adult brain has around 86 billion neurons, and the connections between them change with experience — neuroplasticity. Swart lists several ways it happens: new connections forming and strengthening (often summed up as "neurons that fire together wire together," from the psychologist Donald Hebb's theory), unused connections being pruned, and neurogenesis, the growth of new neurons.

An analogy: a pathway is like a track across a meadow. The more people walk it, the clearer and easier it gets; if you want a new route, it starts out all grass, slow and tiring, while the old track sits right beside it, always tempting you back. That is why change takes energy and why backsliding is the norm: you haven't failed; the old track just hasn't grown over yet.

Why it matters: this is the book's source of optimism — personality, habits of thought and emotional reactions are not fixed — and also its most practical point: short-term insight becomes long-term structure only through repeated practice. Hence her advice to seek out new experiences (new skills, places, people), since novelty and challenge are what drive plasticity.

How it changes the way you see: you swap "this is just who I am" for "this is how I'm currently wired" — the second leaves room for change and reminds you what change costs.

5. Brain agility: six modes of thinking, taking turns

What it is: by "brain agility" Swart means the ability to switch flexibly between modes of thinking as the situation demands, rather than leaning on one. She lists six:

An analogy: a football team made only of strikers. Logic is the striker, great at shooting, but without a midfield to organise play (motivation), defenders to read danger (emotion and intuition) and the legs to last (the body), it can't finish a match. Her readers are often executives used to running on logic alone, so she leans hard on the other five.

All of this sits under one premise: the brain is an organ of the body, and its fuel determines its performance — dehydration, poor diet, chronic stress and too little rest directly erode every capacity above. This is where she stands on firmest ground as a doctor: before you talk about manifesting, don't let your brain run short of water and sleep.

How it changes the way you see: next time you're stuck, the question isn't "have I thought hard enough?" but "which single mode am I using right now?" — maybe what you need is not another round of analysis but a walk, a night's sleep, or a check on what your body is telling you.

6. The action board and the four steps: turning vision into daily practice

What it is: Swart assembles the mechanisms above into a four-step process:

  • Step one: raise self-awareness. Journal about your childhood role and your family's rules and secrets; list six limiting beliefs and, for each, write down the evidence for and against it.
  • Step two: make an "action board." Assemble a collage of images, from magazines or online, of the future you want, and put it somewhere you'll see it every day. She deliberately avoids calling it a vision board, to stress that its job is to drive action rather than to wish — seeing it daily resets the filter and reinforces the tag each time.
  • Step three: practise presence. Meditation, body scans, yoga, or simply giving full attention to a meal or a walk — pulling attention out of the noise in your head and back to present sensation.
  • Step four: deliberate practice. A three-column exercise: goals on the left, the behaviours that actually work against them in the middle, and the beliefs driving those behaviours on the right; plus daily affirmations, and pushing yourself into new experiences to trigger plasticity.

Why it matters: the four steps map onto the four mechanisms — awareness finds the old tags, the action board tunes the attention filter, presence trains bodily awareness and emotional regulation, practice makes plasticity actually happen. Its value lies not in any step being novel but in translating "wanting" into things you can do every day.

How it changes the way you see: getting clear on a goal is only the start. What decides the outcome is what you see each day, what you practise, and which old beliefs you have actually operated on.

7. The Signs: intuition as information you ignore

The Signs (2025) turns the same approach toward intuition. Her claim is that your senses and body are sending you messages all the time, most of which never reach awareness — these are "the signs." The book covers senses we don't realise we use, practices our ancestors used to alter their states of mind, the importance of appreciating the beauty around us, and synchronicity (Carl Jung's term for two causally unconnected events coinciding in time in a way that feels deeply meaningful to the person involved), drawing on her own experience of grief.

Read through the logic of The Source, "signs" fall into two kinds: information the body really is processing below awareness (interoception, unconscious pattern recognition), which has a scientific basis; and the meaning one reads into coincidences, which belongs more to personal experience. One reviewer observed that most of the book is not really about coincidence at all but about cultivating a life that trusts intuition through everyday practices of physical and mental care.

The Spine of the Book

The book sets out to prove one sentence: "think it and it will come" can be translated into a set of brain mechanisms, and once translated it is still useful — just no longer magical.

The argument has three layers. The first is reinterpretation: the "attraction" in the Law of Attraction is selective attention (what you see) plus value tagging (what you care about); the benefit of "abundance" is a brain whose view widens once it leaves threat mode. The second is the possibility of change: neuroplasticity means both attention and tags can be reset, at the price of sustained practice and of first taking care of the brain as an organ. The third is method: replace logic alone with six whole-brain modes, and compress vision into daily behaviour through four steps — awareness, action board, presence, practice. Her evidence is general neuroscience (plasticity, loss aversion, the links between emotion and decision) plus cases from coaching practice, not experiments that test the action board itself — something readers should keep clearly in view.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: "She proves the Law of Attraction is real." Quite the opposite — she strips out its magical part. On her account the universe does not respond to your thoughts; only your own attention and action do. If you think without doing, her theory predicts you get nothing.

Misreading two: "An action board is just a wish board." She renamed it precisely to insist that the board is a tool for tuning attention and has to be followed by action and practice.

The strongest criticisms:

  • The neuroscience veneer. In a 2008 study, Deena Weisberg and colleagues found that adding irrelevant brain-science language to a psychological explanation made laypeople find it more convincing — even when the explanation was wrong. Critics argue that much of the persuasive force of books like this comes from the names of brain regions and neurotransmitters rather than from evidence; many of the recommendations (journaling, meditation, goal-setting) hold up just as well without the neuro-packaging, which adds no new proof.
  • Imagining a bright future can make people try less. Years of experiments by the psychologist Gabriele Oettingen show that simply indulging in positive fantasies about the future lowers motivation and actual achievement — as if the brain had already "consumed" the success. What works better, she found, is mental contrasting: imagine the desired outcome, then concretely imagine the obstacles in the way, and form "if X happens, I'll do Y" plans (her WOOP method). This is the most direct evidence against vision boards of every kind; Swart's action board, with its analysis of limiting beliefs and obstructive behaviour, comes partway toward mental contrasting, but she oversells the board itself.
  • The risk of confirmation bias. Selective attention cuts both ways: it helps you see opportunities and also makes you see only what fits your expectations. The experience of "I thought about it and it appeared" may largely be remembering the hits and forgetting the misses. The point is sharper in The Signs — reading coincidences as "signs" is exactly the over-detection of patterns, apophenia, to which human brains are most prone.
  • Some specific scientific claims are stated too firmly. Whether the adult human hippocampus keeps producing new neurons, for instance, drew opposite conclusions from two 2018 studies (Sorrells and colleagues in Nature, Boldrini and colleagues in Cell Stem Cell) and remains disputed, so it shouldn't be presented to readers as settled. Likewise, "about 90% of the body's serotonin is made in the gut" is roughly true, but gut serotonin essentially cannot cross the blood–brain barrier, so it doesn't follow that a healthy gut means good mood and intuition — the gut–brain connection is real, but its mechanisms are far more complex, and the human evidence is still largely preliminary.
  • Misplaced responsibility. An oft-criticised side effect of the Law of Attraction tradition is the implication that if you didn't get what you wanted, you didn't think the right way. Swart's emphasis on action and practice is sturdier than that, but the book says very little about structural factors — background, resources, luck — making it easy for readers to attribute every outcome to their own mindset.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · Vedanā (feeling-tone): early Buddhism holds that every perception arises with a feeling-tone — pleasant, unpleasant or neutral — and that craving and aversion grow from it. It is almost an ancient version of value tagging. The difference: Swart wants you to re-tag in order to pursue goals; Buddhist practice is to see the tag itself and stop being pulled by it.
  • Neuroscience · Predictive processing: in the predictive processing view of Karl Friston, Andy Clark and others, the brain doesn't passively receive input but constantly predicts, processing mainly the "errors" where reality departs from prediction. It goes beyond a filter: expectation shapes not just what you attend to but what you perceive. Yet it also stresses error signals — the world corrects you, the half manifesting talk tends to forget.
  • Artificial intelligence · Attention mechanisms: "attention" in a Transformer model computes a weight for each part of the input, deciding how much it matters to the task at hand — a mathematical version of selective attention plus value tagging. The difference: a model's weights are learned from its training objective, while some of a person's were set in childhood and may no longer serve today's goals.
  • Evolutionary biology · The smoke-detector principle: the psychiatrist Randolph Nesse noted that missing a real danger costs far more than a false alarm, so selection builds defensive responses to be oversensitive, like a smoke detector that would rather go off too often. That makes loss aversion and the "lack" mindset factory settings rather than personal flaws — and turning them down has a cost.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① The universe isn't listening; your brain is filtering.

② The Law of Attraction = selective attention (what you see) + value tagging (what you care about).

③ A goal doesn't create opportunities, but it decides whether you can see them — the filter only amplifies what is already there.

④ When you know you should but don't want to, the culprit is often an emotional price tag stuck on long ago, without your noticing.

⑤ A brain in threat mode makes short-sighted decisions; that is the testable core of "abundance."

⑥ The brain can change, but slowly, at a cost in energy, and backsliding is normal — the old track hasn't grown over yet.

⑦ Logic alone is half a brain: emotion, motivation, body, intuition, logic and creativity should take turns — and first, don't let the brain run short of water and sleep.

⑧ An action board isn't a wish board: seeing it daily resets the filter, and action has to follow.

⑨ But beware: indulging in a bright fantasy makes people try less; what works is facing the obstacles at the same time.

⑩ The sense that signs are everywhere may be half intuition speaking and half the filter confirming what you expected.
