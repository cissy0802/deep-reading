The Emperor of All Maladies

The Emperor of All Maladies: A Biography of Cancer · Siddhartha Mukherjee · 2010

中文 →

In One Sentence

Cancer is not an invader but a corrupted version of our own cells' normal program for growth; four thousand years of fighting it is the story of humanity repeatedly believing it had found the answer, only to be forced by an enemy grown from our own bodies into a more honest kind of science — from escalating on faith to letting randomized trials, epidemiology and molecular mechanism decide.

Where It Sits

Mukherjee is an oncologist and cancer researcher at Columbia University. The book grew out of the journal he kept during his oncology fellowship in Boston, and it won the 2011 Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction. It turns the history of a disease into a history of how modern medicine's methods were forged under pressure.

The Core Argument

  • Cancer is a disease of ourselves. A cancer cell is not a foreign microbe; it is a normal cell that has accumulated enough mutations to unlock capacities — dividing, surviving, migrating — that are normally kept on a very short leash. Because it is so like us, almost anything that kills it also harms us.
  • One of the greatest enemies in the history of cancer has been our own certainty. From surgery that cut ever more, to chemotherapy dosed ever higher, medicine kept making the same mistake: treating a plausible theory as fact and using patients' bodies to double down on it. Each real advance came from someone insisting on proof before practice.
  • The real gains came from three fronts converging: prevention, early detection and understanding mechanism. There was no single decisive battle. Tobacco epidemiology has probably saved more lives than any cancer drug, and molecular understanding finally made "targeted" therapy real for a handful of cancers.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. A disease of growth: the enemy is our own mirror image

Start with the basic picture. Billions of your cells divide every day to replace worn-out skin, gut lining and blood. Each division is governed by accelerators and brakes: growth signals say "divide now," other signals say "stop," and a separate mechanism orders a damaged cell to kill itself — programmed cell death, or apoptosis. Normal tissue stops growing at the right size because these signals hold each other in check.

Cancer is what happens when that balance fails, bit by bit, inside one cell — so that its descendants outgrow and outlive their neighbors. Mukherjee's point, paraphrased: cancer cells are more vigorous, more resilient, more fertile, more inventive copies of ourselves. A cancer cell is not a monster; it is a cell that has become too successful.

An analogy: every employee in a company has signed a contract — do your job, go home on time, resign if you make a serious mistake. One day a few clauses in one employee's contract are scribbled over. He stops going home, stops resigning, starts copying himself, and learns to climb the fence and set up shop in other branch offices. Those are cancer's four moves: unlimited proliferation, evading apoptosis, invasion, and metastasis (cells leaving the original site and settling in distant organs).

Why it matters: this picture explains why cancer is so hard to treat. Antibiotics work because bacteria are biochemically very different from us, so a drug can hit them and spare us. Cancer cells run on essentially the same machinery as normal ones. Early chemotherapy simply attacked every rapidly dividing cell, which is why patients lost their hair (hair follicles divide fast), developed mouth sores and saw their bone marrow suppressed. With cancer, collateral damage is close to the definition of treatment.

How it changes your view: cancer stops being "a disease" and becomes a process — one cell gaining advantage step by step over many years. That is why cancer is mostly a disease of age: the longer you live, the more divisions your cells undergo and the more chances mutations have to add up. Part of the rise in cancer, Mukherjee notes, is simply that people no longer die young of tuberculosis, cholera and war.

2. "More is better": how a plausible theory harmed generations of patients

Records of cancer are nearly as old as writing. An ancient Egyptian medical papyrus (the Edwin Smith Papyrus, often linked to the tradition of the physician Imhotep) describes bulging masses in the breast and, under "treatment," says in effect: there is none. Hippocrates likened the swollen vessels radiating from a tumor to a crab's legs and called it karkinos — the root of the word "cancer." The Roman physician Galen blamed an excess of black bile, one of the four humors; if the problem was a systemic imbalance of fluids, cutting out a lump was pointless. That theory reigned for over a thousand years, until Vesalius's meticulous dissections in the sixteenth century failed to find black bile anywhere.

When humoral theory fell, surgery stepped in. By the late nineteenth century, anesthesia and antisepsis made major operations survivable, and William Halsted at Johns Hopkins developed the radical mastectomy: removing not only the breast but the chest muscles beneath it and the lymph nodes in the armpit. His rationale was the "centrifugal theory" — cancer spreads outward from its center like a wheel, so cutting wide and deep enough should catch it before it spreads. If the cancer came back, that meant you hadn't cut enough.

Notice the trap in that logic: every failure is explained as insufficient escalation, so the theory itself never gets tested. Halsted's disciples went on to "superradical" operations that removed collarbones, ribs, even parts of the breastbone, leaving women with hollowed chests and lifelong disability. For decades this was standard care for breast cancer.

The turn came from an idea that sounded, at the time, almost unethical: a randomized controlled trial — assigning patients at random to the new treatment or the old one, so that the two groups differ in nothing but the treatment, and then comparing outcomes. In the 1970s the American surgeon Bernard Fisher ran a trial of roughly 1,700 women, comparing radical mastectomy, simple mastectomy plus radiation, and simple mastectomy alone. The result: no significant difference in survival. All that muscle and bone had bought nothing.

Why? Because the centrifugal theory was wrong. Many breast cancers, when found, are either still local — in which case a smaller operation suffices — or have already seeded the bloodstream, in which case no margin is wide enough. The size of the operation was never the variable that mattered.

The book shows this pattern repeating. In the 1980s and 1990s, high-dose chemotherapy with bone-marrow transplant was used for advanced breast cancer: if chemo works, push the dose high enough to destroy the marrow, then rescue the patient with her own previously harvested marrow. Without adequate randomized evidence, patients, lawyers and the press pushed it into wide use; insurers who refused to pay were sued. The randomized trials that eventually came showed no survival benefit, and the South African study by Werner Bezwoda that had reported striking results was found in 2000 to be fraudulent. The same script: an elegant theory plus desperate demand overpowered the evidence.

How it changes your view: whenever you meet a plan built on "doing more will work better" — in medicine, management or education — watch for this structure. If failure can always be blamed on not trying hard enough, the theory is unfalsifiable. The question is not whether it sounds right, but whether anyone has compared it against not doing it.

3. Chemotherapy and "cure": combinations, persistence, and a statistical word

Modern chemotherapy begins in 1947. Sidney Farber, a pathologist at Boston Children's Hospital, was facing acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), a blood cancer that then typically killed children within weeks or months of diagnosis. Knowing that dividing cells need folic acid, he treated the children with an antifolate — aminopterin, a drug that blocks cells from using folate. In 1948 he reported that some of the children went into temporary remission: leukemia cells receded from their blood, and they could walk and play again.

But the remissions lasted only months before the leukemia came roaring back. This exposed the basic flaw of single-drug therapy: a tumor always contains a few cells that happen to be insensitive to the drug, and killing the sensitive ones clears the field for the resistant ones. It is Darwinian selection, running inside one patient over a few months.

The answer was combination chemotherapy, on the same logic used against antibiotic resistance: the odds that a single cell is simultaneously resistant to four drugs with different mechanisms are vanishingly small, so use four at once. In the early 1960s at the National Cancer Institute, Emil Frei and Emil Freireich gave leukemic children a four-drug regimen called VAMP. The toxicity was brutal, and many colleagues thought they were all but poisoning children. Yet the remissions grew deeper and longer.

One piece was still missing. Even when no leukemia cells could be found in the blood, the disease often relapsed — frequently starting in the brain. The blood-brain barrier, a layer of vessels that keeps most drugs out of the brain, had turned it into a sanctuary. At St. Jude Children's Research Hospital in Memphis, Donald Pinkel designed "total therapy": multiple drugs, long maintenance treatment, and radiation aimed at the brain. Long-term survival in childhood ALL climbed from almost zero to around 80–90 percent today — one of the earliest and most complete victories in the whole history of cancer.

There is also a story about persistence. In the 1950s, Min Chiu Li treated choriocarcinoma, a cancer arising from placental tissue, with methotrexate. He noticed the tumor secretes a hormone, hCG, whose blood level works as a readout of how many cancer cells remain. So even after the tumor had vanished from view, he kept treating until the hormone reached zero. This was seen as reckless experimentation, and he was forced out of the NCI. Yet he turned out to be the first person to cure a solid tumor with chemotherapy, and "invisible doesn't mean gone" became a founding principle of the field.

The word "cure" itself is worth understanding. In oncology it is usually statistical: a patient is considered cured when enough years have passed without relapse that the risk falls to roughly that of everyone else. The familiar "five-year survival rate" is a measurement of the same kind. You can never declare that the last cancer cell has died; you can only watch, over time, that it doesn't come back.

How it changes your view: against an adversary that evolves and hides, a single massive blow loses to attacks that are multipronged, sustained and aimed at the sanctuaries — and you need a gauge that can read the residue you can't see.

4. Prevention and screening: the biggest wins are often away from the bedside

In 1775 the London surgeon Percivall Pott noticed that men who had worked as boy chimney sweeps developed cancer of the scrotum at strikingly high rates. He blamed soot lodged in their skin. It was the first time a cancer was tied to a specific environmental exposure: cancer could have external causes, and therefore could be prevented.

The great twentieth-century test was smoking. In 1950 Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill in Britain published a case-control study — comparing people with a disease to similar people without it and looking back at differences in their past exposures — and found that heavy smokers were far more common among lung-cancer patients. That same year Ernst Wynder and Evarts Graham reached similar conclusions in the United States. Doll and Hill then followed tens of thousands of British doctors over time (a cohort study: record people's habits first, then see who gets sick), and the conclusion only hardened. In 1964 the U.S. Surgeon General's report formally declared that smoking causes lung cancer.

Mukherjee gives a lot of space to how the tobacco industry fought back: funding doubt, stressing that "correlation isn't causation," and manufacturing a scientific controversy that could stay open forever. But epidemiology built, precisely here, a working standard for judging causation — risk rises with dose, exposure precedes disease, the finding repeats across populations, and there is a plausible biological mechanism — so causation could be established even though no one could ethically assign people to smoke. The later fall in American lung-cancer deaths came mainly from people smoking less, not from better drugs.

Early detection is the other front. The Pap smear — scraping cervical cells and examining them for precancerous changes, popularized by George Papanicolaou — drove cervical cancer down sharply in many countries. Mammography's story is far messier: some trials showed lower mortality, others were murky. Why is screening so hard to judge? Because of several hidden statistical traps:

TrapWhat it isWhy it misleads
Lead-time biasScreening lets you know earlier, without making you die laterSomeone who would have been diagnosed at 65 and died at 70 is instead diagnosed at 61 and still dies at 70 — "survival after diagnosis" grows from 5 years to 9, which looks like a treatment effect
Length biasPeriodic screening is better at catching slow-growing tumorsFast, aggressive tumors tend to surface between screens, so screen-detected patients naturally do better
OverdiagnosisFinding "cancers" that would never have caused harm in a lifetimeThose people are operated on and irradiated, then counted as cures, making screening look like it saved more lives

How it changes your view: catching it early is not the same as living longer. To judge any test or early-warning system, look at final outcomes, not at how much it detects.

5. Oncogenes and tumor suppressors: a stuck accelerator, a broken brake

In 1910 Peyton Rous in New York found that a cell-free filtrate from a chicken's sarcoma could cause sarcomas in other chickens — something smaller than a bacterium (later known to be a virus) could cause cancer. For decades afterward, "cancer is caused by viruses" was a fashionable theory, and the United States poured money into hunting for human cancer viruses, with far less to show for it than hoped.

The real answer surfaced in 1976. Harold Varmus and J. Michael Bishop, studying src, the cancer-causing gene of the Rous sarcoma virus, found an almost identical gene already present in normal chicken cells. The virus had merely stolen a normal host gene during some past infection and corrupted it. The seeds of cancer were in our own genome all along. These normal genes are called proto-oncogenes; they usually send "grow" signals. Mutated into oncogenes, they are like an accelerator jammed to the floor.

The other half of the puzzle is the brakes. In 1971 Alfred Knudson, studying a childhood eye tumor called retinoblastoma, noticed that children with a family history often developed tumors in both eyes and early, while others usually had it in one eye and later. He proposed the "two-hit hypothesis": every cell has two copies of the relevant gene, and both must be knocked out to cause cancer. Children with a family history are born with one copy already broken and need just one more hit; everyone else needs two strokes of bad luck in the same cell. Such genes are called tumor suppressors — the brakes. Oncogenes are a dominant "too much"; tumor suppressors are a recessive "something missing." The famous p53 gene turned out to be a brake that fails in most cancers.

By 2000, Douglas Hanahan and Robert Weinberg had summarized all this as the "hallmarks of cancer": to thrive, a cancer must acquire a shared set of capacities — self-sufficient growth signals, deafness to stop signals, evasion of apoptosis, limitless replication, recruiting new blood vessels to feed itself, and invasion and metastasis (more hallmarks were added later). Different cancers take different mutational routes, but they arrive at the same destination.

Why it matters: "cancer" turns from hundreds of seemingly unrelated diseases into different ways the same machinery breaks. It is many diseases and one disease at once — different organs, different mutations, the same category of broken growth control. By the same token, "lung cancer" in a single organ can be several entirely different diseases at the genetic level.

How it changes your view: you start classifying cancers by mechanism, not just by location. That is where precision oncology begins.

6. Targeted therapy and evolution inside the body: the magic bullet and its limits

The payoff from understanding mechanism finally arrived around the turn of the century. The most beautiful case is chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). In 1960 researchers in Philadelphia found an abnormally short chromosome in these patients' white cells — the "Philadelphia chromosome." In 1973 Janet Rowley showed it was the result of chromosomes 9 and 22 breaking and swapping pieces. The swap fuses two genes into one, BCR-ABL, which makes an enzyme permanently switched on: a jammed accelerator.

If one stuck switch drives nearly the whole disease, then build a lock that jams only that switch. That is imatinib (Gleevec), developed by Brian Druker with scientists at Novartis and approved in 2001. Many CML patients who once faced a few years of life became people who take a daily pill and live with the disease long term. Breast cancer has a parallel story: about one in five breast cancers overproduces a growth receptor called HER2, and the antibody drug trastuzumab (Herceptin) markedly improved outcomes for those patients.

This was the "magic bullet" Farber's generation had dreamed of — hit the cancer, spare the patient. But Mukherjee warns that magic bullets fail too, because cancer evolves. Some CML patients develop new mutations in BCR-ABL that stop the drug from binding, requiring second- and third-generation drugs. And most solid tumors have no single driver switch like CML's; they carry dozens or hundreds of intertwined mutations, so when you block one pathway, another takes over.

An analogy: fighting cancer is less like storming a castle than playing chess against an opponent who can rebuild its walls in real time. Every move you make selects for the variants that happen to dodge it. Every effective treatment is also an exercise in artificially selecting for resistance.

How it changes your view: "defeating cancer" may itself be the wrong frame. A more realistic goal is to turn some cancers into chronic, long-managed conditions like high blood pressure or diabetes. That is roughly where Mukherjee lands: the question may not be eradication, but whether we can keep pushing death further away.

The Spine of the Book

The whole book reduces to one line. (1) Cancer is normal growth gone rogue, so it is ancient, universal, tied to aging, and inherently hard to hit without hitting us. (2) Humanity's first responses were theory-first: humoral theory said it was systemic and surgery was futile; the centrifugal theory said it was local and more cutting was better. Both rested on belief and were rarely tested. (3) In the twentieth century, advocates like Mary Lasker and physicians like Farber turned cancer into a national mobilization; in 1971 Nixon signed the National Cancer Act, and the "war on cancer" began. (4) That war won real victories against some blood and childhood cancers (combination chemo, total therapy) but moved slowly against most common solid tumors; a 1986 analysis of U.S. data found age-adjusted cancer mortality had risen rather than fallen and bluntly concluded we were losing the war. (5) Whenever medicine escalated past the evidence (radical surgery, high-dose chemo), patients were harmed, and randomized trials pulled it back. (6) Real progress came from three fronts converging: tobacco control and screening bent the curves of incidence and death, and U.S. cancer mortality has fallen steadily since the 1990s; molecular biology revealed oncogenes and tumor suppressors, making targeted therapy real for a few cancers. (7) But cancer evolves inside the body, so the endgame is more likely long-term management than a decisive victory.

How does the book make its case? Through a chronological history of evolving method, in which each stage's errors and corrections are carried by specific people, specific trials and specific patients — interleaved with the treatment of Mukherjee's own leukemia patient, Carla, and with a thought experiment running through the book: how would Atossa, the Persian queen whom Herodotus records as having a breast tumor cut out by a Greek slave physician, have been treated in each era? Atossa's journey through the centuries is, in miniature, the history of what we have learned about cancer.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

  • Misreading one: "It's a triumphal history." Far from it. Much of the book is about failure, overtreatment and self-deception. Mukherjee is wary of the "war on cancer" slogan: the war metaphor makes escalation look like courage and stopping look like weakness, and patients often pay for it with their bodies. The book echoes Susan Sontag's critique, in Illness as Metaphor, of moralizing and militarizing disease.
  • Misreading two: "Cancer is a genetic disease, so sequencing will solve it." The book does pin its hopes on molecular understanding, but it equally stresses heterogeneity: different cells within one patient's tumor can carry different mutations, and environment, age and immunity all play their parts.
  • Criticism one: dated, and it almost misses immunotherapy. Published in 2010, the book predates the field's most important breakthroughs since — immune checkpoint inhibitors (drugs that release the brakes cancer puts on the immune system, letting immune cells attack the tumor again; James Allison and Tasuku Honjo shared the 2018 Nobel Prize for this) and CAR-T therapy (re-engineering a patient's own T cells into guided missiles against cancer cells). Reading it today, you need to know a fourth front has opened.
  • Criticism two: great-man narrative, America-centered. The book revolves mostly around American and British doctors, labs and advocates, with heroic portraits driving the plot; it says comparatively little about the global burden of cancer, access to care, or racial and class disparities. Some reviewers felt the dramatized characters sometimes obscured how collective science really is.
  • Criticism three: selective storytelling. To keep the "from belief to evidence" arc clean, some figures read more like heroes or villains (Halsted versus Fisher, for instance) than the more complicated people history suggests. It is a history with a point of view, not a neutral textbook.

Crossovers

  • Evolutionary biology · the Red Queen hypothesis: In 1973 Leigh Van Valen borrowed the Red Queen's line from Through the Looking-Glass — you have to run as fast as you can just to stay in place — to describe endless arms races between species. Tumors and drugs run exactly such a race: every new drug selects new resisters. Out of this grew "adaptive therapy" (associated with Robert Gatenby and colleagues): rather than trying to kill everything at once, deliberately spare drug-sensitive cells so they keep resistant ones in check and the tumor holds at a stable size. Where it differs: species' races have no finish line; a patient's life does.
  • Evolution of cooperation · "the cheating cell": A multicellular organism is itself a cooperative contract — each cell gives up the right to reproduce without limit in exchange for the whole surviving. In The Cheating Cell, Athena Aktipis treats cancer as a defector against that contract, the same structure as the free rider in a public-goods game. Where it differs: a social free rider can profit; a cancer cell's "success" ends by destroying its host and itself.
  • Distributed systems · Byzantine fault tolerance: In distributed systems the hardest nodes to guard against aren't the crashed ones but "Byzantine" ones that keep running while sending bad information. Apoptosis and checkpoints like p53 resemble mechanisms that make faulty nodes take themselves offline; cancer is a node that disables its self-check and keeps broadcasting "give me more resources." Where it differs: a computer cluster can be rebooted and rebuilt; a body can only be patched in place.
  • Complexity science · Peto's paradox: The epidemiologist Richard Peto observed that whales and elephants have hundreds or thousands of times more cells than humans and should, by the odds, get far more cancer — yet they don't. Later research found elephant genomes carry about twenty copies of the TP53 gene, many extra brakes. As a system scales up, its fault tolerance has to scale too. Where it differs: this design took evolution tens of millions of years, and we can't simply copy it into ourselves.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. Cancer is not an outside enemy but a corrupted version of our own cells' growth program — which is why nearly anything that kills it can also harm us.

2. Cancer is mainly a disease of age: the longer we live, the more our cells divide and the more chances mutations have to add up; some of its rise is the price of longevity.

3. The lesson of radical surgery: if failure is always blamed on "not doing enough," the theory never gets tested.

4. To judge a treatment, don't ask whether it sounds right; ask whether anyone compared it against not doing it — the randomized trial is the sharpest tool in the history of cancer.

5. Single drugs clear the field for resisters; combinations, long maintenance and attacking sanctuaries are what won against childhood leukemia.

6. Min Chiu Li's principle: invisible doesn't mean gone; "cure" is a statistical word confirmed slowly, over time.

7. The biggest victories are often far from the bedside: smoking less may have saved more lives than any cancer drug.

8. Finding it early isn't living longer — lead time, length bias and overdiagnosis can all make screening look better than it is.

9. Oncogenes are jammed accelerators, tumor suppressors broken brakes; the seeds of cancer were in our own genome all along.

10. Targeted drugs are real magic bullets, but every effective treatment selects for resistance; the endgame is more likely long-term control than one decisive victory.
