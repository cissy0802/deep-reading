The Varieties of Religious Experience

William James · 1902 (the Gifford Lectures, Edinburgh, 1901–02)

中文 →

In one sentence

An American psychologist spread a thousand diaries, confessions and conversion narratives across his desk, sorted and compared them the way a naturalist sorts specimens, and arrived at a conclusion that leaves believers and atheists equally uncomfortable: whatever is most real in religion lives not in churches, creeds or ceremonies but in the small, unsayable experiences a person has alone; and whether those experiences deserve to be taken seriously has nothing to do with where they came from — an epileptic fit, a breakdown, a lungful of laughing gas — and everything to do with what they produced in the person who had them.

Where it sits

William James (1842–1910) founded American psychology, led the pragmatist school in philosophy, taught at Harvard, and was the elder brother of the novelist Henry James. In his twenties he suffered a severe breakdown, and he wrestled with depression and heart disease for the rest of his life — a fact that turns out to be load-bearing for this book. The book itself is the Gifford Lectures he gave in Edinburgh in 1901–02 (an endowed series on "natural theology," meaning the study of God by reason and experience rather than revelation, running at four Scottish universities since 1888). Its position is unusual: it founded the psychology of religion, and one hundred and twenty years later it remains the best book in that field — later scholars demolished a good many of its conclusions while continuing to work inside the frame it built.

The central claims

  • Religion in its original form is personal experience, not institution. His definition is deliberately narrow: religion is "the feelings, acts, and experiences of individual men in their solitude, so far as they apprehend themselves to stand in relation to whatever they may consider the divine." Churches, theologies and liturgies are second-hand — the cooled residue of somebody else's first-hand experience.
  • Judge an experience by its fruits, not by its roots. Explaining away an experience by naming its physiological cause is a methodological cheat. The real test lies downstream: what did it make of the person?
  • There is a door at the margin of consciousness, and religious experience comes through it. The door can be described completely by psychology; whether anything stands on the far side of it, psychology cannot say — and James labels his own answer an "over-belief," parks it at the very end of the book, and marks it plainly as a personal bet.

The core concepts, one at a time

"Human documents": his subject is not religion but religious experience

The move that is easiest to skip, and that determines everything else, is his choice of material. James does not read catechisms, count congregations, or compare denominations. He reads documents humains — a phrase borrowed from the French naturalist novelists for raw, unedited first-person records: diaries, letters, conversion accounts, confessions, asylum case notes. His reason is blunt: most people's faith is second-hand, an inherited habit formed after somebody else's original experience had been institutionalized and imitated; if you want to study fire, go to where something is still burning.

So he deliberately collects extremes — "religious geniuses," people whose experiences were intense enough to leave a detailed record, including people who were simultaneously diagnosed as ill. This is microscope logic: to see a structure, find a magnified specimen. The cost is obvious, and the criticism section below bills him for it: conclusions drawn from extreme cases may not describe the ninety per cent who simply show up on Sunday. The gain is real too. There is almost no abstract argument in the book; it is all living people in their own words, and it reads like a casebook of the soul.

Medical materialism: deciding the fruit from the root is a fallacy

James coins a term for his target: medical materialism — the move of naming the physiological cause behind a religious experience and treating that as having disposed of it. Paul on the Damascus road was having a seizure; Teresa of Ávila was a hysteric (the period's catch-all diagnosis for women's emotional symptoms); Francis of Assisi was a hereditary degenerate. The conclusion looks self-evident: if it was pathology, it needn't be taken seriously.

His counter is not to deny the causes but to notice that the rule, once adopted, has to apply evenhandedly. Every mental state has bodily conditions, including the medical materialist's own scepticism, which is equally a product of his liver, his blood sugar and his nerves. If "has a physiological cause" means "is void," then every opinion goes down together, including that one. Origin cannot adjudicate value: that is the methodological gate of the whole book. He compresses it into a line — by their fruits ye shall know them, not by their roots.

Having removed the root, he owes us a new yardstick, and he supplies three tests: immediate luminousness (does the experience carry its own conviction?), philosophical reasonableness (does it collide with the rest of what we know?), and moral helpfulness (does it make the person's life better or worse?). All three must run together: the first prevents us from dismissing every intense state as superstition, the second and third prevent us from accepting every intense state as revelation.

What it changes in how you argue: this is the earliest and most practical clinical workout of the genetic fallacy — substituting an account of where a claim came from for an examination of whether the claim is true. Today's versions merely wear new clothes: your sense of the sacred is temporal-lobe activity, your moral intuitions are evolutionary leftovers, your politics is the size of your amygdala. The sentence structure is identical, and so is James's reply: explaining how a belief arose and establishing whether it is right are two different jobs, and a great many people clock off after the first.

The healthy-minded and the sick soul: two temperaments, not two denominations

The book's most famous pairing, and its most misread. The healthy-minded, also called the "once-born," are constitutionally unable to see evil as anything but local and fixable — a bad debt in an otherwise sound ledger. Whitman is a specimen; so is Emerson; so, in its popular form, was the American "mind-cure" movement then in fashion, which held that thoughts themselves heal and required its followers to refuse systematically to dwell on anything bad. James rates this tradition highly: it works, and its working is an observable fact.

The sick soul, the "twice-born," cannot treat evil as a malfunction; evil looks to him like part of the recipe of the world. Tolstoy at fifty is the specimen — career, family and fame all intact, and life suddenly drained of meaning; in his Confession he writes that he stopped carrying a rope into a room alone and gave up hunting with a gun, for fear of what he might do with them (paraphrased). Bunyan is another: recurring assaults of guilt, years without peace.

The book also contains an anonymous "French correspondent." A young man steps into a dressing room and is seized by absolute dread; there rises before him the image of an epileptic patient he had seen in an asylum, knees drawn to chin, eyes vacant, sitting against the wall all day. That shape is what I am, he says, and nothing keeps me from becoming it (paraphrased). For months afterwards he could not be alone. James presented this as the record of a French correspondent; scholars accept it as his own experience in his twenties. A book that looks like a professor cataloguing other people's specimens turns out to contain one of his own.

The two temperaments. Note James's verdict: the side that can see more wins.

That verdict is the point. James does not treat the sick soul as an illness to be cured; he treats it as the larger field of vision. His reason is strictly empirical. The healthy-minded worldview has a whole region it cannot enter — it has nothing to say in a cancer ward, nothing about extinction, nothing to a person who has failed all the way down, and it shatters the moment its owner meets any of these. The sick soul's worldview, by contrast, can contain every fact the healthy-minded knows; the reverse does not hold. He says it plainly (paraphrased): the completest religions are the ones in which the pessimistic elements are best developed — Buddhism and Christianity both open by declaring that life as such is diseased.

The divided self, and "the habitual centre of personal energy"

Here the book is at its most mechanical, in the good sense. The divided self names a state everybody recognizes: two incompatible sets of aims running at once inside one person, neither able to win. Augustine's "I willed and I willed not" is the prototype; the alcoholic, the person who means to quit and doesn't, the morally torn, are all on this spectrum.

Conversion, for James, is how that civil war ends — and the concept he uses to describe it is unusually portable. Each of us carries a "habitual centre of personal energy": the small cluster of ideas we take most seriously and work outward from. Most ideas merely sit in the mind; only the ones at the centre are hot, commandeering attention, feeling and action. Conversion is not the addition of a new belief. It is a change of centre: a group of ideas that had been sitting cold at the periphery moves to the middle, and what was hot moves out. This is why converts so often change wholesale, as though replaced — nothing single was edited; the sorting key for everything changed.

He distinguishes two routes. The volitional type is gradual, built brick by brick out of conscious effort. The self-surrender type is the opposite: the turn arrives only after the person stops trying entirely. James's explanation of why is excellent. When the thing you want is by its nature not obtainable by exertion, the exertion is itself the only obstacle. The analogies are at hand: trying to fall asleep keeps you awake, trying to laugh naturally produces the falsest laugh, the thought of not being nervous is already nervousness. This is not mysticism; it is a structural property of a certain class of goal.

What matters is that he holds his neutrality here: what he documents is the rearrangement of a psychological landscape, and the fact that it is often permanent (he tracked converts years later). Who or what did the rearranging, he says explicitly, psychology has no instrument to determine.

The subliminal margin: the door

So where do the uninvited things come from — the sudden certainties, the fear that dissolves at a stroke, the thought experienced as an external voice? James borrows a concept from the English psychologist Frederic Myers (a founder of the Society for Psychical Research): the subliminal or subconscious self, meaning an entire region of mental activity running below the threshold of awareness.

Do not confuse it with Freud's unconscious. Freud's is essentially a warehouse of repressed wishes, and its contents are mostly bad. James's subliminal margin is a larger mental hinterland: inspiration, long-forgotten memories, the sentence that arrives while writing from nowhere in particular, and religious experience, all surface from there.

Schematic: James's closing structure — describe up to the door, label whatever lies past it an over-belief.

This is the cleverest piece of engineering in the book: it lets religious experience be described completely without being explained away completely. You can grant that conversion happens entirely through the subliminal margin and still leave open whether anything larger than the individual comes through it — or give a thoroughly naturalistic account on the same map. The psychology of religion has been working along this line ever since.

Saintliness: putting the saints on the scale too

If fruits are the criterion, then the weighing has to be done for real. James first describes the common features of saintliness: a sense of a life wider than one's private interests; a feeling of being continuous with an ideal power; an immense freedom, as the grip of self loosens; a shift of the emotional centre toward yes and toward love. The fruits that grow from this are asceticism, strength of soul, purity and charity.

Then he does something the devout find close to offensive: he marks the saints down with the same yardstick. Devotion in excess degenerates into what he calls a theopathic condition — all the person's energy absorbed in adoring God, of no use to anybody nearby. Asceticism can slide into a competition in self-torment unconnected to anyone's welfare. Purity can slide into a fastidious withdrawal, a refusal to touch the world lest one be soiled. His verdict: these are not too much saintliness but saintliness missing its other half — the friction surface against the actual world.

Yet his final estimate of the saints is very high, and he reaches it by a wager-shaped argument (paraphrased): saints are increasers of the good, auctores — Latin for those who make a thing grow, and the root of our word "author." They extend trust first, and thereby bring into being something worth trusting. Believing a person can be relied on is itself one of the causes of his becoming reliable; living in advance in a better world is one of the ways that world's probability goes up. This is the argument of James's essay "The Will to Believe" landing in practice: there is a class of facts that appear only once you have believed in them, and demanding evidence first is a guarantee that they never will. (Note how tightly he fences it: only for questions that evidence cannot settle, that force a choice now, and where the choice affects the outcome.)

Mysticism: four marks, and a lungful of gas

The high point of the book. James gives four operational marks for identifying a mystical state — a set still used as the working definition in this research a century later:

  • Ineffability. The subject insists it cannot be put into words and resorts to analogy. The test: it resembles a state of feeling more than a transmissible proposition — as you cannot convey a melody to someone who has never heard music.
  • Noetic quality. Though unsayable, the subject is certain he has come to know something, not merely felt something. This mark is the crucial one: it is what makes these states a philosophical problem rather than only a psychological curiosity.
  • Transiency. Usually minutes, rarely as much as half an hour; it cannot be sustained.
  • Passivity. Practices, breathing and drugs can prepare the ground, but when the state arrives the subject feels seized rather than operating.

To get first-hand material James inhaled nitrous oxide himself. The passage he left is the most quoted in the book: our normal waking consciousness, rational consciousness as we call it, is but one special type of consciousness, whilst all about it, parted from it by the filmiest of screens, there lie potential forms of consciousness entirely different. He adds the half-sentence that matters more: you may go your whole life without suspecting they exist, but apply the requisite stimulus and there they are in their completeness — and no account of the universe that claims to be complete is entitled to pretend otherwise.

Can mystical states prove anything, then? Here is the most restrained and most effective move in the book. James offers three conclusions (paraphrased): (1) for the person who has them they are authoritative, and he is under no obligation to be argued out of them; (2) for anyone else they carry no authority whatever, and no one is obliged to accept them; (3) but they do overturn one thing — the monopoly of rational consciousness over what counts as real. The third is what he was after all along. He never argues that God exists. He argues that "sober rational consciousness is the only legitimate channel to reality" is a premise nobody has earned the right to assume.

The argument, stripped to its spine

Twenty lectures reduce to five moves.

One: shrink the object. Cut "religion" down to "what an individual feels, does and undergoes in his solitude insofar as he takes himself to stand in relation to the divine," and set institutions, doctrine and sociology aside. Every strength and every weakness of the book follows from this cut.

Two: change the rules of adjudication. Ban the argument from pathology (medical materialism); adopt fruits, tested by immediate luminousness, philosophical reasonableness and moral helpfulness. This sidesteps theology entirely: he has no intention of ruling on whose doctrine is true, only on what these experiences did.

Three: lay out the material. Two temperaments (healthy-minded / sick soul) → the divided self → conversion as a migration of the centre of personal energy → saintliness and its excesses → the four marks of mysticism. Each link runs phenomenon, then mechanism, then scoring.

Four: close. The shared psychological entrance for all of it is the subliminal margin; the structure common to religions at the psychological level is one and the same — a person becomes aware that something in him is wrong, and the resolution arrives through contact with a MORE. Whether that MORE extends beyond the subliminal margin, James marks off: as science, this is where it stops; past this point everyone is dealing in over-beliefs.

Five: show his own hand, and label it as a hand. In the postscript he states his own over-belief: he leans toward a piecemeal supernaturalism, a higher power producing specific effects at specific moments in specific people rather than an absolute running everything; and he would rather believe that power finite than omnipotent, because an omnipotent and wholly good being does not reconcile with the world's accounts. The last line of the book is his methodological signature: God is real since he produces real effects. Note how careful it is. It does not say what God is. It says only that whatever has real effects belongs on the inventory of the real.

Misreadings, and the serious objections

Misreading one: that this is a defence of religion. It isn't. James belonged to no church and did not accept an orthodox Christian God; his remarks on churches, clergy and theology are frequently caustic — the least creditable chapters of religious history, he suggests, were written by the organizational instinct rather than by religious experience (paraphrased). What he defends is the reality and the consequences of experience, not any body of doctrine.

Misreading two: that "by their fruits" means useful equals true. James himself keeps the distinction: an existential judgment (how a thing arose) and a spiritual judgment (what it is worth) are different, and neither is the same as the truth of what the experience asserts. That an experience made someone's life better does not establish that its object exists. In fairness, though, he does slip forward a step at the end — exactly what "real" is doing in "God is real since he produces real effects" has been argued about for a century. This is the book's genuine soft spot, and there is no reason to cover for him.

Misreading three: that healthy-mindedness is the compliment and the sick soul the diagnosis. The reverse; he thinks the sick soul sees more.

Objection one, the most damaging: a Protestant individualist bias. Later scholars of religion — Wayne Proudfoot, Talal Asad, Grace Jantzen, and the philosopher Charles Taylor — converge on one charge: defining religion as solitary inner experience is itself the self-portrait of a particular historical product (nineteenth-century liberal Protestantism) mistaken for the universal essence of religion. For Judaism, Islam, Catholicism, Confucian ritual and most indigenous traditions, religion is first of all community, law, rite, bodily practice and transmission across generations; the private inner state is a marginal case. James's sample contains almost no congregational worship, no law, no contest over orthodoxy — and those three occupy most of the actual volume of religious history.

Objection two: there is no such thing as unmediated experience. This is Steven Katz's argument (1978) and the core of the "constructivist" reply to James's claim of a shared cross-cultural core: a Buddhist does not experience a personal God and a Christian mystic does not experience nirvana — not because they described one event in different vocabularies afterwards, but because training and concepts were shaping the experience at the moment it occurred. Proudfoot (1985) adds a second cut: the noetic mark already concedes that concepts are present, since "I have come to know something" is a contentful judgment. Even ineffability may be a way of describing that a tradition teaches. This line of attack is strong, and James's claim that all mystical experiences report the same thing does not survive it. What survives is the more basic half: these are real psychological events with observable consequences.

Objection three: the sample is badly skewed. Extreme cases, literate elites, almost entirely Western, and entirely self-report — and a self-report is already a reconstructed narrative. James admits he chose magnified specimens on purpose, which does not excuse the general conclusions drawn from them.

Objection four: what calibrates the yardstick of fruits? A "better person" by whose standard? James assumes a broadly liberal humanist morality without arguing for it, which leaves intense religious experience that bears bad fruit — fanaticism, persecution, martyr-driven violence — in an awkward position in his scheme. He can only say that such saintliness lacked balance, which presupposes what he needs to show.

A contemporary echo, told honestly. The line James opened with that lungful of gas was picked up again by the psychedelic research of the 1960s and, after 2006, by the psilocybin studies at Johns Hopkins: volunteers' reports score on standard scales as classic mystical experiences, and a substantial share still describe the session months later as among the most meaningful events of their lives. That does corroborate the "filmiest of screens." But it is not "James vindicated": these studies have small samples, cannot be genuinely blinded (subjects know what they took), and rely on heavily self-selected volunteers — and the gap between "the experience was profoundly meaningful" and "the experience disclosed reality" is precisely the gap James himself drew, and said science cannot cross.

James beyond this book

Read only this one and you will mistake James for a scholar of religion; he was first a psychologist and a philosopher, and nearly every move here is powered by tools forged elsewhere. The Principles of Psychology (1890) gave us the "stream of consciousness" — awareness is not a string of beads but a continuous flow carrying fringes and halos at every moment; the subliminal margin is that picture extended. The same book's account of habit (habit is the enormous flywheel of society; by thirty, character has set like plaster — paraphrased) explains why a wholesale change of centre is so rare and so violent. "The Will to Believe" (1897) supplies the logic behind the section on saints: where evidence cannot decide, a choice is forced, and the choice affects the result, believing first is legitimate. Pragmatism (1907) gives the full version of the fruits criterion: the meaning of an idea is the practical difference it makes. Essays in Radical Empiricism (1912, posthumous) supplies his pluralistic universe: the world is not a finished whole but a mass of things still in the process of connecting. Put the four together and the finite God at the end of this book stops looking like a throwaway: in an unfinished world there was never any room for an omnipotent one.

Ten sentences

1. He shrinks religion to its smallest unit: what a person feels, does and undergoes alone, insofar as he takes himself to stand in relation to the divine. Churches and theologies are second-hand — somebody else's original experience, gone cold.

2. By their fruits ye shall know them, not by their roots. Calling Paul epileptic and Teresa hysterical disposes of nothing; turn the same move around and the sceptic's scepticism is equally a product of his nervous system.

3. Origin does not adjudicate value. Explaining how a belief arose and establishing whether it is right are two jobs, and most people clock off after the first.

4. The once-born see evil as a correctable error; the twice-born see it as an ingredient of the world. James rules for the second: their worldview can hold every fact the first one knows, and not the other way round.

5. Conversion adds no belief. It moves the habitual centre of personal energy: ideas that sat cold at the periphery become the hot middle, and the sorting key for everything else changes with them.

6. Some things recede the harder you push — sleep, natural laughter, not being nervous. The self-surrender conversion is not mysterious; it is the structure of that class of goal, where the effort is the obstacle.

7. Four marks of mysticism: ineffability, noetic quality (the conviction of having come to know), transiency, passivity. The second is decisive — it is what turns a psychological curiosity into a philosophical problem.

8. "Our normal waking consciousness is but one special type of consciousness, whilst all about it, parted from it by the filmiest of screens, there lie potential forms entirely different." No account of the universe claiming completeness may pretend otherwise.

9. On the authority of such states he gives three rulings: binding on the one who had them, binding on nobody else, and sufficient to end rational consciousness's monopoly over what counts as real. That monopoly, not God, was always the target.

10. The last line is his signature: God is real since he produces real effects. And his own over-belief is labelled as such — a piecemeal, finite God. In an unfinished world there was never room for an omnipotent one.
