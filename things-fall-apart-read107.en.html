Things Fall Apart

Things Fall Apart · Chinua Achebe · 1958

中文 →

In One Sentence

In the 1890s, on the lower Niger, the nine Igbo villages of Umuofia have their own gods, courts, debates, poetry and dignity; their most famous man, Okonkwo, is afraid of exactly one thing — being thought weak. Then the white man arrives: first a lone stranger on an "iron horse," then a church, a school, a court and a trading store. There is never a proper war, yet the society comes loose from the inside, and Okonkwo ends his life hanging from a tree behind his compound. The book's argument: a civilization is rarely defeated — it is taken apart, the outside blade sliding into cracks that were already there; and this time, for the first time, the story is told by an African.

Where It Sits

Achebe (1930–2013) was a Nigerian Igbo, the son of an early Christian catechist, who studied English literature at the University College in Ibadan. Things Fall Apart, his first novel, written in his twenties, is routinely called the starting point of modern African literature: the first book to tell colonial contact in English, from the inside, as an African tragedy rather than as scenery for a European adventure. It opens what became his "African Trilogy."

The Central Claims

  • Precolonial Africa was not a "dark continent without history." Umuofia has law, religion, an economy, etiquette, rhetoric and fierce internal argument. It is not a blank page, so colonialism did not "bring civilization" — it swapped one civilization for another.
  • But it was no paradise, either. This world throws newborn twins into the forest, executes an innocent boy as compensation, and brands a whole caste as permanent outcasts. Achebe writes these cracks honestly, because it was precisely through them that Christianity first won the people their own society had thrown away — the collapse was a collusion of outside force and inner wound.
  • The personal tragedy and the social tragedy are one line. Fear of weakness makes Okonkwo rigid; faced with something new, Umuofia splits into those who bend and those who will not. A man who only knows how to stand firm snaps at a moment that demands flexibility. And the colonial officer who finds his body plans to give him "a reasonable paragraph" in a book — whoever holds the pen decides how history remembers a man.

The Core Concepts, One by One

1. Manhood built out of fear: who Okonkwo really is

The first sentence establishes him: Okonkwo is well known throughout the nine villages and beyond. At eighteen he threw Amalinze the Cat, a wrestler unbeaten for seven years. He then built himself up from nothing, season after season of yams — in Igbo eyes "the king of crops," a man's crop and the measure of wealth — and earned three wives, two titles and a name as a warrior.

Achebe immediately shows us the engine underneath: his whole life was ruled by fear — fear of failure and of weakness (paraphrased). Its source is his father, Unoka: a gentle, flute-playing, palm-wine-loving debtor who never took a title and who died of a swelling sickness, carried into the Evil Forest (the forbidden ground where those who die abominable deaths are left). As a boy, Okonkwo heard a playmate call Unoka agbala and learned that the word meant both "woman" and "a man who has taken no title." From then on, "like my father," "like a woman" and "weak" were the same thing in his mind, and he spent his life becoming his father's opposite.

That engine makes him a success, and it drives the worst thing he ever does. A neighboring village, Mbaino, has murdered a daughter of Umuofia; to avert war it hands over a boy, Ikemefuna, who is placed in Okonkwo's household. Over three years the boy becomes an elder brother to Okonkwo's son Nwoye and calls Okonkwo father. Okonkwo grows fond of him — but never shows it, because showing affection, to him, is a kind of weakness. Then the Oracle of the Hills and Caves decrees that the boy must die. The elder Ezeudu quietly warns him: have nothing to do with it; that boy calls you father. On the path, when Ikemefuna runs toward him crying "My father, they have killed me!", Okonkwo draws his machete and cuts him down — because he is afraid of being thought weak.

The analogy: he is a pillar poured full of steel so it will never bend. It bears more load than anyone — but it has no give, and what cannot bend does not bow at its limit; it breaks.

Why it matters: the most common misreading treats Okonkwo as the embodiment of Igbo tradition. Achebe built him to be the opposite. The society itself is not lopsided the way he is; it prizes balance. During the Week of Peace, when no one may strike another, Okonkwo beats his wife and is fined by the priest of Ani, the earth goddess. His uncle Uchendu teaches him that "Mother is supreme" (Nneka): a man belongs to his fatherland when life is sweet, but in sorrow he takes refuge in his motherland. His friend Obierika doubts, hesitates, and declines to join the party that kills Ikemefuna. Okonkwo is not Igbo culture; he is a distortion of it — one who has mistaken the culture's masculine half for the whole. He even wishes that Ezinma, the daughter who understands him best, had been born a boy.

How it changes your view: a tragic character is often not someone with too many flaws, but someone who has pushed a single virtue so far that its opposite is no longer allowed to exist. In fleeing his father, Okonkwo cut away even what was good in him — tenderness, music, an unguarded warmth toward people. And in cutting down Ikemefuna, he also pushed Nwoye toward the church door.

2. "Proverbs are the palm-oil with which words are eaten": a world with its own logic

The novel has three parts and twenty-five chapters. Part One takes up thirteen of them, contains almost no white people, and simply shows how Umuofia lives. Many readers find it slow; but Part One is the argument.

Among the Igbo, Achebe writes, conversation is a highly regarded art, and "proverbs are the palm-oil with which words are eaten." Palm oil is what you dip dry yam into so it will go down; words, too, need dressing before they can be swallowed with dignity. So we watch a man come to ask for a loan and circle through proverb after proverb before naming his business. We watch disputes brought before the egwugwu — ancestral spirits, impersonated by masked, titled men, who sit in public judgment. A husband has beaten his wife, her brothers have taken her home, both sides plead before the whole village, and the spirits rule that the husband must bring a pot of wine and beg her to return. Okonkwo is behind one of the masks; everyone half-knows and no one says.

There is no king; power is spread across councils of elders, titles, oracles and public assemblies, and status comes from achievement, not birth: age is respected, Achebe says, but achievement is revered, and "if a child washed his hands he could eat with kings." Each person has a chi, a personal god or guiding spirit: "when a man says yes, his chi says yes also" — will can steer destiny.

The analogy: it is like a language you don't speak. Outsiders hear noise and conclude it has no grammar; spend six months with it and you find full inflections, honorifics and rhetoric. Those thirteen chapters are Achebe's six-month course.

Why it matters: the book had targets. Achebe later said one spur was reading Mister Johnson (1939), Joyce Cary's novel set in Nigeria, whose African hero is comic, childish and hollow. The bigger target was Conrad's Heart of Darkness: in a famous later lecture Achebe attacked it for using Africa as backdrop and setting for a European's spiritual crisis, with Africans who mostly howl and are barely granted speech. The whole point of Part One is to prove that people here are talking — and talking with great care. In his own words (paraphrased): African peoples did not hear of culture for the first time from Europeans; their societies were not mindless but often had a philosophy of great depth, value and beauty; they had poetry and, above all, dignity.

How it changes your view: before you call an unfamiliar society backward, ask whether you simply don't understand its language. No writing is not the same as no law; no state is not the same as no order.

3. The cracks were already there: twins, outcasts, and a snapped bow

Achebe does not write a pastoral. Umuofia's custom declares twins an abomination: newborns are put in earthenware pots and left in the Evil Forest, and Obierika has done it to his own wife's children. Ikemefuna, killed on the Oracle's word, had done nothing at all. And there are the osu — people dedicated to a god, and their descendants — who may never marry the freeborn, cut their hair or take titles: a hereditary outcast caste.

These customs are not background; each leaves a wound in someone. Nwoye first feels "something give way inside him" when he hears abandoned twins crying in the forest; the second time is the night Ikemefuna fails to come home — like the snapping of a tightened bow, Achebe writes (paraphrased). After Okonkwo's exile, Obierika sits alone and wonders why a man should suffer so grievously for an accident, and what crime his own twin children had committed. He cannot answer, and nobody else can either.

So when the missionaries arrive, the first to walk into the church are not the pillars of the clan but its offcuts: the osu; the efulefu, worthless men — like someone who sold his machete and went to war with an empty sheath; mothers of twins; and one son terrified of his father. What draws Nwoye is not the "mad logic of the Trinity," which he cannot follow, but the poetry of the new religion: a hymn about brothers who sat in darkness and in fear seems to answer the question that has haunted him — the twins crying in the bush, and Ikemefuna who was killed (paraphrased). The church shaves the osu's matted hair and rescues abandoned twins. It offers exactly what the old order refused to give.

The analogy: when a wall comes down under a sledgehammer, you blame the hammer; but anyone who has knocked down walls knows you swing where the cracks already are.

Why it matters: this is what separates Things Fall Apart from ordinary anti-colonial protest, and why it rewards rereading. Achebe rejects both the colonizer's "they were savages" and the nationalist's "we were perfect." A culture that treats some of its own members as refuse has already recruited the outsider's first converts.

How it changes your view: whenever an organization, a community or a country is "breached" from outside, don't only ask how strong the attacker was; ask how many defenders had long since stopped wanting to defend — and why.

4. "He has put a knife on the things that held us together": colonialism as dismantling, not war

In Parts Two and Three, colonial power arrives in layers. Each layer is "gentle"; together they are fatal. This is the order Achebe lays out:

What arrivesHow it gets inWhat it takes apart
A lesson in force (Abame)The neighboring clan of Abame kills a white man on an "iron horse" (a bicycle); months later, white men massacre the village on market dayTeaches everyone that open resistance means a village vanishes
The churchThe clan grants the missionaries land in the Evil Forest, expecting the spirits to kill them; they don't dieThe authority of the gods — the taboo has failed
School and hospitalThe mild Mr. Brown builds them; the literate become court clerks and messengersThe source of status — achievement starts to mean "knowing the white man's letters"
The court and its messengersA District Commissioner judges by his own law, served by messengers mostly from other peoplesThe egwugwu's authority — the old court is hollowed out
The store and moneyA trading post pays high prices for palm oil and kernels; money flows into Umuofia for the first timeThe will to resist — many people are doing well

Table: the layers by which Umuofia is taken apart (arranged from the novel's plot). No single layer looks like invasion; stacked, they replace the whole order.

When Okonkwo returns after seven years of exile, he barely recognizes the clan. He wants to take up arms and drive the strangers out; Obierika does the arithmetic for him: our own brothers have joined the white man, and our own people run errands for his court. Then he gives the title its meaning — the white man is very clever; he came quietly and peaceably with his religion; we laughed at his foolishness and let him stay; now he has won our brothers, and our clan can no longer act like one; he has put a knife on the things that held us together, and we have fallen apart (paraphrased).

The turn comes through a substitution. Mr. Brown, who debates patiently with the elder Akunna about chi and God and concludes that a frontal attack on the clan's religion would fail, falls ill and leaves. His replacement, Mr. Smith, is a zealot. A convert named Enoch publicly unmasks an egwugwu during the ceremony for the earth goddess — to the Igbo, the killing of an ancestral spirit. The spirits burn the church. The District Commissioner invites six leaders to a "palaver," handcuffs them, has their heads shaved and fines the village two hundred bags of cowries (shell currency); his messengers quietly raise the figure to two hundred and fifty.

At the final assembly, five court messengers arrive to break it up. Okonkwo beheads their leader with his machete. The crowd does not follow; it lets the others escape, and someone asks, "Why did he do it?" — and Okonkwo knows that Umuofia will not go to war. He goes home and hangs himself. Igbo custom makes suicide an offense against the earth; his own clansmen may not touch his body, and strangers must be asked to cut him down. Obierika tells the Commissioner: that man was one of the greatest men in Umuofia; you drove him to kill himself, and now he will be buried like a dog (paraphrased).

Why it matters: most civilizations are not lost on a battlefield; they quietly lose the ability to act together once their members, one by one, have found another way to live. Okonkwo's tragedy is that he is running the wartime script — someone charges, everyone follows — inside a society that has already been rewired.

How it changes your view: the most effective form of coercion is often not a gun but a rewrite of the incentives — who gets promoted, who gets paid, who gets to judge. By the day resistance is needed, people discover they can no longer "act like one."

5. "A reasonable paragraph": whoever holds the pen writes the history

In the final paragraph the point of view abruptly switches into the District Commissioner's head. Looking at the body in the tree, he thinks about the book he is writing, whose title he has already chosen — The Pacification of the Primitive Tribes of the Lower Niger. The story of this man who hanged himself would make interesting reading, he reflects; perhaps not a whole chapter, but a reasonable paragraph.

For two hundred pages we have wrestled, planted, killed, gone into exile, come home and despaired with Okonkwo; in the colonial archive all of it will be compressed into one paragraph, filed under "pacification." It is the coldest irony in the book: the novel you have just finished is the rebuttal to that paragraph. In an interview Achebe cited an African proverb: until the lions have their own historians, the history of the hunt will always glorify the hunter. Things Fall Apart is the lions' historian at work.

The irony is in the title too. It comes from W. B. Yeats's "The Second Coming," whose opening lines serve as the epigraph: the falcon, turning in a widening gyre, can no longer hear the falconer; "things fall apart; the centre cannot hold; mere anarchy is loosed upon the world." Yeats was writing about European civilization unravelling after the First World War. By borrowing a European poem intact and setting it over an African clan, Achebe is saying: you write elegies for the collapse of your civilization; ours collapsed too, and deserves the same grief.

Language is a weapon as well. Achebe chose English, but packed it with Igbo proverbs, Igbo words and the Igbo habit of approaching a point sideways. As he put it (paraphrased), English can carry the weight of my African experience — but it will have to be a new English, still in communion with its ancestral home but altered to suit its new surroundings. The novel has a small joke on the theme: the missionaries' interpreter speaks a different dialect and keeps saying "my buttocks" when he means "myself," while the villagers try not to laugh through his sermon. Translation is never neutral; who translates, and into what, is itself power.

How it changes your view: whenever you read an official account, ask: who wrote this paragraph — and where are the two hundred pages it compressed?

6. Chi and fate: how far will can go

The Igbo saying that "when a man says yes, his chi says yes also" has a living example in Okonkwo: his father left him nothing and he rose with his own hands. In the year he first borrowed seed yams as a young man, drought was followed by torrential rain, the harvest was ruined, and one farmer hanged himself; Okonkwo survived it, and concluded that since he had survived that year, he would survive anything.

Yet elsewhere the novel quietly revises the saying. At Ezeudu's funeral Okonkwo's gun explodes and kills the dead man's sixteen-year-old son — Ezeudu again, the elder who had warned him about Ikemefuna. By custom this is a "female" crime (an accidental killing, as opposed to the "male" crime of deliberate murder), punished by seven years of exile. The narrator observes: his chi was clearly not made for great things; a man could not rise beyond the destiny of his chi (paraphrased).

The analogy: your chi is a business partner — you do the work, he nods, and things succeed; but he has his own mind, and you cannot fire him. Achebe offers no verdict: Okonkwo's ruin is part character, part history, and part something nobody can name.

Why it matters: this is what makes the book a genuine tragedy rather than political propaganda. If colonialism alone had destroyed Okonkwo, he would be a victim; because his own fear, rigidity and machete also took part, he is a tragic hero — in Aristotle's sense of hamartia, a fundamentally decent man brought down by a fatal error of judgment.

How it changes your view: will can drag you out of your father's ruins, but it cannot hold up an entire era as it falls — and the people least able to see that limit are often the bravest.

The Distilled Spine

① First, build a complete world (Part One): Umuofia has courts, gods, an economy, rhetoric and argument — refuting the colonial story of an Africa with no history or culture.

② At the same time, honestly mark its cracks: twins, Ikemefuna, the osu. This world also wounds its own members, and some (Nwoye, Obierika) already doubt it.

③ Set up a man who mistakes half the culture for all of it: Okonkwo, rigid from fear of weakness; his error (killing Ikemefuna) and his misfortune (the accidental killing and exile) take him away for seven years.

④ While he is gone, the world is rewired (Part Two): the church settles in the Evil Forest and gathers in the discarded — his own son among them.

⑤ When he returns, power has changed hands (Part Three): school, court, money; the clan can no longer act as one.

⑥ The old script meets the new world: he strikes, and no one follows; he dies in the one way his clan cannot forgive — cast out by the very code he spent his life defending.

⑦ The last paragraph hands the camera to the colonizer: the whole tragedy shrinks to "a reasonable paragraph" — and the book itself is the answer to that paragraph.

What it proves, in a sentence: a civilization falls when outside force, inner cracks and individual character lock together — and only when the people being described pick up the pen does that fall stop being a footnote in someone else's book.

Misreadings, Criticism, Controversy

Misreading 1: it's a pastoral elegy for precolonial Africa. It isn't. Achebe gives as much space to abandoned infants, a killed foster son, wife-beating and the caste of outcasts. His claim is "we had dignity," not "we had no problems." Read it as nostalgia and you miss why Nwoye left home.

Misreading 2: Okonkwo is the hero or representative of Igbo tradition. He is an extremist within his culture, and the culture itself punishes him again and again — a fine, exile, and finally a burial his own people may not perform. The voices of Igbo wisdom are closer to the doubting Obierika and to Uchendu with his "Mother is supreme."

Misreading 3: it's an ethnographic record. Umuofia is a fictional composite, and Achebe, raised in a Christian household, had sympathies on both sides. The missionaries aren't cut from one cloth: Mr. Brown is restrained and listens; Mr. Smith is the zealot. It is a novel with a point of view, not a field report.

Criticism 1: the language question. The Kenyan writer Ngũgĩ wa Thiong'o argued in Decolonising the Mind (1986) that writing in the colonizer's language prolongs colonization of the mind, and switched to writing in Gikuyu. Achebe's answer: English was already the common tongue among Nigeria's many peoples; the question was not whether to use it but whether it could be remade into "a new English." The debate is still open.

Criticism 2: women. Feminist critics (Florence Stratton among them) point out that the women are mostly background — beaten, inherited, assigned the lesser crops — and that the narration reports domestic violence in a near-level tone; the priestess Chielo and "Mother is supreme" exist but never reach the center of power. Defenders reply that Achebe is faithfully depicting that patriarchal order, which is exactly where Okonkwo's obsession lives; but depicting a male-centered world and narrating from inside a male point of view are not the same thing.

Criticism 3: structure and the portrayal of the British. Some readers find Part One too slow, almost a folklore exhibit, and Parts Two and Three rushed; the white characters, especially Smith and the Commissioner, are thin. Others note that the book also accommodates Western readers — explanatory passages, and the glossary of Igbo words added to later editions — so it carries its own tension about whom it is addressing.

The Achebe Beyond This Book

Things Fall Apart only reaches the threshold of colonial rule. Achebe's later books ask the next question: once the old world has come apart, who carries the new one — and how well?

  • No Longer at Ease (1960): the hero is Obi Okonkwo, Okonkwo's grandson — the son of Nwoye after his conversion. Sent to England on money his village scraped together, he returns to a civil-service job in Lagos, idealistic and against corruption, and ends up arrested for taking a bribe. The grandfather was destroyed because he would not bend; the grandson, because he bent too far — caught between the clan's web of obligations and the colonial system's salary, with neither to lean on.
  • Arrow of God (1964): many critics consider it his most accomplished novel. The chief priest Ezeulu, detained by a colonial officer, misses the days on which he must eat the sacred yams; back home he insists on eating them one a month as custom requires and refuses to call the New Yam festival early. The people cannot harvest, and turn to the church's harvest festival instead. This time the collapse is pushed along not by outsiders but by a priest's rigid fidelity to the rules — and by his feud with rivals in his own community.
  • A Man of the People (1966) and Anthills of the Savannah (1987): turning to independent Africa, they satirize corrupt politicians and military dictatorship; the first ends in a coup, and came out at almost the same moment as Nigeria's first military coup.
  • The essays: the lecture "An Image of Africa: Racism in Conrad's Heart of Darkness" (1975) and "The Novelist as Teacher" (1965), which holds that one of the African writer's first tasks is to help his society regain belief in itself. His late memoir There Was a Country (2012) returns to the Biafran War (1967–70, the civil war that followed the Igbo-led secession), which he lived through.

Running through all of it is an Igbo proverb he liked to quote (paraphrased): "Wherever something stands, something else will stand beside it." No single truth; every view needs another standing next to it. That is both his reason for attacking the colonial story and his reason for refusing to romanticize his own.

Ten Sentences

① His whole life was ruled by fear — of failure and of weakness. (paraphrased) A man's greatest weakness is often the very thing he is running from.

② Agbala means a woman, and also a man who has taken no title. Equating tenderness with failure is where all his violence begins.

③ He cut down the boy who called him father, because he was afraid of being thought weak. (paraphrased) What we do to prove we are not weak is often the weakest thing we do.

④ Proverbs are the palm-oil with which words are eaten. A people who talk this carefully were never a "darkness without culture."

⑤ When a man says yes, his chi says yes also. Will can pull you out of the ruins; it cannot hold up an era.

⑥ The cries of twins in the forest, and the night Ikemefuna did not come home, snapped something in Nwoye like a tightened bow. (paraphrased) A culture is breached first through the people it has wounded.

⑦ A man belongs to his fatherland when life is sweet, and seeks refuge in his motherland in sorrow. (paraphrased) Strength without suppleness does not survive adversity.

⑧ He has put a knife on the things that held us together, and we have fallen apart. (paraphrased) Civilizations are rarely beaten; they are taken apart.

⑨ "Why did he do it?" When a man charges with the old script and no one follows, he learns the world has already changed.

⑩ Perhaps not a whole chapter, but a reasonable paragraph. (paraphrased) Until the lions have their own historians, the hunt glorifies the hunter — this book is the lions' account.
