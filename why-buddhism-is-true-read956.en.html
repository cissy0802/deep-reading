DEEP READING · OFF-LIST

Why Buddhism Is True

Robert Wright · 2017

中文 →

In One Sentence

Your brain was built by natural selection to get genes into the next generation, not to make you happy and not to show you the truth — so it comes loaded with feelings and illusions that mislead you; the Buddha diagnosed this 2,500 years ago without any theory of evolution, and meditation is the tool for seeing through the factory settings.

Where It Sits

Wright is an American science writer and one of the earliest popularizers of evolutionary psychology (The Moral Animal, 1994); he also taught an online course at Princeton on Buddhism and modern psychology. This book is a secular, long-time, self-confessedly mediocre meditator trying to underwrite Buddhism's core diagnosis with evolutionary psychology. It lands at the same crossroads as our #73, The Embodied Mind, but arrives by way of Darwin rather than phenomenology.

The Core Argument

  • A mind shaped by natural selection is systematically dissatisfied and systematically deluded. Pleasure that fades and desire that always points to the next thing aren't bugs; they're the design. Buddhism calls the result dukkha.
  • Buddhism's two most counterintuitive claims, "not-self" and "emptiness," find real support in modern psychology: there's no executive "I" running the show, and the "essences" we perceive in things are largely labels attached by feeling.
  • Meditation, especially mindfulness or insight practice, opens a gap between feeling and reaction, letting you partly slip the programming — which means less suffering, clearer sight, and better behavior. That's the sense of "true" in the title: not that every Buddhist doctrine is true, but that its diagnosis of the human predicament and its prescription are basically right.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Dukkha: pleasure engineered to evaporate

The book opens with The Matrix: Neo swallows the red pill and discovers he has been living inside an illusion spun by machines. Buddhism, Wright says, is a red pill — and the machine spinning the illusion is natural selection.

The Pali word dukkha is usually translated "suffering," but it means something closer to "unsatisfactoriness": not just pain, but the chronic sense that something is a little off, that the next thing will finally do it. Wright's evolutionary account of why is crisp. For an animal, the pleasure of eating, mating or rising in status has to meet three design specs: (1) it has to exist, or you won't pursue it; (2) it can't last, or you'll stop pursuing; (3) your attention has to be on how great the next one will be, not on how fast the last one faded.

Psychologists call this the hedonic treadmill: the lift from a raise, a house, a promotion drifts back to baseline within months, and you start eyeing the next goal. You think it's your personal greed or restlessness; actually it's a finely tuned motivation machine working exactly as intended. It was never built to satisfy you. Satisfied animals don't reproduce.

How it changes the way you see: "why am I not happy now that I have it?" stops being a riddle about your character and becomes a fact about design. There's an odd relief in that: you no longer have to believe the next thing will be the one that finally does it. The Buddha's claim that craving (taṇhā) is the source of suffering now comes with a mechanical explanation.

2. Feelings are suggestions — and often false ones

We instinctively treat feelings as reports on the world: fear means danger, liking means good. Wright argues they're better seen as behavioral suggestions natural selection slips you, there to push you toward actions that (in the ancestral environment) helped pass on genes. Whether what they "report" is accurate is secondary.

The best illustration is the evolutionary psychiatrist Randolph Nesse's smoke detector principle: a smoke detector should go off a hundred times when you burn toast rather than miss one real fire, because a false alarm is cheap and a miss is fatal. Anxiety works the same way. For an ancestor, mistaking wind in the brush for a snake cost a few wasted steps; mistaking a snake for wind cost everything. So our anxiety is tuned hypersensitive, and most of its alarms are false — tomorrow's presentation, a look from the boss, an email with an ambiguous tone all trigger fear far out of proportion to the actual risk.

Worse, these feelings were calibrated for a completely different world. In a hunter-gatherer band of a few dozen people, being cast out was close to a death sentence, so social anxiety ran hot. In a world of eight billion strangers, bombing a talk has trivial real consequences, yet the alarm still fires at "you're about to be expelled from the tribe" intensity. That's environmental mismatch: feelings that were once accurate are often false today.

How it changes the way you see: you start separating "I feel…" from "it is the case that…". A strong feeling no longer automatically counts as reliable information; it's a proposal you can inspect. What meditation trains is the capacity to look at the proposal without immediately obeying it.

3. The modular mind: nobody's in charge

We picture an "I" in the cockpit, taking in information, weighing options, making the call. Drawing on evolutionary psychologists like Robert Kurzban and Douglas Kenrick, Wright argues that the mind is better described as a set of semi-independent modules — self-protection, mate-seeking, status-seeking, friend-making and so on — and whichever one the situation and its feelings activate takes the wheel for a while.

The evidence comes in several kinds. The most famous is Michael Gazzaniga's split-brain research. In epilepsy patients whose hemispheres had been surgically disconnected, if the right hemisphere is shown a snow scene and the left hand picks a matching picture, it picks a shovel. The left hemisphere, which handles speech, never saw the snow — it saw a chicken claw — and when asked why the hand chose a shovel, it instantly invents a plausible reason: you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed. The part of you that talks and explains "why I did that" often doesn't know the real cause; it narrates after the fact.

Kurzban's apt image: the conscious self isn't the CEO, it's the press secretary — it explains the president's decisions fluently to the public, but it didn't make them and is often out of the loop. Another line of evidence comes from priming experiments: one study found people who'd just watched a frightening clip were more inclined to conform, while men who'd watched a romantic clip were more inclined to stand out. Same person, different activated "mode," different preferences. (Priming research has since taken heavy damage in the replication crisis, so discount the specific results; the broader claim that situations reshape personality has more support.)

How it changes the way you see: when you say "I decided," it's worth asking which module decided. The "you" spiraling at 2 a.m. and the "you" after morning coffee may be two different systems talking. That sets up the next idea.

4. Not-self (anattā): the Buddha's argument, psychology's corroboration

Not-self is the Buddhist claim moderns find hardest to swallow. How can there be no self? I'm right here, thinking. Wright handles it cleverly by going back to one of the earliest scriptures, the Anattalakkhana Sutta (the Discourse on the Not-Self Characteristic). There the Buddha takes his first five disciples through the "five aggregates" that make up a person — form (the body), feeling, perception, mental formations (volitions and dispositions), and consciousness — and asks the same thing of each: if this were the self, it would obey you; but it leads to affliction, and you can't say "let my body be thus, not thus" — so it isn't the self.

The test, Wright points out, is control: whatever a self is, it should at least be the thing in charge. And the modular-mind findings suggest there's no central commander uniformly controlling body, feelings and thoughts. Thoughts pop up on their own (try thinking nothing for a minute), feelings arise on their own, and decisions are often made before awareness catches up. So not-self needn't mean "you don't exist"; it means "the CEO you assumed was running everything doesn't exist."

Wright is candid that he isn't defending a metaphysical "absolutely no self." His concern is experiential: in meditation you can actually watch thoughts and feelings come and go on their own; they just happen rather than being done by "me." Once you see a wave of anxiety as "anxiety happening" rather than "I am anxious," it loses some of its grip.

How it changes the way you see: not-self isn't nihilism; it's a loosening. A great deal of suffering comes from over-identifying with "me" — my face, my opinion, my failure. Once you see these as some module raising an alarm, you gain room not to run with it.

5. Emptiness: "essences" are stuck on by feeling

Buddhism's other core claim is emptiness (śūnyatā): nothing has an independent, inherent essence. It sounds even more mystical than not-self, but Wright gives it a down-to-earth psychological version: when we perceive something, we automatically attribute an essence to it, and that essence is largely a label applied by feeling.

The psychologist Paul Bloom has studied our essentialism. The same painting gives people very different pleasure depending on whether they believe it's a Picasso or a forgery, even when they can't tell the difference by looking; objects owned by celebrities fetch absurd prices at auction because people feel something of the person has rubbed off on them. Another study found that the same wine, labeled as expensive, not only tasted better to drinkers but produced stronger activity in brain regions associated with pleasure. You're not tasting just the wine; you're tasting what you believe it is.

Wright extends this to people. Our view of a person is often an "essence" tinted by feeling: a colleague you dislike seems suspect whatever he does; everything someone from the "other side" says reads as evidence of bad faith. Psychology's fundamental attribution error is exactly this: when others mess up, we blame their nature; when we mess up, we blame the circumstances.

In Buddhist terms, every moment of perception carries a layer of vedanā — feeling tone: pleasant, unpleasant or neutral — and that layer drives the craving and aversion that follow. Meditation trains you to see the feeling tone itself rather than being carried by it into labeling the world.

How it changes the way you see: you grow suspicious of judgments that feel obvious at a glance. Emptiness doesn't say things don't exist; it says a good part of the "essence" you see is projected by your own feelings. Noticing that is a first step toward less tribal hostility and fewer misreadings of other people.

6. Meditation: a gap between feeling and reaction

If the first five ideas are the diagnosis, meditation is the treatment. Buddhism's chain of "dependent origination" includes two crucial links: feeling (vedanā) → craving (taṇhā). Pleasant feeling automatically breeds "more"; unpleasant feeling automatically breeds "get it away." Wright sees this as the core mechanism natural selection installed: feelings drive behavior. What mindfulness meditation does is open a gap between those two links — you see the feeling arise, see it clearly, and aren't immediately shoved into chasing or fleeing.

How is it practiced? The basic form is sitting and attending to the breath, noticing when a thought arrives and returning to the breath. A step further is to place attention directly on an uncomfortable feeling — a spike of anxiety, an itch, a craving — not pushing it away but examining where it sits in the body, what its texture is, how it changes. Many practitioners report that when you look this way the feeling remains but feels less like "mine," and its orders lose their urgency.

Wright cites supporting research. The psychiatrist Judson Brewer's team found that experienced meditators show markedly reduced activity in the brain's default mode network — regions associated with mind-wandering, rumination and self-referential thought — while meditating. In another of their studies, mindfulness training helped smokers quit better than a standard cessation program, and the key was teaching people to observe their cravings with curiosity rather than white-knuckle them.

How it changes the way you see: you stop treating "suppress" and "indulge" as the only options. The third way is to watch: let the feeling come, see it clearly, and it leaves on its own. It isn't willpower defeating desire; it's desire losing its authority to command.

7. Seeing clearly makes you better: truth, happiness and morality converge

The book's last ambition is moral. Wright's central claim is that in Buddhism, seeing more clearly, suffering less and becoming better are the same thing, because the illusions that make us suffer — runaway anxiety, attachment to self, snap judgments about other people's essence — are the very illusions that make us selfish, bigoted and hostile.

Someone who sees that his anger is one module sounding an alarm, and that his loathing of the "other side" is partly a label stuck on by feeling, is harder to sweep up in tribal passion. Wright ties this to a concern that runs through his work: in a world where technology makes humanity ever more interdependent, a mind still running on tribal "us versus them" settings could be catastrophic. Meditation, for him, isn't just personal stress relief; it may be a civilizational necessity.

How it changes the way you see: it challenges a popular assumption — that truth hurts and illusion comforts. For Wright, at least where the mind is concerned, it's the reverse: the illusions are what hurt; seeing through them is the release.

The Skeleton

Premise: the brain is a product of natural selection; its goal is gene replication, not your happiness or your accurate knowledge.

Inference 1: so pleasure must fade and desire never ends (dukkha), and feelings are often false alarms (delusion).

Inference 2: the mind is competing modules with no unified controller (not-self), and the "essences" we perceive are colored by feeling (emptiness).

Parallel: the Buddha reached broadly the same diagnosis 2,500 years ago through introspection.

Treatment: meditation reveals the gap between feeling and reaction, loosening the programming's grip.

Payoff: seeing more clearly, suffering less, being less hostile — one thing, not three.

What the book establishes is this: Buddhism's diagnosis of the human mind isn't mysticism but a user's manual partly testable by evolutionary psychology and neuroscience, and meditation is a technique that can, to a degree, rewrite the factory settings. It rests on one clear bridge: natural selection explains why the mind deceives you; Buddhism and meditation explain how to see through it.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "The title claims all Buddhist doctrine is true." Wright says plainly he's setting aside rebirth, karma and the supernatural; his subject is a meditation-centered, naturalistic "Western Buddhism." "True" means the diagnosis of the human predicament and the prescription are basically right — and he concedes the title is a deliberate provocation.

Misreading 2: "Not-self means I don't exist." Wright stresses repeatedly that the point is the absence of a central controller, and the possibility of not identifying with thoughts and feelings in experience — not a denial of continuous memory, personality or body.

Criticism 1: a cherry-picked, domesticated Buddhism. The philosopher Evan Thompson — one of the authors of our #73, The Embodied Mind — took direct aim at Wright, among others, in Why I Am Not a Buddhist (2020). His target is "Buddhist exceptionalism," the idea that Buddhism is more scientific or rational than other religions. Thompson argues that this story strips Buddhism from its religious, ritual and ethical traditions, keeps only what fits modern science, and then announces that science has confirmed it — which is circular.

Criticism 2: evolutionary just-so stories. Many evolutionary-psychology explanations — this trait exists "in order to" solve some ancestral problem — are hard to test directly. Critics call them "just-so stories," after Kipling's children's tales of how the leopard got its spots: the story is neat but nearly impossible to falsify. Modularity itself is still debated in cognitive science, not settled.

Criticism 3: overhyped meditation science. In 2018 a group of psychologists and neuroscientists published "Mind the Hype," arguing that mindfulness research is riddled with small samples, weak control groups and inconsistent definitions, and that media and industry claims far outrun the evidence. Many studies Wright cites should be read in that light; some priming results have also been hit by the replication crisis.

Criticism 4: "McMindfulness." Critics like the sociologist Ronald Purser argue that packaging meditation as a personal stress-reduction and productivity tool strips out Buddhism's original critique of greed and social injustice, teaching people to be exploited more calmly inside unjust systems. Wright's book is much better than the typical mindfulness bestseller on this — he insists on the moral dimension — but his framework remains centered on individual psychology.

Wright Beyond This Book

This book is where three decades of Wright's thinking land. His breakout The Moral Animal (1994), a classic popularization of evolutionary psychology, argued that our moral sense itself — guilt, gratitude, indignation, the obsession with fairness — is a product of natural selection, serving genes rather than necessarily serving goodness. That lays the entire foundation for this book's claim that feelings are misleading suggestions: if even our moral feelings may be designed to steer us, then seeing through feelings becomes a precondition for moral progress.

In Nonzero (2000) he argued that history has a direction: from zero-sum games (I win, you lose) toward ever more non-zero-sum games (both can win), as trade, technology and communication bind more people's fates together. But our minds are still running tribal, zero-sum settings. That's the source of this book's closing moral urgency: meditation, for Wright, is one way to let the mind catch up with the world.

Crossovers

  • Western philosophy · Stoic impression and assent (Epictetus): "It is not things that disturb us, but our judgments about things," says Enchiridion 5. The Stoics split the impression (phantasia) from the assent (synkatathesis) and trained the pause between them, which is Wright's gap between feeling and reaction. The difference: the Stoics hand the vetting to a rational ruling faculty, the hegemonikon, which reinstalls exactly the CEO Wright wants to dismiss.
  • Complexity science · Harvester ants without a boss (Deborah Gordon): In Gordon's harvester-ant colonies nobody gives orders; whether an ant goes out to forage depends on how often it meets returning foragers, and the colony's "decision" emerges from local encounters. That is the modular mind, minus one thing: no ant steps up afterward to announce that it decided.
  • Quantum physics · Rovelli's relational quantum mechanics (1996): Carlo Rovelli argues that physical quantities have definite values only relative to another system, with no intrinsic properties outside relations, and in Helgoland he explicitly invokes Nagarjuna. It rhymes with emptiness, but his claim is about physical variables, while Wright's emptiness is about essences that feelings paint onto perception.
  • Investing · Myopic loss aversion (Benartzi & Thaler, 1995): Losses hurt roughly twice as much as equal gains, so the more often you check your portfolio, the more losses you see and the less stock you can bear to hold, which helps explain the equity premium puzzle. It is the smoke detector misfiring in a new environment; the difference is that a market false alarm is not a few wasted steps but real compounding forgone.

The Book in Ten Sentences

① The brain was designed to pass on genes, not to make you happy or show you the truth.

② Pleasure is built to fade and always promise that the next one will be better — the mechanical version of what the Buddha called dukkha.

③ Feelings are behavioral suggestions, not factual reports; anxiety is a smoke detector that would rather go off a hundred times than miss once.

④ Many feelings were accurate in the tribe and are false alarms today — the environment changed, the settings didn't.

⑤ The mind is competing modules; the conscious "I" is more press secretary than CEO.

⑥ The Buddha's test for self was control: if it were me, it would obey me — and neither thoughts nor feelings do.

⑦ Emptiness doesn't say things don't exist; it says much of the "essence" you see is stuck on by your own feelings.

⑧ The heart of meditation is opening a gap between feeling and craving: see it clearly, and you don't have to obey.

⑨ Seeing clearly, suffering less and becoming better are one thing: the illusions that hurt you are the same ones that make you bigoted and hostile.

⑩ Read the "true" in the title as "the diagnosis and prescription are basically right," not "every doctrine is true" — which is exactly why it draws the strong charge of cherry-picking Buddhism.
