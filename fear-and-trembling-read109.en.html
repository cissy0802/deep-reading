Fear and Trembling

Frygt og Bæven · Søren Kierkegaard, writing as "Johannes de silentio" · 1843

中文 →

In One Sentence

Genesis 22: God tells Abraham to take his son, his only son Isaac, whom he loves, up Mount Moriah and offer him as a burnt offering. Abraham asks nothing. He saddles his donkey early the next morning, travels three days, binds the boy and raises the knife — and only then does an angel call out, and a ram takes Isaac's place. For millennia the pulpit has held this up as a model of faith. Kierkegaard makes you stop at the moment the knife is raised and ask the question nobody wants to ask: if ethics says that killing your son is murder, and Abraham is nonetheless honored as the father of faith, then either faith is something higher than ethics, or Abraham is simply a man who tried to kill his child — there is no third option. The whole book presses on that either/or: what must faith be, if it deserves to be approached in fear and trembling?

Where It Sits

Kierkegaard (1813–1855), a Dane from Copenhagen, is widely regarded as the forerunner of existentialism — the strand of philosophy that puts "how does a particular person live?" ahead of "what is the world made of?" Fear and Trembling appeared in October 1843 under the pseudonym Johannes de silentio ("John of silence"); it is his most widely read book, and his most widely misread. It was written two years after he broke off his engagement to Regine Olsen, and many scholars see his own shadow in an Abraham who gives up what he loves for a higher calling — but that is conjecture; he never said so.

The Core Argument

  • Faith is not a credulous stand-in for reason, nor a beginner's stage that philosophy can "go beyond." It is the highest passion a human being can reach, and it begins exactly where thinking leaves off. The fashionable Hegelian philosophy of the day believed it had digested faith into its rational system and "gone further." Kierkegaard's reply: you have not even reached the starting line.
  • If Abraham is in the right, there must be a "teleological suspension of the ethical": a single person, as a single person, can stand in a relation higher than universal ethics — a direct relation to God. This is a paradox. No reasoning can make it intelligible, and no one else can confirm it for you.
  • Real faith comes with fear and trembling. Turning Abraham into an uplifting tale is a counterfeit of faith. If the story doesn't keep you up at night, you haven't understood it — and a "believer" who feels no anxiety and no doubt has thereby shown he is no knight of faith.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Feel the dread first: four versions of Abraham

The book opens not with an argument but with a story. Johannes describes a man who loved the tale of Abraham as a boy, understood it less the older he got, and finally became obsessed by it. He replays the journey four times, each time imagining an Abraham whose faith fails (the section is called "Attunement" — Danish Stemning, also translated "Exordium" — tuning the reader's mood to the right pitch before anything else):

  • The first Abraham, on the mountain, deliberately acts like a monster, so that Isaac will lose faith in his father rather than in God — better the boy hate me, he thinks, than lose his trust in You.
  • The second does everything in silence, sacrifices the ram and takes his son home; but from that day his eyes are darkened and he never knows joy again. He obeyed, but he did not believe.
  • The third rides back to Moriah alone, throws himself on the ground and begs God to forgive his sin — the sin of having been willing to sacrifice Isaac. To his death he cannot tell whether his obedience that day was faith or crime.
  • The fourth raises the knife, and Isaac sees his father's left hand clenched in despair, a shudder running through his whole body — and Isaac loses his faith.

Each variation is paired with an image of weaning: a mother blackens her breast so the child will stop nursing; the child loses the milk, but the mother is still the same tender mother. The point of the four variations: the same outward acts — the three-day journey, the raised knife, the ram — can contain wholly different inner lives, and faith lives not in the act but in the heart. The four almost-Abrahams are understandable, human. The real one is incomprehensible.

Then Johannes skewers the Sunday sermon. The pastor glides through "Abraham was willing to offer God the best he had," and the congregation goes home comforted. Suppose one sleepless listener actually took it in and went home to do the same — the pastor would rush over and denounce him as a monster. So tell us, pastor: the same act is called faith in Abraham and murder in this man — where exactly is the difference? If you can't say, you are comforting people with a story you don't understand.

How it changes your view: we read stories knowing the ending. Abraham's tale gets told warmly because everyone knows the angel will intervene. Kierkegaard insists you strip the ending away and walk those three days — when no one knew there would be a ram, not even Abraham. Every real decision is like that: at the moment you choose, you don't hold the ending.

2. The knight of resignation vs. the knight of faith: the "double movement"

This is the book's most elegant pair of ideas and its most overlooked. Johannes tells a parable: a poor young man falls in love with a princess. The love is the whole content of his life, and the marriage is, in reality, utterly impossible.

The knight of infinite resignation does not do what the philistine does ("oh well, plenty more fish"), nor does he fool himself that there's still hope. He gathers the whole love into himself, gives up the princess completely as a worldly prospect, and turns the love itself into something eternal and spiritual — "I love her in eternity." The pain remains, but he has made peace with it and found a kind of serenity. It's a great movement that takes enormous strength, and Johannes says he himself can make it. Stoicism (the ancient philosophy of letting go of what you can't control and guarding only your inner self), religious renunciation, monastic withdrawal — all live at this level.

The knight of faith makes exactly the same renunciation — complete, nothing held back — and then a second movement: "by virtue of the absurd," he believes he will have the princess in this life, in this world. Not in heaven, not in his heart: he will actually marry her. That is the "double movement": first give everything up, then receive everything back.

Back to Abraham. A mere knight of resignation would raise the knife, sacrifice Isaac, and cherish his son forever in memory, with a tragic calm — that's the second variation. The real Abraham, even as he raises the knife, believes God will give Isaac back to him — in this life, not the next. That is why he can come home and be a joyful father again. Johannes's crucial confession (paraphrased): resignation I understand and can do; but the second movement — after giving up everything, to take back the finite world with joy — I cannot do. I can only marvel.

"The absurd" here doesn't mean logical contradiction or nonsense. It means: by every human calculation, this cannot happen — and he believes it will, while fully knowing it cannot. The resigned man sees the impossibility and lets go; the man of faith sees it too, and keeps believing anyway. As the book's eulogy to Abraham puts it (paraphrased): one became great by expecting the possible, another by expecting the eternal, but the one who expected the impossible became the greatest of all.

The most surprising touch is what a knight of faith looks like. If you met one, Johannes says, you'd be disappointed: he looks just like a tax collector. He takes walks, watches workmen, strolls home wondering whether his wife has made a roast lamb's head with vegetables for supper, and enjoys it thoroughly; he takes an interest in everything, indistinguishable from any solid citizen. The difference is entirely inward: at every moment he is making the double movement — surrendering the finite world and receiving it back by virtue of the absurd — which is exactly why he can enjoy the ordinary so freely. The knight of resignation, by contrast, is easy to spot: a proud gait, an eternal melancholy, the air of a stranger in the world.

How it changes your view: many people treat "letting go" as the summit of the spiritual life. Kierkegaard says it's only the penultimate step — "infinite resignation is the last stage before faith" (paraphrased): whoever hasn't let go can't have faith, but whoever stops at letting go has lost the world itself. The harder thing is, after letting go, to dare to love again, to want again, to enjoy supper — rather than live on in a been-there, seen-through-it detachment.

3. The teleological suspension of the ethical: the universal and the tragic hero

The body of the book is three "Problemata." The first asks: is there a teleological suspension of the ethical? Unpack the intimidating phrase: "teleological" (from the Greek telos, end or purpose) means "for the sake of some higher end"; "suspension" means not abolition but a temporary halt to its authority. Together: can ethics be set aside, for the moment, for the sake of a purpose higher than ethics?

"Ethics" here has a specific, Hegelian meaning: the ethical is "the universal" — norms that apply to everyone at every time, that can be spoken aloud, publicly debated and understood by others. Hegel called the concrete version Sittlichkeit, "ethical life": the norms embodied in family, civil society and state. On this picture, the individual's highest task is to dissolve his particularity into the universal; to insist on your own particularity, to refuse to be explained by the universal, is evil.

Johannes accepts the framework, then points out: if the ethical is the highest, Abraham is lost. To show how strange Abraham's position is, he sets beside him the "tragic hero," with three examples:

  • Agamemnon: commander of the Greek fleet, becalmed at Aulis; the seer declares he must sacrifice his daughter Iphigenia to appease the goddess.
  • Jephthah: the Israelite judge in the Book of Judges who vowed before battle to sacrifice whatever first came out of his house to greet him on his victorious return — and out came his only daughter.
  • Lucius Junius Brutus: founder of the Roman Republic who, as consul, condemned his own two sons to death for conspiring to restore the monarchy.

All three killed their children and are called tragic heroes, not murderers. Why? Because their acts stay inside the ethical: a higher ethical duty (to the state, the people, a sacred vow) overrides a lower one (to family). Agamemnon can explain himself to all Greece; the whole army understands and weeps with him. His anguish can be spoken, so the universal catches him. The tragic hero gives up something certain for something even more certain — the understanding of the universal.

Abraham has none of this. He isn't saving a nation, calming the gods, or keeping a vow — no higher ethical purpose can justify him. He does it purely "for God's sake and — what is exactly the same — for his own sake": for God, because God demands this proof; for himself, so that he can give it. In the language of ethics those reasons simply don't register. Hence: either there is a relation higher than ethics, or Abraham is an old man who meant to kill his son.

Table: outwardly the tragic hero and the knight of faith do the same thing; inwardly their structures are opposite.

Why it matters: this section challenges a default assumption of modern ethics — that whatever is right must be publicly justifiable to any rational person. Kant's universalizability test (a maxim is moral only if you could will it to be a law for everyone) and Hegel's ethical life both run on that idea. Kierkegaard's challenge: if that's the whole story, then nothing in a human life goes deeper than "socially intelligible," and faith is a mere ornament on ethics.

4. The single individual and the absolute duty to God

The second Problema: is there an absolute duty to God? In Hegelian ethics "duty to God" is a manner of speaking: love your neighbor, do your job, and you've done your duty to God. God gets thinned out into "the sum of duties," and the individual only ever deals with the universal, never with God alone.

Kierkegaard flips this. The paradox of faith is that "the single individual" (den Enkelte), as a single individual, is higher than the universal and stands in an absolute relation to the absolute. "The single individual" is one of the central ideas of his whole life: not me as citizen, son, employee or church member, but the "I" left over when every social role is stripped off — the one who must answer for his own existence, alone. He later said that if he were to have an inscription on his gravestone, it should be simply "That Single Individual."

He sharpens the point with Luke 14:26: "If any man come to me, and hate not his father, and mother, and wife, and children, and brethren, and sisters, yea, and his own life also, he cannot be my disciple." The usual commentary softens "hate" into "love less." Johannes refuses to soften it: the verse really is that terrible. But then comes the key turn — Abraham doesn't hate Isaac; he must love Isaac with his whole heart, or offering him would be no sacrifice at all. If he hated the boy, there'd be no trial. So faith doesn't replace love for people with love for God; it surrenders that love precisely at its fullest.

The chilling consequence: no one can tell you whether you really stand in that absolute relation. The tragic hero can consult others, be talked out of it, be consoled. The knight of faith carries it alone. He can never know whether he's being tested or deceived; he is utterly solitary, and he cannot make himself understood by anyone. That uncertainty and solitude are where the title comes from — Philippians 2:12: "work out your own salvation with fear and trembling."

How it changes your view: modern people habitually outsource big decisions to the universal — experts, norms, the majority — and usually that's sensible. But Kierkegaard reminds you that on the most fundamental questions (who am I, what do I live for), no group can decide for you, and no after-the-fact justification relieves you of answering for it alone. "The crowd is untruth," he wrote elsewhere: hiding in the crowd is a way of hiding from becoming a self.

5. Silence: speaking would be betrayal

The third Problema: was Abraham ethically defensible in concealing his purpose from Sarah his wife, Eliezer his steward, and Isaac?

Ethics demands disclosure. The ethical is the universal, and the universal is what can be said; concealment withdraws you from the universal and is therefore, ethically, guilt. Here again the tragic hero and Abraham part ways: Agamemnon can speak, must speak, and his greatness lies in speaking.

Abraham cannot — not because he won't, but because it's impossible. Whatever he says gets translated into the language of the universal, and in that language what he is about to do has only one name: killing his son. Any explanation would make him a madman or a murderer. So he is silent — and the silence isn't a secret; it's the nature of his relation to God. Faith cannot be communicated or "mediated" (Hegel's term for reconciling two opposites through a higher third); it can only be lived.

The book's subtlest moment is here. On the way up, Isaac asks: "Behold the fire and the wood: but where is the lamb for a burnt offering?" Abraham answers: "My son, God will provide himself a lamb for a burnt offering." Johannes reads the line closely. It isn't a lie — God might provide a lamb, and Abraham truly believes He will. Yet it says nothing — Isaac can't grasp what it means. It's speech in the mode of irony: he speaks, and says nothing. It is the only way a knight of faith can talk.

For contrast, Johannes offers several cases of "aesthetic concealment." The most haunting is "Agnes and the Merman": in the Danish legend a merman rises from the sea to lure the girl Agnes away; in Johannes's retelling her complete trust undoes him — he can't go through with it, yet he can't confess to her what he is. Such silences come from guilt (not daring to speak) or the demonic (refusing to) — silences ethics or aesthetics can still make sense of. Abraham's alone is silent because the relation he stands in exceeds language itself.

How it changes your view: we tend to assume that whatever can't be put into words doesn't count, and a choice you can't explain to others is mere willfulness. Kierkegaard proposes another possibility: the deepest inner experiences may be incommunicable in principle — not because they're vague, but because they're so particular they belong to one person at one moment. It also explains why he wrote all his life under pseudonyms, practicing "indirect communication": what really matters can't be handed to you directly; it can only push you to touch it yourself.

The Skeleton

The whole book in one line:

① Starting point: the age believes it has gone beyond faith (the preface: in the world of ideas everything is on clearance sale), and everyone praises Abraham without seriously thinking about what he did.

② Feel it before you think it: four failed Abrahams and an ironic sermon force the reader to admit the story is terrifying — and that removing the terror removes the faith.

③ Define faith: distinguish resignation from faith. Faith is the double movement — give up the finite completely, then receive it back by virtue of the absurd. No philosophy can make that movement for you.

④ Three tests: if the ethical (the universal) is highest, then it can't be suspended (Problema I), there is no absolute duty to God (II), and concealment is guilt (III) — and on all three counts Abraham stands condemned.

⑤ Conclusion, as an either/or: either accept the paradox that the single individual is higher than the universal — that is, faith — or admit that Abraham is lost. Hegelian philosophy will admit neither, so it ends up praising Abraham while turning him into someone it cannot explain.

⑥ Close: faith is the highest passion in a human being; every generation must begin it afresh, and no one goes further.

What does it actually establish? Strictly, it doesn't prove that faith is true or that Abraham was right. It establishes a dilemma: either faith is something above ethics that reason cannot mediate, or Abraham does not deserve our praise. It works not by proving God exists but by putting the reader on that three-day road, to feel where the authority of universal ethics, as life's final court, runs out. Johannes himself stays outside the door, admitting he has no faith and can only marvel — and that is the book's method: a man without faith describing, as rigorously as he can, how hard faith is, so that neither easy faith nor easy "going beyond" faith has anywhere to hide.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Common misreadings

  • "Kierkegaard says that if you feel God commands it, you may break morality." The reverse. The book insists no one can prove he is a knight of faith, and anyone who feels certain and at ease about it is not one. The marks of the knight of faith are precisely fear and trembling — and the most intense love for Isaac. Suspending the ethical is not a license; it is a terrifying position that must be borne alone and can never be confirmed.
  • "This is Kierkegaard's own manifesto." The author is "Johannes de silentio," a man who says he lacks faith and can only look on from outside. Kierkegaard explicitly asked that the views in his pseudonymous works not be attributed directly to him — part of his strategy of indirect communication. The book's verdicts belong to a particular vantage point, and readers should keep a certain distance from it.
  • "The 'leap of faith' comes from this book." The exact phrase "leap of faith" doesn't appear in Kierkegaard's writings; "leap" (Spring) shows up mainly in The Concept of Anxiety and the Postscript. This book talks about "movements" and "the absurd." Summarizing it as a leap isn't badly wrong, but it invites picturing faith as an eyes-shut impulse — when the book stresses that faith must first pass through the most clear-eyed infinite resignation.

The strongest criticisms

  • Kant had already answered it (The Conflict of the Faculties, 1798). In a footnote Kant says Abraham should have replied to the voice roughly as follows: "That I ought not to kill my good son is quite certain. But that you, this apparition, are God — of that I am not certain, and never can be." Any "revelation" demanding a breach of the moral law can, for that very reason, be judged not to come from God. The objection cuts deep: if the knight of faith himself can't verify the voice, why should something uncertain override something certain? Kierkegaard's defenders reply that he concedes exactly this uncertainty — that's why he calls it a paradox. Critics answer that "paradox" may then be just a noble name for a dangerous act.
  • Levinas: the climax isn't the raised knife but the second voice. Emmanuel Levinas, the French philosopher whose ethics centers on the face of the other person summoning me to responsibility, argued that Kierkegaard pictured ethics as cold "universal rules," and so felt the need to suspend it; but the true source of ethics is responsibility in the face of another human being. For Levinas the story's highest moment is when Abraham hears the voice that stops his hand and returns him to the ethical — religion lies not in transcending ethics but in ethics itself being holy.
  • Derrida: every act of responsibility is a sacrifice of Isaac. In The Gift of Death (1992), Jacques Derrida, the philosopher of deconstruction, universalizes the book: whenever I care for one person or answer one call, I sacrifice countless others with equal claims on me — feeding my own cat means not feeding all the other hungry cats in the world. The clash between absolute responsibility and universal ethics isn't Abraham's special case; it's everyone's daily condition. That both extends the book and dissolves its claim that only the knight of faith is in this position.
  • The shadow of religious violence. After 2001, "suspending ethics in God's name" reads very harshly. Critics argue that however many "fear and trembling" qualifications the book adds, its structure does offer a philosophical template for private revelation trumping public morality. Defenders reply that the knight of faith coerces no one, sacrifices only what he himself loves most, and is perpetually anxious about being deceived — the very opposite of the fanatic's certainty. The trouble is that from the outside the two cannot be told apart — as the book itself admits.
  • Unfair to Hegel; nearly blind to Isaac and Sarah. Many scholars think the book's "Hegel" is a caricature; Hegel's ethics is not simply "follow the crowd." And the book stands almost wholly in Abraham's shoes: Isaac's terror (glimpsed only in the fourth variation) and Sarah's position get almost no serious attention — whereas the Jewish tradition of interpreting this chapter (the Akedah, "the binding of Isaac") gives both of them far richer voices.

Kierkegaard Beyond This Book

Read Fear and Trembling alone and you might take Kierkegaard for a religious thinker whose message is "faith over morality." In fact, across a whole cast of pseudonyms, he spent his life drawing one map from many angles: how a person actually becomes a self. This book is one corner of that map.

  • The three stages of existence (Either/Or, 1843; Stages on Life's Way, 1845): there are, roughly, three ways to live. The aesthetic: living for pleasure, novelty and the interesting, epitomized by the seducer in volume one of Either/Or — a life that ends in boredom and despair, forever fleeing repetition. The ethical: making commitments and taking responsibility, epitomized by Judge William, who champions marriage in volume two — "choosing yourself" gives life continuity. The religious: standing before God as a single individual. Fear and Trembling is about the rift between the ethical and the religious — and there's no smooth path between stages; each is crossed only by a personal decision.
  • Anxiety as "the dizziness of freedom" (The Concept of Anxiety, 1844): anxiety (Angst) differs from fear of a definite object. It's the vertigo of facing "I could" — at a cliff edge you fear not only falling but the possibility that you might jump. Anxiety is the by-product of freedom: only a being that can choose feels it. The insight passed directly into Heidegger and Sartre.
  • Despair as "the sickness unto death" (The Sickness unto Death, 1849): he defines the self as "a relation that relates itself to itself" — not a finished thing but an ongoing balancing act between the finite and the infinite, necessity and possibility. Despair is that relation out of balance: either despairingly not willing to be oneself (escaping into the crowd, into roles, into distraction) or despairingly willing to be oneself (forcing a self into being by sheer will). Most people, he thought, are in despair without knowing it — the most common kind of all.
  • "Truth is subjectivity" (Concluding Unscientific Postscript, 1846): on questions of existence, what matters is not only what you believe but how you stand in relation to it. His famous formulation, paraphrased: truth is an objective uncertainty held fast in the most passionate inwardness. A pagan praying with his whole heart before an idol may be more "in the truth" than a Christian praying absent-mindedly in the true God's house. This isn't relativism; it's the claim that life's most important truths can't be possessed as a spectator.
  • The attack on "Christendom" (1854–55): at the end of his life he publicly assailed the Danish state church. When an entire nation is "Christian" by birth, Christianity has been abolished — if everyone is a Christian, no one is. It's the same target as the comfortable sermon mocked in Fear and Trembling: his lifelong enemy was turning the hardest thing into the most convenient one.

The Book in Ten Sentences

① In the marketplace of ideas everything is on sale, and everyone wants to "go further." But faith is the highest passion in a human being; each generation starts it from scratch, and no one goes further.

② Take away Abraham's dread and all that's left is a story. If you can sleep soundly after reading it, you haven't understood it.

③ The same raised knife can hold faith or despair. Faith lives not in the shape of the act but in the heart.

④ Infinite resignation is the last stage before faith (paraphrased). Letting go is only the next-to-last step; daring to take the whole world back afterwards — that is faith.

⑤ By virtue of the absurd, he believed he would have Isaac back in this life. Faith isn't failing to see the impossible; it's seeing it clearly and believing anyway.

⑥ The one who expected the impossible became the greatest of all (paraphrased). Yet the knight of faith looks like a tax collector — living joyfully in the ordinary.

⑦ The tragic hero stays within the ethical, and the whole world weeps for him. Abraham has no higher ethic to lean on: either faith exists, or he is a murderer.

⑧ The single individual, as a single individual, is higher than the universal. It's a paradox — it can't be mediated, and no one can confirm it for you.

⑨ "My son, God will provide himself a lamb." He spoke and said nothing: faith can be lived, not communicated.

⑩ I can understand Abraham's greatness, but I cannot do what he did (paraphrased). Taking faith seriously starts with admitting how hard it is.
