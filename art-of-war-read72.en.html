The Art of War

Sunzi bingfa · Sun Tzu · c. 5th century BCE · thirteen chapters, roughly six thousand characters

中文 →

In One Sentence

This is a manual on how to fight that spends most of its length arguing for fighting less, fighting faster, and ideally not fighting at all — because for Sun Tzu war is not a stage for heroes but a bill that can bankrupt a state, and the great commander is not the one who saves the day on the battlefield but the one who has already finished the arithmetic before the first move, and only moves when the thing is already won.

Coordinates

Sun Tzu (Sun Wu) lived at the end of the Spring and Autumn period; according to Sima Qian's Records of the Grand Historian he presented his thirteen chapters to King Helü of Wu around the close of the sixth century BCE and afterward served him. The whole text runs to about six thousand characters, yet it is the earliest systematic treatise on war anywhere — some twenty-three centuries older than Clausewitz's On War. Cao Cao, the warlord-poet of the Three Kingdoms era, wrote its most important commentary and stands first among the eleven classical annotators. It survives not because it teaches fighting, but because it is the earliest and most compact grammar we have for adversarial decisions made under scarcity.

The Central Claims

  • War is first an arithmetic problem, not an honour test. The opening line is that war is the great affair of the state, the ground of life and death, the road to survival or ruin, and must be studied. The first question is never "how do we win" but "is this worth doing, and can we afford how long it will take."
  • The highest victory is the one you never have to fight for. "To win a hundred battles out of a hundred is not the finest skill; to subdue the enemy without fighting is." On his scorecard, an unbroken run of victories is merely second-rate.
  • The outcome is settled before the fighting starts, by form and momentum. "The victorious army wins first and then seeks battle; the defeated army fights first and then looks for a way to win." Brilliance under fire is usually a symptom of inadequate preparation, not proof of skill.

The Core Concepts, One by One

1. War as an expense: why speed beats cleverness

Almost every book on war opens with how to win. Chapter two of this one opens with a budget. Sun Tzu counts it out: a thousand fast chariots, a thousand baggage wagons, a hundred thousand armoured troops, grain hauled a thousand li, plus diplomatic outlays and the constant attrition of gear and draft animals — "a thousand pieces of gold a day, and only then can a hundred-thousand-man army be put in the field." It is the passage most readers skim and the one that defines the book's temperament: war is written first as a cost sheet, and only then as an art.

From this comes his least romantic maxim: one hears of clumsy haste in war, but has never seen a skilful protracted campaign; no state has ever profited from a long war. The mechanism is concrete. Drag it out and weapons dull, morale is ground away, treasure is exhausted — and once you are hollow at home, a third power moves in, at which point "no one, however wise, can put things right." Hence his instruction to feed off the enemy, with an exchange rate attached: one measure of the enemy's grain is worth twenty of your own. That is not rhetoric but the real loss curve of pre-modern overland supply — the men and animals hauling the grain eat it as they go, and the longer the road, the smaller the fraction that arrives.

The modern name for what he is afraid of is a war of attrition: a contest neither side can end quickly, decided by who runs out first. Its horror is that in an attritional fight the winner's bill and the loser's bill look much alike; the loser merely goes bankrupt sooner — the three-year price war, the five-year lawsuit where the judgment says you won and the ledger says you are finished. The book circles back to this at the end: a ruler must not raise an army out of anger, nor a general give battle out of pique; move when it serves your interest, stop when it does not. And then the coldest line in it: a ruined state cannot be restored, and the dead cannot be brought back to life. The shift it forces on you: demote "can we win" to the second question, and promote "at what cost, for how long" to the first.

2. The five factors and the seven comparisons: the outcome is computable in advance

Sun Tzu thinks pre-war forecasting rests neither on omens nor on nerve, but on a scoring sheet. First the five factors:

  • The Way — that the people and the ruler want the same thing, so troops will live and die with him without fear. Note that the most notoriously cold-blooded book in the canon puts political cohesion ahead of weather, terrain and generalship.
  • Heaven — night and day, cold and heat, the turn of the seasons: in short, timing.
  • Earth — distance, difficulty of ground, open or narrow, terrain you can withdraw from versus terrain you cannot.
  • The Commander — wisdom, credibility, humanity, courage, discipline. Watch the order: wisdom comes first and courage only fourth. Bravery here is a necessary low-level requirement, never a selection criterion.
  • Method — unit organisation, chains of command and authority, control of supply. In today's vocabulary: org chart, reporting lines, logistics.

Then the seven comparisons, which turn those factors into questions you must answer side by side for both armies: which ruler has the Way; which commander is more able; which side has heaven and earth; whose orders are actually carried out; whose troops are stronger; whose soldiers are better drilled; whose rewards and punishments are clearer. The conclusion: the side whose pre-war calculations in the ancestral temple come out ahead has counted more; more counting beats less counting, and how much worse is no counting at all.

Translated into modern terms, this is due diligence, invented before the common era. Its value is less in any single item than in the way it forces a vague feeling of confidence into seven specific questions. You will find you cannot answer three or four of them — and that, by itself, is the answer. Which is how it changes the way you see: when an outcome feels unknowable, that is usually not because the world is opaque but because you have not yet counted the things that can be counted.

3. Whole victory: redefining what winning means

Chapter three is the book's value system. To take a state whole is best, to shatter it is second best; to take an army whole is best, to break it second best. Whole means intact and functioning; a thing you smashed to get is no longer worth what you paid for it. That is economics, not mercy. From it follows the famous line — a hundred victories in a hundred battles is not the finest skill; subduing the enemy without fighting is — and a ladder of four options:

"Attack his plans; next, his alliances; next, his army; and worst of all, his cities." One objective, four methods whose costs differ by an order of magnitude.
RankWhat it meansCost
Attack his plans (best)Make him work out for himself, while still deliberating, that he cannot win or cannot afford to; the campaign dies in his own councilnear zero
Attack his alliancesDetach or buy off his partners until he stands alone and has to stand downdiplomacy, concessions
Attack his armyFight him in the fieldlives and treasure
Attack his cities (worst)Assault fortifications — only when nothing else is leftsee below

Why is siege nailed to the bottom? Because he costs it out. Three months to build the mantlets and armoured wagons and assemble the gear; three more to raise earthworks against the walls; and then the commander, unable to bear the delay, loses patience and sends his men swarming up the wall like ants — "a third of them killed and the city still not taken." Siege is the worst option not because it lacks courage but because it has the worst return on investment, and because of how it fails: what collapses first is the general's patience, not the enemy's wall.

The cleanest modern instance is deterrence: the most successful nuclear deterrent is the one never used, since its entire effect takes place on the other side's spreadsheet. Commercially the same — far better that a rival works out before entering that the ground is not worth taking than that you bleed each other white once he is in. The shift it forces: when you notice you are assaulting a wall, step back and ask whether you have confused the means (beating him) with the end (getting the thing you wanted).

4. Win first, then fight: victory is prepared, not improvised

Chapter four is about form — realised, visible strength and disposition. Its opening is the hardest sentence in the book: the skilled warriors of old first made themselves unbeatable, and then waited for the enemy to become beatable; being unbeatable lies with you, being beatable lies with him. Sun Tzu then concedes something remarkable: a good commander can make himself impossible to defeat, but cannot make the enemy certain to be defeated. Strategy can guarantee that you do not lose. It cannot guarantee that you win. What you can do is turn yourself into a hard target and wait — and if the opening never comes, go on waiting.

Hence the line that belongs above every desk: the victorious army wins first and then seeks battle; the defeated army fights first and then looks for a way to win. And its anti-heroic companion: the victories of the truly skilled bring "no reputation for wisdom and no credit for valour" — the real masters have no legendary battles, because they win too plainly for the story to be worth telling. The analogy holds everywhere today: the best infrastructure engineer has no thrilling firefighting stories; you cannot recall your best doctor performing any dramatic surgery. Wherever heroics are required, form was insufficient.

So what is form? He gives a picture: the way a victorious army fights is like releasing pent-up water down a thousand-foot ravine. Form is stored potential — troops, grain, ground, cohesion, time advantage, all accumulated beforehand, so that opening the battle is merely opening the sluice gate. The shift it forces: when you find yourself repeatedly rescued by overtime, by one person's heroics, by improvisation under pressure, do not praise the rescuer. You are being told your form is thin.

5. Momentum and the orthodox/extraordinary: build the slope instead of blaming the man

If form is stored potential, shi — momentum — is its release. Chapter five makes two moves.

The first is the orthodox and the extraordinary (zheng and qi): in battle you engage with the orthodox and win with the extraordinary. The orthodox is the force committed frontally, in the expected way; the extraordinary is what arrives from where he was not looking. The crucial gloss follows immediately — the two "give birth to each other, endlessly, like a ring without a beginning." They are not two standing units but two roles defined entirely by the enemy's expectations: whatever he has anticipated is orthodox, whatever he has not is extraordinary. The same battalion can switch roles within an afternoon. This is why readers get stuck here: they go looking for a special-forces unit when what they should be looking at is the table of expectations inside the opponent's head.

The second is momentum and timing: rushing water that tumbles boulders is momentum; the falcon's strike that breaks the prey's back is timing. The torrent moves stones because of accumulated fall; the bird kills because of the distance and instant it chooses to unfold. He supplies a mechanical version too — momentum is the drawn crossbow, timing is the release of the trigger. Store one, judge the other; either alone is wasted.

Then comes the most useful sentence in the book for anyone who manages people: the skilled commander looks for victory in momentum and does not demand it from individuals; he selects his people and lets the momentum do the work. He turns it into an image: employing men in battle is like rolling logs and stones — by nature they lie still on level ground and move on a slope, stop if they are square and roll if they are round; the momentum of a good commander is like round stones rolling down a mountain a thousand feet high. Logs and stones have no fighting spirit. Put the round one at the top and the rest happens by itself.

This is the same insight as modern situationism in social psychology — behaviour is governed far more by circumstance than by character — arrived at two and a half millennia early. And it cashes out very concretely: before you open your mouth to call someone unmotivated, ask what gradient you have placed them on. Broken incentives, blocked information, responsibility sliced into fragments, patched over with exhortations to take ownership — that, in Sun Tzu's vocabulary, is demanding victory from individuals.

6. Full and empty: initiative is the only scarce resource

Chapter six, ranked by the classical commentators as the finest in the book, answers one question: whose tempo is everyone moving to? "Whoever occupies the battlefield first and awaits the enemy is at ease; whoever arrives late and rushes into battle is worn out. So the skilled commander brings the enemy to him and is not brought to the enemy." The difference between those two states often decides more than the difference in numbers does.

How is it done? By avoiding the full and striking the empty. Full and empty here do not mean strong units and weak units; they mean places that are defended at this moment and places that are not — hence "you attack and certainly take because you attack what he does not defend; you defend and certainly hold because you hold what he does not attack." Then the underrated operational core: make him show his form while you show none, and you are concentrated while he is divided; "I am one, he is ten, so I attack his one with my ten." This is pure economics of force: total strength unchanged, and yet, purely through information asymmetry, a local ten-to-one is conjured at the point of contact.

Finally the most quoted and most misread image in the book: military form is like water; water avoids heights and runs downward, an army avoids the full and strikes the empty; water shapes its course to the ground, an army shapes its victory to the enemy; so an army has no constant momentum, as water has no constant shape. Readers turn this into "be soft like water." It is not that. The point of water is not that it is soft but that it has no shape of its own: its form is dictated entirely by the vessel and the ground. Do not bring a preferred formation to the field — let the enemy's shape determine yours. Whoever fixes his form first has handed over his weak points first.

The shift it forces: in any contest the scarce resource is never material, it is initiative. Look at who is setting the agenda and who is answering. The answering party pays the cost of arriving worn out even in the rounds it wins.

7. Deception, spies, and foreknowledge: intelligence as the first armament

"War is the way of deception. So when able, appear unable; when active, appear inactive; when near, appear far; when far, appear near… strike where he is unprepared, appear where he does not expect you." Read as a villain's creed, this misses how technical it is. Deception is not for amusement; its purpose is to make the enemy's own calculations run on a false map, so that all his preparation is spent in the wrong place — you are manufacturing his emptiness. Convince him to fortify the east and the east becomes full, the west becomes empty, and the emptiness is your own handiwork.

The other half is the input side. The final chapter sorts spies into five kinds — local agents recruited from the enemy's countryside, inside agents bought among his officials, double agents turned back from his own service, expendable agents fed false information who will not survive its exposure, and surviving agents who come back with what they saw. Then a startling line for a rigidly hierarchical age: of the whole army, none is closer to the commander than his spies, none more richly rewarded, no business more secret.

The deepest sentence is this one: what enables an enlightened ruler and a wise general to strike and succeed beyond ordinary men is foreknowledge — and foreknowledge cannot be had from spirits, nor by analogy with past events, nor by calculation from the heavens; it must be had from people, from those who know the enemy's situation. No divination, no reasoning from historical parallels, no astrological reckoning — only living human beings who have looked. In an era when no campaign began without consulting the oracle, that amounts to evicting mysticism from military decision-making.

He also makes stinginess about intelligence a moral failing: to face each other for years for the sake of a single decisive day, and then begrudge a hundred pieces of gold for knowledge of the enemy, is "the height of inhumanity." The shift it forces: what you take for a shortfall of judgement is usually a shortfall of information — and information can nearly always be bought, for orders of magnitude less than a fight.

8. The commander's autonomy and the five dangers: organisational risk, written into a war manual

Sun Tzu treats your own side as a major cause of defeat. Chapter three lists three ways a sovereign brings ruin on his army: ordering an advance when the army cannot advance (hobbling it, as with a tether); administering the army as he administers a state, without understanding its affairs (confusion); interfering in appointments and command without understanding flexibility (mistrust). Hence "he whose general is able and not interfered with by the sovereign wins," and the famous "there are commands of the sovereign that are not to be obeyed" — which in context sits in a list: there are roads not to be taken, armies not to be attacked, cities not to be assaulted, ground not to be contested, and orders not to be obeyed. Anything whose execution harms the whole may be declined, the ruler's instruction included. It is an early statement of the principal–agent problem: the distant authoriser's information always lags the field.

The other half dissects the commander himself — the five dangers:

  • Recklessly brave — set a trap and kill him; his behaviour is entirely predictable.
  • Too attached to life — press him and he is captured.
  • Quick to anger — insult him and he will trade his judgement for the satisfaction of a reply.
  • Too jealous of his honour — smear him and he will move rashly to clear his name.
  • Too fond of his people — harass what he is protecting and he must come to the rescue, and can be run ragged.

What makes the list sting is that three of the five are virtues: integrity, care for civilians, refusal to retreat. Sun Tzu's verdict is that these five are the commander's faults and calamities in war — meaning that any sufficiently stable, sufficiently predictable trait of character is an interface the enemy can call. The more famously you care about something, the better everyone knows where to press.

Its mirror image is chapter ten's positive standard: the commander who advances without seeking fame and withdraws without fearing blame, whose only concern is to protect his men in a way that serves his sovereign, is "the treasure of the state." That is one of the earliest formulations of accountability to reality outranking accountability upward.

The Argument in Brief

The thirteen chapters are not thirteen topics but one loop that runs from the council chamber to the field and back: should we fight (1–2) → how do we fight less (3) → make ourselves unloseable first (4) → manufacture advantage structurally rather than personally (5) → seize the initiative once engaged (6–7) → and the organisation and intelligence that hold all of it up (8–13).

The eight concepts above cover chapters one through six and thirteen. Three things in the remainder deserve their own line:

  • Manoeuvre: "take the indirect road to arrive first" — the fastest route is rarely the straight one; "swift as the wind, quiet as the forest, ravaging as fire, immovable as a mountain" (the four lines the Japanese warlord Takeda Shingen put on his battle standard); and "leave an opening for a surrounded army, do not press a desperate enemy" — always leave a besieged force a way out, because a man with none will fight to the death and you will pay for it.
  • March, Terrain, The Nine Grounds: the least mystical part of the book, a field handbook — how to camp, how to use six kinds of terrain and nine kinds of ground. The much-abused line "throw them into a place with no way out and they will survive; put them on death ground and they will live" comes from here. It is an extreme measure in a taxonomy of ground, not a motto for life.
  • The Nine Variations and Attack by Fire: "do not count on his not coming; count on being ready for him." And having explained the most savage instrument available, the book turns and closes on the ruler who must not raise an army out of anger — landing on what it calls the way to keep the state secure and the army whole.

It opens with "this must be studied" and ends with "not out of anger": the whole book is bracketed by two forms of restraint.

Misreadings, Criticisms, Live Disputes

  • "Know your enemy and know yourself, and you will win a hundred battles" is a misquotation. The text says you will not be in danger in a hundred battles. The gap between those two is an entire worldview: Sun Tzu never promises victory, only that you will not walk into a fight you cannot read. The following clauses matter as much — know yourself but not the enemy and you will win one and lose one; know neither and you will be in danger every time.
  • The Thirty-Six Stratagems is not by Sun Tzu. That catalogue of ruses is a far later compilation, generally dated to the Ming–Qing period, and has no authorial link to the thirteen chapters. Treating this book as a bag of tricks is the commonest way of diminishing it: it contains almost no specific stratagems at all, only criteria for judgement.
  • "War is the way of deception" is not a licence for general ruthlessness. Deception is information management aimed at an enemy — a matter of means. On ends he is strict: the Way, meaning shared purpose between ruler and people, heads the five factors; prisoners are to be well treated and absorbed; no campaign may be launched that does not serve the state's interest. Nor is he a pacifist — he simply refuses to move without advantage, and moves decisively when there is one. Reading caution as reluctance is its own error.
  • The strongest opposing case: Clausewitz. On War collides with Sun Tzu's central claim head-on, arguing in substance that kind-hearted people may imagine some ingenious way to disarm an enemy without much bloodshed, and that this is a dangerous error. Clausewitz insists on war's built-in tendency to escalate toward the extreme, on friction (the countless small resistances — weather, exhaustion, a garbled order — that pull every plan out of shape) and on the fog of war (information at the moment of decision is always incomplete and partly wrong). Sun Tzu's world is broadly calculable and designable; Clausewitz's is ruled by chance, will and passion. In fairness, Sun Tzu is not blind to uncertainty — "no constant momentum," "shape your victory to the enemy" — but he does believe a first-rate commander can compress it a long way.
The two great treatises collide on one question: can war be designed in advance?

  • The price of aphorism: it is close to unfalsifiable. Six thousand characters of compressed principle, and both sides of most questions are in there: surround him if you outnumber him ten to one, but avoid him if you are weaker; speed is everything, yet chapter eleven contemplates long penetration. So a win can be credited to striking the empty and a loss blamed on failing to know the enemy. A theory that explains any outcome cannot direct a specific decision. The right way to read it is as a checklist of questions — have I run the seven comparisons? is my form established? who is moving whom? — not as a book of answers.
  • "Business is war" is a bad analogy. War is zero-sum and often negative-sum; the objective is to strip the other side of its capacity to continue. Most commercial competition is positive-sum: customers are not cities to be stormed, employees are not expendable troops. Bolt "deception" and "spies" onto that and you get poor ethics and worse strategy. What genuinely transfers is the skeleton — reluctance to fight, counting the cost, contesting the initiative, building advantage structurally, refusing to confuse means with ends.
  • Authorship and date remain unsettled. Sun Wu does not appear in the Zuo Zhuan, and Chinese scholars from the Song dynasty onward have suspected the thirteen chapters of being a Warring States composition; Sima Qian's story of the palace drill at Wu, in which Sun Tzu beheads two of the king's favourite concubines to establish discipline, is plainly legendary in flavour. The 1972 excavation of the Han tombs at Yinqueshan in Shandong, which produced bamboo-slip texts of both this book and Sun Bin's separate Art of War, settled that they are two different works and that the thirteen chapters were fixed by the Western Han — but not who wrote them, or when.
  • Reading it as mysterious oriental cunning is also a misreading. Its serious Western readers found the opposite. The British strategist B. H. Liddell Hart used it to corroborate his "indirect approach" — going around an opponent's strongest front and working on the weak points in his dispositions and his mind. Colonel John Boyd of the US Air Force, whose OODA loop (observe, orient, decide, act) argues for cycling faster than the adversary so that his judgements always rest on a stale picture, named Sun Tzu as a principal source. And the text itself is not mystical in the least: chapter two prices out wagons and fodder; chapter nine is an observational field guide, telling you that dust rising high and sharp means chariots, low and wide means infantry, and birds bursting into flight mean an ambush below. It reads more like an engineering manual than a book of maxims.

Ten Sentences

1. War is the great affair of the state, the ground of life and death, the road to survival or ruin — so the first question is never how to win, but whether it is worth doing and for how long you can afford it.

2. One hears of clumsy haste, never of skilful protraction: drag a fight out and weapons dull, treasure drains, and a third party moves into the hollow you have made — the judgment says you won and the ledger says you are finished.

3. "A hundred victories in a hundred battles is not the finest skill; subduing the enemy without fighting is" — on this scorecard, an unbroken winning streak is second-rate.

4. Attack his plans, then his alliances, then his army, and worst of all his cities: one objective, costs an order of magnitude apart. Siege is last not for want of courage but because what usually breaks first is the general's patience, not the wall.

5. Make yourself unbeatable first, then wait for him to become beatable: being unbeatable is yours to arrange, being beatable is his. Hence the victorious army wins first and then fights; the defeated army fights first and then hunts for a way to win.

6. The victories of the truly skilled bring no reputation for wisdom and no credit for valour — wherever heroics are needed, preparation was short.

7. Look for victory in momentum, do not demand it from individuals. Stones lie still on the flat and roll on a slope; before calling someone unmotivated, look at the gradient you put them on.

8. Bring the enemy to you rather than being brought to him, and strike the empty rather than the full: the scarce resource is initiative, and information asymmetry alone can produce ten-to-one at the point of contact without adding a single soldier.

9. An army is like water — it shapes its course to the ground as an army shapes its victory to the enemy. The point of water is not softness but that it has no shape of its own: bring no preferred formation, and let his shape decide yours.

10. Foreknowledge cannot be had from spirits, from historical analogy, or from the stars — only from people who know the enemy's situation. To begrudge a hundred pieces of gold for that while feeding a hundred thousand men into an attritional war is, in his words, the height of inhumanity.
