The Stranger

L'Étranger · Albert Camus · 1942

中文 →

In one sentence

A clerk named Meursault does not cry at his mother's funeral; the next day he goes swimming, sees a comedy, and sleeps with a woman. Weeks later, blinded by sun on a beach, he shoots a man dead. Then a court sentences him to die — and what it convicts him of is barely the shot; it is the not crying. In a novel of scarcely more than a hundred pages, written in sentences that seem to have been cut off at the root, Camus stages something genuinely unnerving: what a society cannot forgive is not killing but refusing to perform the feelings the script calls for. You may do wrong; what you may not do is decline to act in the play everyone else is acting in. And on his last night in the condemned cell, Meursault turns out to be the only happy man in the book.

Where it sits

Camus (1913–1960) was born poor in French Algeria. The Stranger appeared in 1942 in German-occupied Paris alongside the essay The Myth of Sisyphus and the play Caligula — what he called his cycle of the absurd: the essay argues the case, the novel performs it. (There is a separate piece on The Myth of Sisyphus on this site, Read 77a.) It is among the most widely read French novels of the twentieth century, and the book that carried the word "absurd" out of philosophy and into ordinary speech.

The core claims

  • Meursault's crime is not the killing; it is that he will not lie. He refuses to name feelings he has not actually felt and refuses to inflate judgements he is unsure of — and the daily running of a society rests precisely on those small, harmless, universally licensed lies. He will not pay that entry fee, so he is treated as a monster.
  • Meaning does not grow out of facts; it is fitted onto them afterwards by narration. The novel tells the same string of facts twice: first as they happen, then as they are tried. Nothing new is added the second time — yet once the material is strung into motive, character and cause, it becomes a premeditated murderer.
  • And when every consolation is stripped away — mother, love, the afterlife, the appeal — what is left is not despair but a strange calm: an opening to "the tender indifference of the world." Stop expecting the world to answer and it can no longer let you down.

The concepts, one at a time

"Maman died today": a sentence with the causation cut out

The novel opens with one of the most famous first lines in literature: "Maman died today. Or yesterday maybe, I don't know." (Aujourd'hui, maman est morte. Ou peut-être hier, je ne sais pas.) Notice first what it does not do: no grief, no adjectives, no wrenched heart — it does not even establish the date. It reports two things: a fact, and an uncertainty about the fact.

What matters more is the tense underneath it. Camus writes in the passé composé, the French compound past used for finished, isolated, recently completed acts — the tense of speech, not of literature. Formal French narration uses the passé simple, which carries a built-in sense of a story being told, invisible threads running between sentences and tying them into plot. In his celebrated 1943 essay on the novel, Sartre named the trick: writing an entire book in the compound past turns every sentence into an island. "I slept. I woke up. I had lunch." Between one sentence and the next there is only and then, never and therefore. Causation has been drained out; what remains is one thing after another.

Roland Barthes later gave the style a name in Writing Degree Zero (1953): white writing (l'écriture blanche) — "degree zero" meaning that the author has turned his own stance, emotion and rhetoric down to nothing. No judging, no shading, no nudging; a deposition rather than a narrative. Why does that matter? Because ordinary fiction quietly instructs you through its adjectives and its connectives ("he said cruelly," "out of jealousy, he…"), and Camus has removed the whole apparatus. The reader is left alone with bare fact — and discovers, with some discomfort, that he cannot stop supplying motive. That reflex is exactly what Part Two puts on trial.

One detail from the translation history makes the point. Stuart Gilbert's 1946 English version opens "Mother died today"; Matthew Ward, retranslating in 1988, insisted on keeping the French Maman. The difference is not small. Mother is formal, distant, almost legal; maman is what a child calls her. A single word moves Meursault from cold-blooded to merely unable to perform — which is the novel's thesis in miniature: conviction happens in the wording.

Meursault: not unfeeling, just unwilling to add a word

Most readers' first reaction is that something is wrong with the man. His mother dies and he does not weep; asked whether he wants the coffin opened for a last look, he says no; he keeps vigil with white coffee and cigarettes and dozes off. His employer offers him a posting in Paris; he says it makes no difference. Marie asks whether he loves her and he says he probably doesn't; asked whether he wants to marry her, he says it's all the same to him, and if she wants to, they can.

Line these up and a strict discipline shows through: he never says anything he has not actually felt. It isn't that he has no feelings — he says plainly that he loved his mother, that Marie's laugh made him want her, that when old Salamano lost the mangy dog he had cursed for eight years and began to cry, Meursault suddenly thought of his own mother. What he lacks is not feeling but the skill — or the willingness — to translate feeling into the socially expected phrasing. For him "love" either names something definite or names nothing; since he isn't sure, he says he probably doesn't. In a courtroom, that honest sentence is a hundred times more lethal than an insincere "I loved her."

Camus supplied the bluntest gloss himself, in a preface written for an American edition in 1955 (paraphrasing): in our society, any man who does not weep at his mother's funeral runs the risk of being sentenced to death. Meursault's one distinguishing trait, he said, is that he refuses to play the game — the game being the tacit business of saying a little more here and a little less there until you resemble something society can read. He also wrote a line that is quoted far too often: that Meursault is the only Christ we deserve. Discount it. It came thirteen years after publication, as self-defence against readers who had taken his character for a monster, and it plainly gilds him. Reading Meursault as a martyred saint and reading him as a psychopath are two ends of the same error — he is neither saint nor patient, but an ordinary man who values the honesty of his words above his own life.

Once you have hold of this, the world looks different. You start noticing how much of daily life consists of things one is obliged to say: the required degree of grief at a memorial, of joy at a wedding, of gratitude in a resignation letter, of enthusiasm for life online. Most of it is harmless, even kind — social lubricant. But The Stranger points out that the lubricant has teeth: refuse to pay your share of the performance and the price can be raised all the way to death.

Sun, sweat and sea: a man not driven by motive

What actually moves people in this novel is not thought but body. Meursault registers temperature, light, smell, fatigue: on the day of the funeral what he remembers is the glare off the road, the heat on the walk from Marengo to the cemetery making him dizzy, the nurse explaining that walking slowly risks sunstroke and walking fast means arriving sweat-soaked and catching a chill in the church. In his telling, the funeral is barely a mourning; it is an episode of heat exhaustion.

The killing pushes this to its limit. On the beach the sun stabs at his eyes like a blade, sweat gums his eyelids into a warm film, the Arab draws his knife and the light comes off the steel straight at him — and he fires. Then, after a pause, four more shots into a body that is no longer moving. His own account is that the four short reports were like four sharp knocks at the door of unhappiness.

Later, in court, the presiding judge asks why he fired, and the most famous line in the book arrives: "because of the sun." The gallery laughs. Generations of readers have taken this as proof that Meursault is deranged; in fact it is the most precise sentence in the novel. It is not a reason — it is the absence of a reason, forced into a format that demands one. The court's grammar has exactly one construction, because X, therefore Y, while what Meursault underwent was a chain of sensory events with no causal links at all. He can either invent a motive — jealousy, revenge, racial hatred; the court would happily take any of them — or report the one thing that was actually pressing on him in that instant: the sun. He chooses the second, and it sounds like madness.

Why does this matter? Because it inverts an assumption we live by. We believe motive comes first and action follows; the novel suggests that very often action comes first and motive is fitted on afterwards, so that the act can be told. Every time you explain to someone — or to yourself — why you did what you did, you are probably doing this. Meursault's only oddity is that he declines to.

Two mirrored halves: the same facts, and the second time they grow motives

The book's structure is its finest and least noticed piece of engineering. Part One (six chapters) is the happening: funeral, weekend, Marie, Raymond, the shots — reported day by day in degree-zero prose, explained never. Part Two (five chapters) is the trial: the same facts retold by prosecutor, lawyer and witnesses, this time equipped with character, motive, causation and moral colour.

The beauty of it is that Part Two introduces no new facts. Every item used to convict him, the reader has already watched happen. The white coffee is, in Part One, a cup the caretaker hands him in the middle of an all-night vigil; in Part Two it is proof of a man taking refreshment beside his mother's body. Swimming the next day, the Fernandel comedy (Fernandel being France's most popular screen comedian at the time), the night with Marie — in Part One that is a young man's weekend; in Part Two it is debauchery over a body not yet cold.

Part Two adds no evidence — it merely retells Part One. The conviction happens in the narration, not in the facts.

Which means the novel is doing something considerably larger than criticising courts: it walks the reader through the installation of meaning in real time. You see the facts naked, then you see them dressed, and afterwards you cannot pretend the clothes were part of the body. This is Camus's absurd translated into fiction: the world supplies no meaning, meaning is added from our side — and once you have watched the adding, you can never again mistake what was added for what was there.

What is really on trial: a soul, not a shot

The courtroom chapters are the coldest and sharpest in the book. Sitting in the dock, Meursault slowly registers something peculiar: everyone is discussing him and no one is speaking to him; the case is being handled as though he were not required, and he has been excluded from his own trial. At one point he wants to raise his hand and say something, then decides there is no point. He is a stranger even at his own execution — the second meaning of the title closes here.

Worse is where the prosecution puts its weight. The prosecutor spends far more time on the funeral than on the dead man, and delivers the sentence that decides the case: that Meursault buried his mother with the heart of a criminal (paraphrased). Note the shape of the accusation: it is not about the gun, it is about the heart. What the state sets out to prove is not what he did but what kind of man he is — and the method of proof is to retell his ordinary life as a character file. He smoked; he did not know his mother's exact age; he befriended a pimp; he swam; he slept. Not one of these is a crime, and together they establish that he is not one of us. The climax of the trial is not a legal argument at all but the prosecutor's announcement that this man's heart is empty, and that he therefore threatens society more gravely than the parricide due to be tried next (paraphrased).

If you have read Foucault's Discipline and Punish (Read 12 on this site) you will recognise the literary version of the same finding: modern justice nominally tries acts, but increasingly tries souls — motive, character, remorse, corrigibility. Meursault is precisely the input this machine cannot process: he offers no interior drama to interpret and manufactures no repentance. The system does not need him to confess; it needs him to perform what a man about to confess looks like. He cannot supply the performance, so the system rules that he is not a man.

That is also why his own lawyer and the magistrate unsettle us more than the prosecutor does. The lawyer wants him to testify that he had mastered his natural feelings that day. The examining magistrate brandishes a crucifix and demands that he believe; when he says he does not, the magistrate cries out, nearly undone, asking whether Meursault means his life to have no meaning. Notice which way that runs: it is not Meursault who needs God, it is the magistrate who needs Meursault to need God. A whole society stands around one man who will not join the chorus, in something close to panic.

The chaplain, and "the tender indifference of the world"

Meursault loses control exactly once, in the condemned cell, at the chaplain who has come a third time to press him toward repentance. The chaplain insists that he must at some point have longed for another life, that he must be able to see a divine face on the stone wall. Meursault is first bored, then detonates — and here, for the first time, Camus lets the language run long and hot; two hundred pages of degree-zero prose have been storing pressure for this page.

What he shouts, in substance: none of the chaplain's certainties is worth one hair of a woman's head — because the priest is not even certain he is alive, whereas Meursault is certain of that much. He looks empty-handed, he says, but he is sure of himself, sure of everything, surer than the priest; he holds this truth as firmly as it holds him. Then comes the heaviest stroke in the book: since everyone dies, it makes no difference how one lives or when one goes; and if that is so, then other people's deaths, a mother's love, the way anyone chooses to live — none of it can be weighed against anyone else, because the same ending has been picked out for all of them. It reads like nihilism until you look at where it lands: not "therefore nothing matters," but "therefore the ledger they have been using to rank, grade and convict people is a forgery."

With the chaplain thrown out comes one of the most beautiful passages in modern fiction. Meursault quiets, smells the night, earth and salt, feels the stars on his face: "For the first time I laid my heart open to the tender indifference of the world." (je m'ouvrais pour la première fois à la tendre indifférence du monde)

The phrase repays taking apart. "Indifference" is the world not answering, not caring, offering no account of itself — Camus's absurd, exactly. "Tender" is the novel's own addition: once you genuinely stop expecting an answer, the silence stops being a refusal and becomes something closer to company without hostility. An analogy: while you are still waiting for someone to reply, a quiet phone is torture; the day you truly stop waiting, the same quiet turns into peace. Nothing about the world has changed — what changed is that he withdrew the demand. This is where the novel goes a step beyond the essay: it does not argue the absurd into liveability, it simply lets you watch a man arrive there.

He then says he had been happy, and was happy still. And the last sentence stops most first-time readers cold: so that everything might be consummated, and so that he might feel less alone, he hoped for a large crowd of spectators on the day of his execution, greeting him with cries of hatred. This is not a wish for punishment. It is the book's single, absolute act of revolt: he wants no pardon, no understanding, no reconciliation — each of those would fold him back into the performance he has refused all along; he wants the opposition preserved intact to the last second. Cries of hatred are at least real. A moving memorial service would be a lie.

The distilled architecture

The book is a spare two-part structure in which the halves mirror each other: Part One shows you a man living his life, Part Two shows you a society rewriting that same life as a crime, and the ending shows you that after losing everything, he possesses something for the first time.

  • Part One (the happening): the mother's death → an ordinary weekend → entanglement with Raymond → the beach, the sun, five shots. Degree-zero narration, a tense that severs cause, facts laid bare — and the reader forced to supply motive. Supplying it is already taking the bait.
  • Part Two (the retelling): the same facts strung by the prosecution into character and premeditation; the burden of proof slides from what he did to what he is; Meursault becomes a spectator at his own trial and is condemned for having buried his mother with the heart of a criminal.
  • The ending (the turn): the chaplain refused → "not one of his certainties is worth a hair of a woman's head" → the tender indifference of the world → the admission of happiness → the wish to be met with cries of hatred.

What does it prove? Strictly, a novel proves nothing — and Camus laid down the rule himself in The Myth of Sisyphus: a genuinely absurd work describes and does not explain. The Stranger is that rule's model case. It states no doctrine; it arranges the material so that "meaning gets added afterwards" happens to the reader, on the spot. You have read the book properly only if you caught yourself supplying Meursault's motives, and caught yourself finding the prosecutor rather persuasive.

Misreadings and honest criticism

  • The commonest misreading: Meursault is a psychopath. The text runs the other way — he loved his mother, he is moved by Salamano's crying, he says he is like everybody else, he registers his own fatigue and desire without disguise. He is not without an interior; he is without the will to translate it into social language. Pathologising him repeats what the prosecutor does: using "he is abnormal" to excuse oneself from understanding him.
  • The second misreading: the book teaches detachment, teaches not caring. The ending says the opposite. His most alive and most violent moment is the tirade at the chaplain, and his happiness comes from an undiscounted appetite for the physical world — sun, seawater, a woman's hair, the smell of night. This is not a cold book; it is a book about what it costs to refuse to say things you do not mean.
  • The criticism that matters most: the murdered man has no name. He is only "the Arab" — he does not speak, no family appears, he has no history; the novel's moral weight sits entirely inside the killer while the killed is a patch of glare on a beach. Edward Said made exactly this cut in Culture and Imperialism (1993), arguing that Camus's Algeria is a Frenchman's landscape in which the indigenous population has been systematically flattened into scenery. In 2013 the Algerian novelist Kamel Daoud answered with The Meursault Investigation, narrated by the dead man's brother, which begins by giving him a name: Moussa. No contemporary reading of The Stranger can walk past this, and "Camus's position on colonialism was complicated" does not dispose of it.
  • The trial's plausibility has also been questioned. Critics including Conor Cruise O'Brien have pointed out that in 1940s French Algeria, a European sentenced to death for shooting an Arab was very nearly unthinkable in the actual workings of colonial justice. In other words, to make "society tries the soul" land, the novel quietly borrows a courtroom far fairer than the real one. That is both an artistic necessity and the weak point of the book taken as colonial realism.
  • The women are thin. Marie appears almost entirely as body, laughter and a dress, and her proposal is dispatched with a shrug; the novel takes no interest in her interior. It is the same flaw as the Don Juan chapter of The Myth of Sisyphus: Camus's absurd heroes are uniformly male, and women tend to be objects of perception rather than perceivers.
  • Discount Camus's own "the only Christ we deserve." It is a defence written thirteen years later, re-explaining a character built precisely to withhold explanation, and its tone sits badly against the novel's restraint. The plainer line from the same preface is closer to the text: Meursault simply refuses to play the game.
  • "Existentialist novel" is the wrong label. Camus rejected the term all his life and broke publicly with Sartre in 1952 over The Rebel. Sartrean existentialism turns on a self created through choice; Meursault is a man who barely chooses at all. Filing the book under Sartre loses the actual bone of it: not free choice, but the refusal to lie.

Ten sentences

1. "Maman died today. Or yesterday maybe, I don't know." — the register of the whole book is fixed in the first line: report the fact and the uncertainty, and never add an adjective.

2. Camus writes the novel in the compound past, cutting each sentence into an island: between them there is only "and then," never "and therefore" — causation drained out, so the reader supplies it, which is exactly the reflex Part Two puts on trial.

3. Meursault is not unfeeling; he will not translate feeling into the socially expected phrasing. He says he probably doesn't love Marie because he isn't sure — and in court that honesty is a hundred times more lethal than an insincere "I loved her."

4. Camus's own gloss is the harshest: in our society, any man who does not weep at his mother's funeral risks being sentenced to death (paraphrased). Meursault's sole offence is refusing to play the game everyone else is playing.

5. "Because of the sun." — not madness, but the absence of a reason forced into a format that demands one; the court knows only because X, therefore Y, while he underwent a chain of sensations with no links.

6. Part Two introduces no new facts and merely retells Part One: coffee becomes cold blood, a swim becomes debauchery — the conviction happens in the narration, not in the facts.

7. The prosecutor's summing-up never argues about the gun, only about the heart: he buried his mother with the heart of a criminal — modern justice nominally tries acts and actually tries souls, and Meursault is the one input the machine cannot process.

8. He is discussed throughout his trial and addressed by no one — a stranger even at his own execution, which is where the second meaning of the title closes.

9. The outburst at the chaplain is his one moment of eloquence: none of the priest's certainties is worth one hair of a woman's head (paraphrased) — because the priest is not even sure he is alive, and Meursault is.

10. "For the first time I laid my heart open to the tender indifference of the world." — "indifference" is the world never answering; "tender" is your finally stopping asking. Nothing in the world has changed; he withdrew the demand. And wanting cries of hatred at his execution is not masochism but a refusal to let any reconciliation smooth the opposition away.
