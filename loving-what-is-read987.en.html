Loving What Is

Loving What Is: Four Questions That Can Change Your Life · Byron Katie with Stephen Mitchell · 2002

中文 →

In One Sentence

What hurts you is never what happened; it is a thought you believe that fights with reality: "He should listen to me," "I shouldn't have been laid off," "My mother should have loved me more." Katie doesn't tell you to stop thinking it, and she doesn't hand you a nicer thought to put in its place. She gives you one fixed tool: write the thought down, put it through four questions, then turn it around and see whether the opposite is just as true, or truer. She calls the method The Work. The whole book is those four questions plus dozens of transcripts of Katie walking people through them live.

Where It Sits

Katie, born Byron Kathleen Reid in 1942, grew up in the desert town of Barstow, California. She is not an academic and comes from no religious lineage. Her co-author Stephen Mitchell is her husband, known for his translations of the Tao Te Ching and Gilgamesh; he shaped her spoken teaching into a book. It belongs to the family of modern spiritual books that rely on self-inquiry rather than doctrine, and it is the most manual-like of them: one worksheet, four questions, one turnaround. Most of her later books apply the same Work to other material: I Need Your Love—Is That True? to relationships, A Thousand Names for Joy to the Tao Te Ching, A Mind at Home with Itself to the Diamond Sutra. Understand this one and you have most of Katie.

The Core Claims

  • Suffering comes directly from believing a thought that argues with reality. The thing has already happened, and you keep insisting it shouldn't have. You can't win that argument. In one of her most quoted lines, when you argue with reality you lose, but only 100% of the time (paraphrased).
  • A thought is harmless until you believe it; the attachment does the damage. Thoughts arise on their own, like weather, and you can't stop them, but you can examine them. A thought that has been honestly questioned loosens by itself; you don't have to suppress it or swap it out (paraphrased).
  • Your judgments of other people usually apply just as well to you. Turn "he should..." into "I should..." and you often find the sentence you most needed to hear. That makes the people who annoy you most into raw material for knowing yourself.

The Key Concepts, One by One

1. Arguing with reality: the formula for suffering

What it is. Katie's diagnosis is blunt: whenever you think reality should be different from what it is, you suffer. "He shouldn't have treated me that way." "It shouldn't be raining." "I should be thinner." Each sentence carries a "should" or "shouldn't," and each has the same shape: reality is A, and I insist it ought to be B. Her image for this: wanting reality to be different is like trying to teach a cat to bark. You can try for the rest of your life, and in the end the cat will look up at you and say meow (paraphrased).

The mechanism. Separate two things: the event, and your thought about the event. Rain is just rain; it doesn't hurt. What hurts is "It shouldn't be raining today, my plans are ruined" playing on a loop. Katie doesn't deny that events cause real losses. Her point is that the loss is one thing, and the thought "this shouldn't have happened" stacks a second layer of suffering on top of it, a layer that is often heavier and lasts longer than the first. She uses herself as the example: she says she loves what is not because she is a spiritual person but because it hurts when she argues with reality (paraphrased). Acceptance, on this view, is not a moral duty. It is an observation about what causes pain.

An example. A traffic jam. You sit there thinking "Who taught these people to drive?" and "I shouldn't be late." Your blood pressure rises, your hands tighten on the wheel, you start swearing. The traffic moves exactly as fast as before. The only thing you can change is your own situation: call ahead, put something on to listen to, take another route. Katie's claim is that a mind that isn't fighting reality is clearer and finds those practical moves faster, while a mind full of "shoulds" spends most of its energy wrestling with a fact that has already happened.

How it changes your view. The next time a "should" comes up, you can notice it as a sentence at war with reality. Noticing it doesn't mean approving of what someone did, and it doesn't mean giving up on action. It just separates "how I want things to be" from "how things are right now." The first can set the direction of your next move; the second is where the move starts. Blur them together and you end up furious and standing still.

2. Three kinds of business: mine, yours, and God's

What it is. Katie says she can find only three kinds of business in the universe: mine, yours, and God's. She uses "God" to mean reality, since reality is what runs things. What I wear today and how I respond are my business. What you think of me and whether you quit smoking are your business. Earthquakes, the weather and when I die are God's business, and nobody controls them.

The mechanism. Her observation is that much of our stress comes from mentally living in someone else's business. When you think "You should get a real job" or "You should take better care of yourself," your mind has moved into the other person's business. And then, she asks, if you're living in my business, who is here living yours? We're both over there (paraphrased). What gets abandoned is the one territory where you can actually do something. Worrying about earthquakes or aging is meddling in God's business, which changes nothing except how anxious you are.

An example. A mother lies awake over her adult daughter's choice of partner. Sort it with the three categories: who the daughter marries is the daughter's business; whether the marriage turns out happy is, to a large extent, God's business; and what the mother can do right now is her own business: how she talks to her daughter, whether she sleeps tonight, how she lives her own life outside this one worry. The problem doesn't vanish, but she now knows where her effort can go.

How it changes your view. It works as a quick sorting screen. Whenever you feel irritated, lonely or offended, ask: whose business am I in right now? Katie says that simply noticing you've wandered into someone else's business can bring you back to yourself (paraphrased). This isn't indifference. You can still care, advise and help; you just stop making "they have to change the way I want" the condition for your own peace.

3. The Judge-Your-Neighbor Worksheet: catching the thought

What it is. The Work starts not with meditation but with a sheet of paper. Katie's Judge-Your-Neighbor Worksheet asks you to pick one specific situation with one specific person and write down, honestly, six kinds of thought (paraphrased):

  1. Who angers, confuses or saddens you, and why? (For example: I'm angry at Paul because he doesn't listen to me.)
  2. How do you want them to change? What do you want them to do?
  3. What should or shouldn't they do, be, think or feel? What advice would you give them?
  4. What do you need from them in order to be happy?
  5. What do you think of them? Make a list.
  6. What is it about this person or situation that you never want to experience again?

Why write it this way. Her instruction is the opposite of what you'd expect: be petty, be judgmental, be childish; don't be generous and don't be "spiritual" (paraphrased). Only judgments that land on the page raw and unedited give the questions something to work on. Thoughts circling in your head are slippery; the moment you notice one, it reshapes itself into something respectable ("I'm just concerned about him"). Written down, it is pinned in place. She also suggests starting with other people rather than yourself, because judgments aimed outward are more direct and less guarded, and, as the turnaround will show, they eventually come back to you anyway.

How it changes your view. The worksheet shows you that your suffering is made of sentences you can write down, not an amorphous fog of feeling. Anything you can write as a sentence, you can test one sentence at a time.

4. The four questions: putting one thought on trial

Take one line from the worksheet, say "Paul should listen to me," and ask four questions in order:

StepQuestionWhat it does
1Is it true?Yes or no only; interrupts automatic belief
2Can you absolutely know that it's true?Separates "I think" from "I know"
3How do you react when you believe that thought?Shows the cost in body, emotion and behavior
4Who would you be without the thought?Lets you experience the same situation without it
TurnaroundFlip the sentence and find at least three examples of the opposite being trueTests whether the opposite is as true or truer

The full sequence: one worksheet, one sentence at a time, four questions, then the turnaround.

Questions one and two: shaking the certainty. The first two questions want a one-word answer. Katie stresses that the answer isn't something you figure out; you get quiet and wait for it to surface, and she calls The Work a form of meditation (paraphrased). "Paul should listen to me." Is it true? Most people's first reaction is "Of course." The second question pushes further: can you absolutely know that? Can you know that his listening right now would be best for him, for you, for the relationship? Is there a law in the universe saying a person "should" do this? Many people stop here and realize, for the first time, that the "should" was their own wish dressed up as a fact. Answering yes is fine too; you simply go on to question three.

Question three: seeing the cost. This is where the method pushes hardest. What happens when you believe "Paul should listen to me"? You interrupt him when he talks, roll your eyes, go cold. Your chest tightens and your stomach drops. You replay every time he hasn't listened and feel more and more wronged. Maybe you phone a friend to complain, or eat a bag of chips. In the book Katie follows up: how do you treat him then? How do you treat yourself? Does the thought bring peace or stress? (paraphrased) The point isn't to scold you; it's to let you see with your own eyes that every time you believe the thought, you pay, and the other person usually has no idea.

Question four: you without the thought. Now picture the same scene: Paul is looking at his phone, you're talking, he isn't listening. Nothing about the scene changes except that the thought "he should listen to me" simply isn't in your head. Who would you be? People in the dialogues answer things like "I'd just look at him; maybe I'd notice he's tired," "I'd stop and go do something else," "I'd be so much lighter." Katie doesn't let people rush to an empty "I'd be better"; she asks them to actually feel that state. Set questions three and four side by side and, for the first time, you can tell the difference: what hurt was the thought, not the fact that Paul wasn't listening.

How it changes your view. The strength of the four questions is that they never argue with the content of the thought and never supply a "more correct" replacement. They just make visible the wire running from the thought to the pain. You don't have to let go of the thought, Katie says; once it is seen clearly, it lets go of you (paraphrased).

5. The turnaround: flipping the sentence

What it is. After the four questions comes the turnaround: flip the original sentence into its opposites, then find at least three genuine, specific examples for each turnaround and see whether it is as true as the original, or truer. The three common directions:

  • To the self: "Paul should listen to me" becomes "I should listen to me." Am I listening to myself? Part of me knew it wasn't the moment for this conversation and I pushed ahead anyway; my body was tired and I ignored it.
  • To the other: "I should listen to Paul." He said he was exhausted today. Did I hear that? I was busy talking about my own thing; I was the one not listening.
  • To the opposite: "Paul shouldn't listen to me." Katie's reasoning is that in reality he isn't listening right now, so by reality's lights he "shouldn't" be. He's answering a work email that may genuinely be more urgent; he may have heard this story five times already.

The sixth worksheet statement turns around differently: "I never want to..." becomes "I'm willing to..." and "I look forward to...." "I never want to argue with Paul again" becomes "I'm willing to argue with Paul again" and "I look forward to arguing with Paul again," because if it happens again it will surface whatever thoughts you haven't yet questioned and give you another chance to do The Work (paraphrased).

Why it matters. The turnaround is what sets this book apart from ordinary self-questioning. The accusations you level at others are often demands you make of yourself, or things you are doing yourself, projected outward. Katie says the advice you write for other people is exactly your own prescription (paraphrased). With practice, the person you find most unbearable becomes a mirror; one of her lines, roughly, is that until you can see the enemy as a friend, your Work is not done.

The limits she sets. A turnaround is not a confession, and it is not an excuse for the other person. Katie says repeatedly that the point is not "it's your fault too" but a wider view: the original sentence gave you one angle, and the turnarounds show you others that stand up just as well (paraphrased). If you can't find real examples for a turnaround, drop it rather than forcing it.

How it changes your view. Anything that leaves you seething now comes with at least four viewpoints: the original, the turnaround to the self, to the other, and to the opposite. Once you can hold all four, it is hard to cling to "it's all his fault."

6. Underlying beliefs and the worst that could happen

What it is. The later part of the book takes The Work deeper. One target is the lifelong beliefs underneath everything else: "Life should be fair," "People should be kind," "I need other people's approval." The other is what you fear most: abandonment, illness, death. Katie's stance on fear is that what frightens you is usually not the event itself but your thoughts about it: the scenes you rehearse, the "I could never survive that."

Mechanism and example. Take "If he leaves me, my life is over." Is it true? Can you absolutely know it? How do you treat him when you believe it? You cling, check his phone, tiptoe around him, and push him further away. Who would you be without the thought? More relaxed with him, and more like yourself. Turn it around: "If he leaves me, my life begins." Three examples: I'd have time for things I've shelved for years; I'd stop shrinking myself; I'd meet new people. The Work doesn't change the fact that he might leave; it dismantles the catastrophe you've attached to that possibility.

Her own story. This is Katie's account of herself. For roughly a decade before 1986, she says, she was sunk in depression, rage and self-loathing, often unable to get out of bed. She ended up in a halfway house for women with eating disorders in Los Angeles, felt unworthy of a bed, and slept on the floor. One morning a cockroach crawled over her foot, and she found that all her thoughts, her whole old story, were gone, replaced by a joy she couldn't contain. Her summary, roughly: when I believed my thoughts I suffered, when I didn't believe them I didn't suffer, and this is true for every human being. It is her own testimony, with no outside corroboration. Later she broke that experience down into four questions anyone can follow.

The Skeleton of the Argument

Strip away the dialogues and examples, and the argument is a single line:

Diagnosis: people suffer not from events but from particular thoughts about events, especially "should" thoughts, which set you against reality. Location: those thoughts are concrete, writable sentences, mostly about other people's business or God's; the worksheet pins them to the page. Testing: the four questions remove your certainty about the thought, show you what it costs, and let you experience yourself without it. Reversal: the turnaround moves your attention from "he should" back to "I can," converting blame into self-knowledge. Conclusion: a thought seen clearly loses its grip; you become calmer and better able to act sanely on what is actually in front of you.

How does the book establish this? Almost entirely by demonstration, not argument. Most of its pages are live dialogues between Katie and participants: a wife questioning her husband's coldness, a businessman questioning a colleague's incompetence, someone questioning their own body, a mother questioning her rebellious child. You watch people soften, laugh and cry on the page, then try it on your own thoughts. The demonstrations, plus what you feel after doing one yourself, are the book's evidence.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "loving what is" means accepting whatever is done to you. Katie has said many times that The Work doesn't ask you to stay where you're being harmed or to approve of injustice. Her claim is that a mind not arguing with reality is clearer and better able to do what needs doing, including leaving, saying no, or calling the police (paraphrased). Still, the book spends far more time on how not to suffer than on when to act, which feeds the criticisms below.

Misreading 2: The Work is positive thinking. Positive thinking covers a bad thought with a good one. The Work supplies no replacement thought; it makes you stare at the original one, and even asks you to write it more meanly. The turnaround isn't "say it more positively" either; it is a search for real evidence that the opposite holds.

Misreading 3: the turnaround means "it's all my fault." Turning "He shouldn't have hurt me" into "I shouldn't hurt me" asks whether you are keeping the hurt going through replay and self-attack. It does not say you brought the harm on yourself.

Criticism 1: it overlaps heavily with cognitive behavioral therapy. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), developed from the 1960s by the psychiatrist Aaron Beck and others, centers on cognitive restructuring: you catch the negative thoughts that pop up on their own (automatic thoughts), cross-examine them ("What's the evidence for this? Against it? How else could I see it?"), and arrive at a more balanced view. Earlier still, the psychologist Albert Ellis built rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT) around attacking absolute "shoulds" and "musts," which he saw as the core of emotional disturbance. Side by side, "Is it true?" and "Can you absolutely know?" are close to Socratic questioning, and "arguing with reality" is close to what Ellis called absolutist demands. The genuinely new parts are two: question four, which asks you to experience a state without the thought rather than reason your way to a new one, and the turnaround. Critics therefore see The Work as an existing psychological technique rebranded in spiritual language, without the assessment and safety norms that come with clinical practice.

Criticism 2: the evidence is thin. CBT is backed by hundreds of randomized controlled trials. For The Work, as far as we know, the published research amounts to a handful of small pilot studies, mostly from a few researchers in Israel, generally without rigorous controls and with short follow-up. Its reputation travels mainly through personal experience and the charisma of Katie's live sessions. Katie herself has said The Work is not therapy, but saying so doesn't substitute for evidence of what it does.

Criticism 3: it can push victims toward self-blame and minimize real harm. For someone who has been abused, assaulted or treated unjustly for years, asking "He shouldn't have hit me: is it true?" and then hunting for three reasons why "he should have hit me" can be deeply wounding. It can shift attention away from the perpetrator's responsibility, and away from safety and getting help, toward why the victim is "still suffering." Katie's answer is that The Work denies no facts and blocks no action, but using this method with people living with trauma, depression or severe anxiety, without a professional in the loop, carries real risk. In those cases, evidence-based trauma treatment and medical care come first.

Criticism 4: facilitators are unlicensed. Katie's organization runs its own facilitator certification, but certified facilitators need not be licensed therapists and are not bound by clinical ethics codes or professional regulation. The Work, one-on-one or on stage, often reaches very deep emotion, and someone without clinical training may not recognize who needs referral or when to stop.

Criticism 5: worries about group dynamics. Katie's public events and paid multi-day intensives (The School for The Work) are emotionally charged: people confront their most painful thoughts on stage, around a founder whom followers hold in very high regard. Some critics and former participants describe the atmosphere as cult-like. That is their characterization; we know of no legal finding to that effect, and many participants report real benefit. The fair version is this: any group that turns one person's awakening into a universal method and centers on a charismatic founder deserves your independent judgment, especially when it asks for large fees or long commitments.

Criticism 6: an epistemic slide. Question two invites you to doubt a value judgment like "Paul should listen to me," which is fair, since a "should" really isn't a fact. But the same move can be used to doubt anything, including well-founded judgments like "I was treated unfairly." The Work offers no criterion for which thoughts deserve doubt and which deserve to be held onto.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · The second arrow: The Pali Sallatha Sutta says everyone is struck by a first arrow (bodily pain), but the untrained then shoot themselves with a second (resistance and grief). Alike in splitting suffering into event and added reaction; unlike in that the Buddha has you attend to the feeling itself, not test a sentence for truth.
  • Western philosophy · Epictetus: The Stoic Epictetus writes in the Enchiridion that people are disturbed not by things but by their judgments about things, and sorts matters into what is and isn't up to us. Close to a prototype of the three kinds of business; unlike in that Stoics reason toward right action, while Katie lets a thought loosen under questioning.
  • Western philosophy · Nietzsche's amor fati: In Ecce Homo, Nietzsche calls amor fati his formula for greatness: wanting nothing to be different, not merely bearing what is necessary but loving it (paraphrased). Alike in a full yes to what has happened; unlike in that Nietzsche embraces suffering itself, while Katie thinks most suffering comes from thoughts and can be questioned away.
  • Psychology · Jung's projection: Jung held that we project traits we won't own (the "shadow") onto others, so what grates most in them is often a doorway to ourselves (paraphrased). The same shape as the turnaround to the self; unlike in that projection is unconscious and takes long analysis, while a turnaround is a deliberate few-minute exercise.
  • Neuroscience · Memory reconsolidation: In 2000 Karim Nader and colleagues showed in rats that a reactivated fear memory becomes briefly unstable and must be stored again, opening a window in which it can be altered. Structurally, question three vividly reactivates the painful scene and question four immediately pairs that same scene with a different feeling, much like editing within that window. Unlike in that no study has tested whether The Work actually acts through this mechanism, and reconsolidation effects in humans remain contested.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. What hurts you is not what happened but a thought you believe that argues with reality.

2. Argue with reality and you lose, every single time (paraphrased).

3. There are only three kinds of business: mine, yours and God's; minding the last two is where stress comes from.

4. Write the thought down, as petty as you can: only a thought pinned to paper can be tested.

5. Is it true? Can you absolutely know it's true? How do you react when you believe it? Who would you be without it?

6. Question three shows what the thought costs; question four shows you without it. Side by side, you see the pain came from the thought, not the other person.

7. You don't have to let go of a thought; once seen clearly, it lets go of you (paraphrased).

8. Turn it around: what you demand of others is often your own prescription.

9. Loving what is isn't resignation or approval of harm; a clear mind is better at saying no, leaving, and doing what needs doing.

10. But it overlaps heavily with CBT, its evidence is thin, and it carries risk around trauma: treat it as a tool you can test for yourself, not a person to follow.
