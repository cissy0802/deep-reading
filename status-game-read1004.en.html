The Status Game

The Status Game: On Social Position and How We Use It · Will Storr · 2021

中文 →

In One Sentence

Humans are group animals who need not only to belong but to rise; our brains experience life as a series of games played for status — with three kinds of rules: dominance, virtue and success — and much of what is best and worst in human civilization comes down to how we play, and which game we choose.

Where It Sits

Storr is a British journalist and nonfiction writer whose earlier books include The Science of Storytelling and Selfie; his long-running subject is how culture shapes the self. This book stitches social psychology, evolutionary anthropology and historical case studies into a single framework — a popular "big theory" synthesis rather than original research.

The Core Argument

  • Status isn't vanity; it's a basic need. Our ancestors could survive only in groups, and your position in the group decided your share of food, mates and protection. So evolution gave us two powerful social drives: to connect with others (getting along) and to rise among them (getting ahead).
  • Life can be understood as playing many status games at once. Each game has its own rules and symbols: which behavior earns respect, who counts as "higher." The games come in three types — dominance games won by force, virtue games won by being dutiful and morally correct, and success games won by skill and results.
  • Games rewrite our beliefs. To win within a group, we come to sincerely believe its rules are the truth about the world. When a virtue game turns closed and zealous, and gains power, it can become persecution and tyranny. Seeing this helps you read history — and choose your own games more wisely.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Status hunger: why being thought well of was a matter of life and death

Storr starts from an evolutionary observation. Through the long era of hunting and gathering, a person who was shunned or held in low regard had sharply worse odds of surviving and reproducing. So the brain continuously, automatically tracks one thing — how much am I worth in other people's eyes? Borrowing from the personality psychologist Robert Hogan, Storr boils human social motivation down to two drives: getting along and getting ahead. The first makes us crave belonging; the second makes us crave status.

Status isn't money or a title; those are just its symbols. Status itself is a sense of value granted to you by others: people are willing to listen to you, defer to you, copy you. It is relative and exists only in comparison.

A key piece of evidence Storr draws on is the Whitehall Studies, in which the epidemiologist Michael Marmot followed British civil servants for years. Even though all of them had respectable jobs and access to doctors, the lower someone's grade, the higher their risk of heart disease and early death — a gradient that ran step by step down the hierarchy and couldn't be explained away by smoking, diet or other habits. Simply occupying a low rung puts the body under chronic stress. Low status doesn't just feel bad; it gets written into your health.

An analogy: your sense of status is like a program always running in the background. You rarely notice it, but it decides whether you dare to speak up in a meeting, how furious you get when someone cuts in line, and that little sting when an old classmate gets promoted.

How it changes your view: plenty of seemingly irrational behavior — a fistfight over an insult, months of salary on a watch, arguing past midnight with a stranger online — becomes perfectly intelligible once you put it on the status ledger. What people care about is often not the thing itself but what it says about who they are and where they rank.

2. Three games: dominance, virtue, success

Storr argues there are essentially only three ways humans win status, matching three kinds of game:

GameWhere status comes fromTypical settingsDark side
DominanceThreat, force and fear make others give wayGangs, school bullying, despotic rulePaid for by harming others; status collapses along with the power behind it
VirtueFollowing the rules, displaying loyalty and moral correctnessReligious communities, etiquette-bound societies, ideological movementsSlides easily into conformity, denunciation and purging the "impure"
SuccessSkill, knowledge, results and real contributions to the groupScience, crafts, sport, businessCan also become endless one-upmanship, but is the likeliest to grow the pie

Table: Storr's three kinds of status game. Most real games mix all three in different proportions.

Behind this scheme lies the distinction drawn by the evolutionary anthropologists Joseph Henrich and Francisco Gil-White between two forms of status: dominance is earned by making others afraid of you; prestige is earned by making others want to learn from you. A chimpanzee alpha rules mainly through dominance. What's distinctive about humans is prestige: we voluntarily respect, approach and imitate skilled people, because learning from them helps us live better, and in exchange they receive deference and a following. Storr splits prestige further into being respected because you are "good" (virtue) and because you are "good at things" (success).

Why it matters: the distinction shows that the same respected position can rest on entirely different foundations. A boss may be respected for competence — or "respected" because he can cost you your job. The first kind you quietly copy; the second you merely bow to.

How it changes your view: in any group, you can ask — how do people move up here? Through volume and threats, through displays of loyalty, or through getting things done? The answer nearly predicts how healthy the group is. Storr's view is that the more a society lets success games prevail, the likelier it is to flourish; the more dominance and virtue games take over, the likelier it is to stagnate or turn to persecution.

3. Games are relative, endless and nested

Status games differ from ordinary goals in one fundamental way: there's no finish line. Earn a million while everyone around you earns ten, and you won't feel like a winner. Status is a ranking, rankings are relative, and there is always another rung. Storr notes this is why people in societies that have grown vastly richer haven't become correspondingly more content — the score is set by comparison.

The second feature is that games are nested and multiple. One person may be playing different games at once — at work, in the family, in a running club, on a hobby forum. Lose in one, and you instinctively look elsewhere for one you can win. Storr thinks this explains why identity matters so much: the groups you identify with are the games you've chosen to play, and your position in them gives you your sense of worth. Someone invisible at the office may be a revered master in a model-building community — people naturally migrate toward games they can win.

The third feature is that every game has its own symbols and rules. Among programmers, wearing a suit may cost you points; at an investment bank, not wearing one might. In academia the symbol is citations; on the street, it's whether you hit back. We are acutely sensitive to these symbols and often adjust our clothes, slang, taste and even opinions to the group's rules without noticing.

An analogy: everyone is logged into several online games at once, each with its own leaderboard and gear system. Get stuck in one and you grind harder in another — and it's easy to forget that every one of these leaderboards is man-made.

How it changes your view: "Why am I never satisfied?" gets a structural answer — you're playing a game designed never to end. Rather than hoping to win enough in one game to feel content, choose your games deliberately, play several, and avoid staking your whole self on a single leaderboard.

4. The brain as a hero-maker

In The Science of Storytelling, Storr argued that the brain constantly edits experience into a story starring "me," and that in this story we are almost always the good guy. Here he connects that to status: to protect our sense of standing, the brain automatically argues our case — our successes are our doing, our failures the circumstances'; our group is righteous, the other side suspect.

Psychology has abundant evidence for this self-serving bias, the tendency to credit ourselves for good outcomes and blame outside forces for bad ones. Most people rate their own morality, driving and fairness as above average — which can't be true of everyone. Storr's inference: everyone is the hero of their own story, so almost no one thinks of themselves as the villain — including people who have done terrible things.

Why it matters: this is why conflicts are so hard to resolve. Both sides sincerely believe they're defending what's right, and every rebuttal sounds like an attack on their standing. We aren't exchanging views; we're defending our place in the story.

How it changes your view: next time you feel the heat of righteous anger in an argument, ask — am I defending the facts, or my status? And recognizing that the other person also sees themselves as the hero is where negotiation and reconciliation begin.

5. Humiliation: the flip side of status, and fuel for violence

If status is value granted by others, humiliation is having that value publicly stripped away. Storr argues that humiliation is among the most intense and dangerous emotions a person can feel. It isn't only painful; it stirs an urge to even the score — either by winning status back or by destroying the game or the person that shamed you.

The book discusses a range of cases: individuals long excluded and mocked who turned their resentment into violence against others, and, on a larger scale, how a people that feels collectively humiliated can be readily mobilized by a leader promising to restore its glory. He treats Germany's sense of humiliation after defeat in the First World War as part of the psychological soil in which Nazism grew — a game that promised to turn the "losers" back into the superior had a terrible pull.

An analogy: status is like social body temperature. Humiliation is a sudden plunge; the body shivers violently and hunts for any heat source, sometimes willing to set the whole house on fire.

How it changes your view: faced with an angry person or group, rather than asking "what do they want?", ask "where do they feel they've been looked down on?" Many conflicts that look like fights over interests are really fights over dignity, and letting the other side save face often defuses things better than concessions. Conversely, publicly humiliating someone — even someone genuinely in the wrong — often buys deeper hostility rather than reform.

6. When virtue games become tyranny: belief, conformity and purity spirals

This is the book's sharpest section. Storr argues that joining a game means not only following its rules but sincerely believing they describe how the world really is. Religions, political ideologies and professional cultures all supply a full account of what is good and who is high or low; the more deeply people play, the harder it becomes to see these accounts as the rules of a game rather than unquestionable fact.

Virtue games have a particularly dangerous dynamic. In a group where status is won by moral correctness, showing stronger loyalty and condemning heretics more harshly raises your standing. So everyone has an incentive to be a little more extreme than the next person, and the bar keeps rising — a purity spiral in which, eventually, no one is pure enough, and denunciation and purges become ways to win status. Storr uses historical cases such as China's Cultural Revolution and the Soviet Union's political purges to show that when a virtue game fuses with a dominance game and gains the power to punish, a moral movement can devour its own participants.

He extends the same logic to today's social media: public call-outs and online pile-ons are, in his reading, often virtue games too — participants display and raise their moral standing by condemning others, while whether the target's offense was actually serious matters surprisingly little. (This is the most contested part of the book; see below.)

Why it matters: it explains a recurring historical puzzle — why ordinary, even kind, people eagerly join in persecuting their neighbors. Not because they're innately cruel, but because in that game, persecution is how you win status.

How it changes your view: if your group grows ever keener to find people who aren't pure enough and ever less tolerant of internal dissent, that's an alarm. Storr's advice, roughly: stake more of your status on success games — earn respect through genuinely useful skills and contributions — and be generous in granting status to others, because status isn't always zero-sum, and an environment where everyone can find a dignified place is the one least likely to spin out of control.

The Spine of the Book

The whole book reduces to one line. (1) Humans evolved in groups, where being accepted and rising both bore directly on survival, so the brain is built to track status. (2) Status is relative value granted by others, won in three ways: dominance (being feared), virtue (following the rules and showing loyalty) and success (being skilled and useful); the latter two are the uniquely human forms of prestige. (3) We play many nested status games at once, and they never end — so material plenty doesn't bring contentment, and people keep migrating toward games they can win. (4) To win, the brain casts us as heroes of our own stories and makes us sincerely believe our group's rules are the truth. (5) Losing status — above all, being humiliated — provokes a fierce urge for revenge, fuel for much individual violence and collective frenzy. (6) Once a virtue game closes in on itself and gains the power to punish, purity spirals follow, and with them persecution and tyranny. (7) So, for individuals and societies alike, the better strategy is to play success games, beware of dominance and zealous virtue games, and give status generously to others.

How does it make its case? By threading a large body of existing research — prestige theory from evolutionary anthropology, epidemiology on status and health, social psychology on conformity and self-serving bias — through a long run of historical and contemporary cases, using "status" as the key that opens every lock. Its persuasive power comes from how much it explains, and so does its weakness.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

  • Misreading one: "It's a guide to climbing the ladder." It isn't a success manual. Storr describes status games so you can see how they manipulate you; his advice is closer to cooling down — recognize the games are man-made, don't stake your whole self on one leaderboard, play fewer dominance games, and be kind to other people's need for status.
  • Misreading two: "Seeking status is shameful." Storr insists the drive is simply part of human nature, nothing to be ashamed of; success games are precisely the engine behind progress in science, art and the economy. The issue is not wanting respect but how, and in which game, you earn it.
  • Criticism one: one cause to explain everything. The most common objection is that the book reduces nearly all human behavior to status, so that love, curiosity, the search for meaning or plain fun are easily recast as "really about status after all." A theory that can explain everything is usually hard to falsify. When any counterexample can be filed under "another game," the framework's predictive power suffers.
  • Criticism two: uneven evidence in a popular synthesis. Storr is a journalist, not a researcher, and he deploys solid findings (like Whitehall) at the same level as looser evolutionary narratives and historical analogies. Evolutionary psychology's accounts of why our ancestors behaved a certain way can be hard to test — critics call them "just-so stories," plausible-sounding origin tales invented after the fact that can't be verified.
  • Criticism three: selective about contemporary politics. By placing today's online moral condemnation on the same continuum as historical persecutions, the book struck some reviewers as unbalanced and colored by the author's own politics, and as less even-handed about the virtue games played across different ideological camps.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · conceit (māna): Buddhism lists māna, the conceit of comparing oneself with others, among the root afflictions, and the texts often divide it into three — thinking "I am better," "I am equal," or "I am worse." All three count as conceit, because each assumes a self that needs ranking. This is the same structure as Storr's status hunger: suffering comes from the background machine endlessly computing "where do I rank?" Where it differs: Storr wants you to switch to a better game; Buddhism wants you to see through the "I" who is playing.
  • Philosophy · Hegel's recognition: In the Phenomenology of Spirit, Hegel's master–slave dialectic holds that self-consciousness needs to be recognized by another self-consciousness, so the two struggle for recognition. Recognition is the heart of any status game. Where it differs: Hegel thinks the master ends up stuck — recognition from someone he doesn't regard as an equal is hollow — a bleaker picture than Storr's "winners."
  • Anthropology and literary theory · Girard's mimetic desire: René Girard argued that desire isn't spontaneous but imitative: we want things because our models want them. Desires converge, rivalry intensifies, and groups often discharge the conflict by uniting against a scapegoat — strikingly close to the purity spirals and purges of virtue games. Where it differs: Girard's engine is contagious desire; Storr's is the ranking of status.
  • Neuroscience and primatology · Sapolsky's baboons: The neurobiologist Robert Sapolsky spent decades studying wild baboons in Kenya and found that low-ranking animals typically have higher stress-hormone levels — a remarkable echo of the Whitehall findings in humans. More intriguingly, after the troop's most aggressive males died from eating infected garbage, its culture became more peaceful and stayed that way for years, even as new males joined (Sapolsky and Share, 2004). Where it differs: baboon hierarchy is almost entirely a dominance game, while humans have invented games of prestige and virtue.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. We have two fundamental social drives: to be accepted and to rise; the hunger for status isn't vanity but a survival need evolution wrote into the brain.

2. Low status doesn't just feel bad — it gets written into your health as chronic stress; in Whitehall, each rung down carried a little more risk.

3. Money and titles are only symbols; status itself is the value others are willing to grant you, and it exists only in comparison.

4. There are three ways to win: dominance makes people fear you, virtue makes them trust you, success makes them want to learn from you.

5. Status games have no finish line, so material plenty doesn't automatically bring contentment — the leaderboard always has another rung.

6. We play many games at once, and when we lose one we migrate to another we can win; identity is the game you've chosen.

7. The brain is a hero-maker: almost no one thinks of themselves as the villain of the story.

8. Humiliation is a sudden drop in status; many conflicts that look like fights over interests are really fights over dignity.

9. Once a virtue game holds the power to punish, it becomes a purity spiral, and persecution becomes a way to win status.

10. The better way to play: favor success games, remember the leaderboards are man-made, and give status generously to others.
