Ethics

Ethica, Ordine Geometrico Demonstrata · Baruch de Spinoza · 1677

中文 →

In one sentence

A man expelled from his own community at twenty-three, who made his living grinding optical lenses, wrote a book about how to live in the format of a geometry textbook — definitions, axioms, propositions, proofs — and reached this conclusion: there is only one thing in the universe; it is both "God" and "Nature"; and you are not a product it manufactured but a fold in it. This God has no will, no plan, no preferences, hands out no rewards and no punishments — "strictly speaking, God loves no one and hates no one." Hence no miracles, no purposes, and no free will: you feel you are choosing only because you are aware of your desires and ignorant of their causes. It sounds like total disenchantment, but Spinoza's ambition runs the other way. He set out to prove that seeing all of this clearly is the one thing that gives a person any chance of not being dragged around by their own emotions. Freedom is not "I could have done otherwise"; freedom is acting from your own nature rather than being pushed from outside — and the road to it is not the suppression of feeling but its understanding, because "an affect can be checked only by a contrary and stronger affect."

Coordinates

Spinoza (1632–1677) was born in Amsterdam into the Portuguese-Jewish community, descendants of refugees driven out of Iberia by the Inquisition. In 1656, not yet twenty-four, he was permanently expelled by a cherem (writ of excommunication) whose language is the harshest in that congregation's records — the charges left vague, citing only "abominable heresies" and "monstrous deeds." He dropped the Hebrew name Baruch for the Latin Benedictus (both mean "blessed"). He never married, earned his living grinding lenses, lived in rented rooms in The Hague, and in 1673 turned down a chair in philosophy at Heidelberg — because the offer came with a clause requiring him not to disturb the established religion. He died of a lung ailment in 1677 at forty-four, quite possibly aggravated by years of inhaled glass dust. Only one book appeared under his own name in his lifetime (a textbook-style reworking of Descartes); the Ethics was published anonymously by friends months after his death and banned outright by the Dutch authorities the following year. For more than a century afterward, "Spinozist" was simply European for "atheist" — until the German Pantheism Controversy of 1785 dug him back up, and Goethe, Novalis (who called him "the God-intoxicated man"), Hegel and eventually Einstein claimed him one after another.

The central claims

Five hundred pages of proofs circle three claims, each resting on the last:

  • There is exactly one substance; it is God, and it is Nature. Descartes had left a three-storey world — God, mind, matter — with the storeys separate. Spinoza flattens it to one: only one thing can possibly explain itself, and everything else — galaxies, your body, this thought — is not an independent thing but a state of that one thing. God is therefore neither outside the world nor prior to it. God is the necessity by which the world runs.
  • Which means nature contains no purposes, no accidents, and no free will. Rain does not fall "for" the crops, eyes are not "for" seeing, and you could not "have" not sent that message. Human beings see purpose everywhere because they know what they want without knowing why they want it, and project that illusion onto the cosmos. Good and evil, beauty and ugliness, order and chaos are not properties of the world but modes of imagining it.
  • None of which leads to resignation — it is where the ethics starts. The essence of any thing is the effort by which it keeps itself in existence. Being shoved around by external things is bondage; acting from your own nature is freedom. The difference lies not in whether you have emotions but in whether your idea of them is confused or clear. "Blessedness is not the reward of virtue, but virtue itself" — salvation is not settled after death; it is paid out at the moment of understanding.

The core concepts, one at a time

The geometrical order: why a book about feelings is written like Euclid

The form puts most readers off before page two. Part I opens with eight definitions and seven axioms, then thirty-six propositions, each trailed by a demonstration and occasionally a scholium (a note). This is not showing off. It is a claim about the world enforced at the level of style: if the universe is a system of exceptionless necessity, then the right way to describe it is the way one describes a triangle — without pleading, moralizing, or awe.

He states the point outright in the preface to Part III (paraphrased): most writers on the emotions denounce or ridicule human nature, as though man were "a kingdom within a kingdom" exempt from the laws of nature; I intend to treat human actions and appetites as if they were lines, planes and solids. That phrase — imperium in imperio, "a state within a state" — needs unpacking on the spot: it names the assumption that there is one enclave in the universe not governed by natural law, namely the human will. Spinoza denies the enclave exists. Your hatred, envy and cowardice have determinate causes exactly as a falling stone does, and therefore are to be explained, not condemned. That single sentence sets the moral temperature of the whole book: he never scolds.

One practical note on reading it: the philosophy usually lives in the scholia and prefaces, not in the demonstrations. The proofs are often thin and sometimes skip steps; in the notes he suddenly loosens up and writes satire, examples, polemic. Experienced readers do not grind through the proof chains — they read the propositions and then read the notes. Get the three famous ones (the Appendix to Part I, the second scholium to II/40, the scholium to III/9) and you have the skeleton.

Substance, attribute, mode: a wave is not in the sea; it is the sea, waving

Three terms carry all of Part I, and they have to be nailed down.

Substance: "that which is in itself and is conceived through itself" — in plain terms, the thing that needs nothing else in order to be explained. Explain a tree and you must mention seed, soil and sun; explain a thought and you must mention brain and history. Substance is where that chain ends, because its existence follows from its own essence (this is what causa sui, "self-caused," means).

Attribute: "what the intellect perceives of substance as constituting its essence" — i.e. one of the faces substance presents to understanding. We have access to exactly two: Thought and Extension (the space-occupying face). Substance itself has infinitely many; the rest are out of our reach.

Mode: "an affection of substance, that which is in another and conceived through another" — a particular state of substance, one crest of it. You, me, this table, the French Revolution: all modes.

The standard mistake is to read these as material, property and product. The right picture is the sea and its waves. A wave is not manufactured by the sea and then placed in it; a wave has no inventory of its own; take the sea away and the wave does not lose its home, it simply never was. A wave is the sea doing a certain thing. Likewise you are not an artifact God made. You are God, happening in the manner called "you." His chain of reasoning runs roughly: two substances cannot share an attribute (nothing would distinguish them) → substance cannot be produced by anything else → substance must be infinite → therefore there is exactly one, and it must possess every attribute. That one substance is what he calls God.

Why does this change how you see things? Because it dissolves the inside/outside frame in a single stroke. On this picture "I" and "the world" are not two things transacting but two stretches of one process; death is not the annihilation of a thing but the ceasing of a pattern — which is why Part V's talk of "the eternity of the mind" is not, as we will see, about a soul going anywhere.

God, i.e. Nature: a God who neither loves you nor hates you

The most famous phrase in the book is the Latin Deus sive Natura — "God, or in other words, Nature." That sive is not a choice between options; it is the "that is to say" of apposition. He is saying these are two names for one thing. The move offended everyone at once: to the churches he had demoted God to rain and rocks; to atheists he had perversely kept the word "God."

What he actually removes is the personality. God has no intellect or will in our sense, makes no choices, changes no mind for anyone's prayer, plays no favorites. Part I proposition 33 puts it bluntly (paraphrased): things could have been produced by God in no other way and in no other order than they have been. This world is not the one selected from a shortlist of possible worlds; it is the only possible world. By Part V he draws the coldest and most liberating corollary (paraphrased): "Strictly speaking, God loves no one and hates no one."

Paired with this is a distinction that must be explained rather than dropped: natura naturans ("nature naturing") versus natura naturata ("nature natured"). The first is nature as producing — substance and its attributes, the eternal laws themselves; the second is nature as produced, the whole population of particular things. The analogy is not sculptor and statue (those are two things) but the rules of chess and every position ever played: the rules do not sit in a separate room from the board, they exist only in the moves — yet they are not identical to any move. God stands to things as rules stand to play.

Once that cut is made, the entire emotional plumbing of religion is severed: there is no one to placate, no bargain to strike in prayer, no justice being held in escrow for you. Einstein's famous 1929 cable, answering whether he believed in God, said exactly this (paraphrased): I believe in Spinoza's God, who reveals himself in the lawful harmony of what exists, not in a God who concerns himself with the fates and doings of mankind. Note the second half — that is where the whole weight sits.

The illusion of purpose: "the will of God" as the sanctuary of ignorance

The Appendix that closes Part I is the most readable and the sharpest stretch of the book, essentially a free-standing essay in psychology. It answers one question: if nature contains no purposes, why does everyone believe it does?

Three steps, each exact. (1) People are born ignorant of the causes of things but perfectly aware of their own appetites. (2) So they interpret everything through "what is it for" — teeth for chewing, grass for the cattle, the sun for light. (3) And if these things were arranged for human use, there must be an arranger, one with tastes like ours — so people build a god in their own temperament and then try to please him with offerings.

Then comes the fatal example. A stone falls from a roof and kills a man. It was God's will, says the believer in purposes. But why did it fall precisely then? Because the wind blew. Why was the man passing precisely then? Because a friend had invited him. Why the invitation? Push far enough and the answer is always: "such was the will of God." Spinoza gave that manoeuvre a name it has never shaken off — asylum ignorantiae, the sanctuary of ignorance: not an explanation but the shutting down of explanation, performed with the confidence of piety.

In passing he disposes of value terms too (paraphrased): whatever conduces to health and worship people call good, the opposite evil; what agrees with their nerves they call beautiful, and what they cannot grasp they call confusion — none of which are properties of things, only ways of imagining. The same forest is a resource to the logger, sacred to the poet, terrifying to the lost. The reason this section still lands is that its real target is not religion but a default setting of the human mind: we feel first, and then back-infer an intention. The instinct that "someone must be behind this," reading today's news, runs on exactly that old circuit.

Parallelism: the mind just is the idea of the body

Descartes had bequeathed modern philosophy its largest mess. Mind is unextended thinking substance, body is extended matter — so how can two utterly unlike things move each other? Descartes's answer was the pineal gland, which Spinoza mocks in the preface to Part V with something close to rudeness (paraphrase: the hypothesis this great philosopher offers is more occult than any occult quality).

His own solution abolishes the question. Part II, proposition 7: "The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things." Mind and body are not two items interacting; they are one item read under two attributes — under Extension it is a body, under Thought it is a mind. Hence proposition 13: the object of the mind is the body — the mind simply is the idea of this body.

Pick the analogy carefully. Not vague talk of "two sides of a coin"; his own example is exact: the circle existing in nature and the idea of that circle are one and the same thing expressed through different attributes. A modern equivalent works better for most readers: one computation can be described as a sequence of logical steps or as a pattern of voltages on a chip — not two things acting on each other, but two ledgers of one process, always line-for-line in step, neither pushing the other.

Two consequences are worth holding onto. First, causation never crosses attributes: the body cannot move the mind and the mind cannot move the body (III/2). Your arm does not rise "because you decided" — bodily states are caused by bodily states and thoughts by thoughts, the two chains running in exact parallel. This pulls the floor out from under the folk mythology of willpower. Second, a consequence often skipped: since all things are modes of one substance, not only humans but all individuals are animate, though in differing degrees (II/13 scholium, paraphrased) — a stone has the stone's exceedingly impoverished "idea." In the seventeenth century this read as an oddity; today it makes Spinoza the most-cited ancestor of panpsychism (the view that mind or proto-mind is pervasive in matter) and of dual-aspect monism.

Free will is an illusion — and "freedom" gets redefined

Spinoza's handling of free will is the most decisive in the history of philosophy. His diagnosis is one sentence (paraphrased): people believe themselves free because they are conscious of their volitions while ignorant of the causes that determine them. His examples are plain enough to sting: the infant believes it freely wants milk, the angry child freely wants revenge, the coward freely chooses flight, and the drunk believes he freely says what he later, sober, wishes he had not. In a letter to a friend — not in the Ethics, and the distinction matters — he pushes the image to its limit: a stone in flight, were it conscious, would be convinced it was flying of its own free will.

But the real contribution is that he does not throw the word "freedom" out along with the illusion. He redefines it. Part I, definition 7: a thing is free when it exists from the necessity of its own nature alone and is determined to act by itself alone; a thing is compelled when it is determined by something else. Note what freedom is being opposed to here: not to being determined, but to being determined from outside.

That single move changes the game. Since freedom now comes in degrees (only God/Nature is fully self-caused), the ethical task is not to win an uncaused choice that does not exist, but to shift the source of your behaviour inward wherever you can: the same act done out of provocation and done out of a clear grasp of the situation may look identical from outside, yet your freedom in the two cases is not remotely the same. It also explains why his moral tone is so unusual. If no one could have done otherwise, blame has no target left; what remains is understanding — and understanding is itself the only thing that changes anything.

Conatus: everything strains to persist — and value gets inverted

Part III proposition 6 is the engine of the book: "Each thing, insofar as it is in itself, strives to persevere in its being." That striving is conatus (Latin: effort, exertion, tendency). Proposition 7 follows with the harder claim: this striving is not a property the thing has; it is the thing's actual essence.

Read that slowly. It is not the sentimental thought that living things want to survive. It says that what makes a thing this thing is that it is a bundle of activity currently maintaining itself. The analogy: a whirlpool in a river. You cannot separate the whirlpool from the flow that sustains it; it does not exist first and then work at persisting — it is the persisting. Stop the water and no whirlpool has died, because there was no whirlpool over and above that. A candle flame is the same case. This is self-organization three hundred years early, and it is why the neuroscientist Antonio Damasio wrote a book called Looking for Spinoza, hooking conatus up to homeostasis in living bodies.

From conatus he draws the boldest inversion in the history of ethics (III/9 scholium, paraphrased): we do not strive for a thing because we judge it good; rather, we judge it good because we strive for it. Desire comes first; value comes after. "Good" is not a reading on some cosmic gauge — it is another way of saying "conducive to the continuation of this particular striving." The sentence topples the Platonic Form of the Good and every "understand the principle, then act" sermon in one stroke: you do not see the good and then want it; you want, and then call it good.

The chemistry of the affects: three primaries, dozens of compounds

This is the most useful and most underrated part of the Ethics. Spinoza treats the emotions not as a tangle but as compounds derivable from three elements:

  • Desire (cupiditas) — conatus as it appears once we are conscious of it;
  • Joy (laetitia) — the passage from a lesser to a greater perfection, i.e. an increase in the power of acting;
  • Sadness (tristitia) — the passage the other way, a decrease in that power.

Everything hangs on the word "passage." Joy is not a state but a derivative — the slope of your capability curve, pointing up. Which explains a daily oddity: who is happier, the person who has a great deal but is static, or the person who has little and is improving? Spinoza's answer is the second, because joy books the change, not the balance.

All other affects are these three plus an idea of their cause. Love = joy + the idea of an external cause; hatred = sadness + the idea of an external cause. Structurally love and hate are mirror images, which is precisely why they flip into each other so easily.

The recipe table (compiled from the "Definitions of the Affects" in Part III). The point: every affect is joy or sadness plus an idea of its cause — change the idea and the affect changes.
AffectRecipeSpinoza's point

That last row is his least commonsensical and most chewable claim: repentance, shame and pity — the "moral" emotions — are all species of sadness, hence reductions in the power of acting, hence not virtues. He is not preaching coldness; he concedes immediately that for most people, who are not led by reason, such feelings are better than nothing (pity at least produces mutual aid). But as an ethical target he wants the side that makes people stronger — understanding, friendship, fortitude — not self-punishment driven by guilt. Essentially every modern argument that shame is a poor fuel for behaviour change has its prototype here, three centuries early.

Active and passive: the way out is not to sit on the feeling but to see it

The hinge of Parts III–V is a single distinction: action versus passion. The criterion is clean. When something can be explained fully from your own nature, you are its adequate cause and you are acting; when it can only be partly explained by you, the rest requiring external causes, you are merely a partial cause and you are undergoing. An affect arising from an inadequate idea — an idea missing its causal lineage, a stump of an idea — is a passion, and life ruled by passions is what Part IV's title calls "human bondage."

The exit is Part V proposition 3 (paraphrased): an affect that is a passion ceases to be a passion as soon as we form a clear and distinct idea of it. And proposition 6: insofar as the mind understands things as necessary, it has more power over the affects.

This is easily misread as "just look on the bright side," so the mechanism is worth spelling out. A passion draws its force from the confused idea it is fastened to — "he did it to get at me," "this should never have happened," "it's his fault." When you fill in that causal story (what his situation was, where your own sore spot came from, how this chain necessarily produced this moment), the energy is not repressed; it is re-anchored: it no longer hangs on an external malefactor but on a process you understand. The first leaves you passive, the second makes you active — because the grasp of necessity is generated from inside your own mind, and of that you are the adequate cause.

And here is the book's most counterintuitive, most-skipped proposition, Part IV/7: "An affect can neither be restrained nor removed except by a contrary and stronger affect." Notice what that overturns: rational knowledge on its own cannot defeat a passion. Knowing that staying up late harms you does not put you to bed. So Spinoza's programme is not "use reason to suppress feeling"; it is to make understanding itself an affect — the joy arising from clear knowledge (what he finally calls the intellectual love of God) must be strong enough to displace fear and craving on their own emotional turf. This is exactly why he criticizes the Stoics by name, holding that they overestimated the will's command over the emotions. His path is narrower and more honest: you cannot order yourself not to be afraid; you can only cultivate something stronger to occupy that place.

The three kinds of knowledge and "under the aspect of eternity"

The second scholium to II/40 sorts human knowing into three grades, with an example so neat it is worth memorizing: if 1 is to 2 as 3 is to what?

The three kinds of knowledge (II/40, scholium 2). The example — the merchant's "rule of three" — is Spinoza's own. The first kind is the sole source of error; the other two are necessarily true.

② ReasonInference from "common notions" — properties shared by all things, and so not distortable by local experienceProving the fourth term is 6 from the theory of proportionsNecessarily true, but takes a route
③ Intuitive knowledgeFrom an adequate idea of God's attributes straight to the essence of a particular thingLooking at 1, 2, 3 and seeing 6 at onceNecessarily true, and immediate

The third kind is neither mystical experience nor lucky guessing. It is the grasp of someone who has seen the structure and therefore no longer needs to compute — the old player who glances at the board and knows the position is dead, the good clinician who registers that something is wrong on walking into the room. The immediacy is compressed understanding, not a substitute for it. Spinoza's ambition is to apply that grasp to particular things: to see, in this flower or this person, how it follows necessarily from the laws of the whole of nature. He calls that vantage sub specie aeternitatis, "under the aspect of eternity" — which does not mean taking the very long view (that is still time) but viewing things through timeless necessity: not asking when something happened, only why it had to be so.

From this come Part V's two hardest conclusions. First, the eternity of the mind (V/23, paraphrased): the human mind cannot be wholly destroyed with the body; something of it remains that is eternal. Say at once what this is not: it is not the immortality of a soul, because memory and imagination perish with the body (V/21) — which means "you," the person with a biography and recollections, does not persist. What remains is the idea of this body's essence in God, considered as an eternal truth — closer to saying: that there was such a way of existing as you is, at the level of necessity, forever the case. Second, the intellectual love of God (amor Dei intellectualis): understanding things by the third kind of knowledge is accompanied by a joy whose cause is conceived as God/Nature itself; and this love, he goes on, is one and the same as the infinite love with which God loves himself.

The landing point is V/42 (paraphrased): "Blessedness is not the reward of virtue but virtue itself; nor do we enjoy it because we restrain our lusts — on the contrary, because we enjoy it we are able to restrain them." The order is reversed: not suffer now, collect later, but understanding is itself the payment, and once it is in hand restraint no longer requires willpower. The book's final sentence concedes how hard that is: "But all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare."

The distilled spine

Straightened out, the proof-chain is a single line running from metaphysics to a way of living, each step resting on the last:

  • Part I (Of God): substance is self-caused and infinite → two substances cannot share an attribute → therefore exactly one substance, God = Nature → all things are its modes, following necessarily, no alternative possible → Appendix: purpose is a human projection, "God's will" is the sanctuary of ignorance, and good/evil/beauty are only modes of imagining.
  • Part II (Of the Mind): Thought and Extension are two attributes of one substance → the order of ideas is the order of things, the mind is the idea of the body → causation does not cross attributes, so nothing "commands" anything → knowledge comes in three grades, and only the first can be false.
  • Part III (Of the Affects): the essence of each thing is conatus → striving comes first, value second → affects are compounds of desire, joy and sadness → the decisive split: what follows from adequate ideas is action; what follows from inadequate ideas is passion.
  • Part IV (Human Bondage): we are small parts of nature, always outmatched by external causes, so passions are inevitable → good and evil are relative to what sustains us → an affect yields only to a stronger affect, never to a bare command of reason → people led by reason are maximally useful to one another, so "man is a God to man" (a deliberate rewrite of Hobbes's "man is a wolf to man") → "a free man thinks of nothing less than of death, and his wisdom is a meditation on life, not on death."
  • Part V (The Power of the Intellect): form a clear idea of an affect and it stops being a passion → understand things as necessary and their grip loosens → the third kind of knowledge → self-understanding under the aspect of eternity → something in the mind is eternal → the intellectual love of God = blessedness = virtue itself.

What does it actually establish? Not that there is a God, and not that there isn't, but a route that merges the two and still has an ethics left over: once purpose, reward, punishment and free will are gone, the question "how should I live" does not vanish — it acquires, for the first time, a non-arbitrary answer. Whatever increases your power of acting is good; and the ultimate means of increasing it is understanding. Spinoza's whole wager rides on one proposition: that understanding is itself an emotional force, and the strongest one available. Whether that wager comes off is what the last three centuries have been arguing about.

Misreadings & honest criticism

  • Misreading 1: pantheism means "everything is sacred, isn't nature lovely." There is not a gram of warmth in Spinoza's God: no purposes, no favourites, no answered prayers, no universe running on your behalf. Reading him as a lyricist of nature-worship discards the harder half. The correct sensation is double — total disenchantment and total belonging at once: you are not an orphan dropped into the cosmos; you are a stretch of it.
  • Misreading 2: determinism means fatalism means effort is pointless. Fatalism says the outcome is fixed whatever you do. Spinoza says what you do is a link in the causal chain, and is in fact your essence. What gets abolished is blame and the "it could have been otherwise" of regret, not action. This is the practical reason he keeps being invoked, from Schopenhauer to contemporary free-will sceptics: remove moral resentment and what is left is understanding, plus the design of institutions.
  • Misreading 3: he is a Stoic teaching emotional suppression. He criticizes the Stoics by name in the preface to Part V for overrating the will's authority over the affects, and his own IV/7 could not be plainer: an affect is defeated only by a stronger affect. The goal is not to feel less but to swap passive affects for active ones — not cutting the engine, changing the fuel.
  • Criticism 1: is the geometrical form rigour, or a shell? This is the mainstream technical complaint. The key inferences of Part I — especially the run from "no two substances share an attribute" to "there is only one substance" — have been charged, from Leibniz onward, with assuming their conclusion or leaning on unstated premises. The most influential contemporary reading (Della Rocca's) holds that what actually drives the book is a principle never listed among the axioms: the principle of sufficient reason, that for everything that exists or fails to exist there is a reason why. Push it without exception and monism, determinism and parallelism all fall out. In other words the force of the Ethics comes not from its geometry but from one commitment carried through to the end: that nothing is brute. Which also means the honest options are to accept the principle and take the consequences, or to refuse it at the source.
  • Criticism 2: does Part V collapse or culminate? This is the most famous fault line in Spinoza scholarship. The analytic commentator Jonathan Bennett wrote in his classic study that the later stretch of Part V is an unmitigated disaster, "rubbish which causes others to write rubbish" (paraphrased) — on the grounds that the eternity of the mind neither follows from the first four parts nor is coherently stated. Defenders (Nadler, Garrett) offer a thinned-out reading: eternity involves no personal survival at all, only the claim that the truth about this individual's essence is an eternal truth, making the "salvation" impersonal and cognitive. The fair verdict: Part V's ambition plainly outruns its argumentative supply; it reads partly as philosophy and partly as a report of a religious experience.
  • Criticism 3: the individual is swallowed by substance (Hegel's "acosmism"). Hegel's formulation is neat: Spinozism is not atheism but acosmism — what gets negated is not God but the reality of the world and of individuals. If you and I are merely modes of the one substance, is there any room left for genuine individuality, for real becoming and change? Hegel said no: Spinoza's substance is a motionless abyss, lacking the negativity that would let it develop. The objection still bites, and it is how German Idealism digested Spinoza rather than refuting him.
  • Criticism 4: this ethics only works for a few. The road to freedom runs through adequate ideas up to the third kind of knowledge — which takes leisure, education and uncommon intellectual power. Spinoza effectively conceded the point: in the Theological-Political Treatise he leaves a second road for the majority who will not reach blessedness by reason — obedience and piety, which is where he locates religion's practical value. But it leaves his system with an elitist tint that is hard to scrub off: salvation distributed by cognitive capacity.
  • A technical note. The Ethics was written in Latin and English translations differ on load-bearing words: affectus appears as "affect," "emotion" or "passion"; laetitia/tristitia as "joy/sadness" or "pleasure/pain"; conatus is usually left in Latin. The Curley translation is the current scholarly default. The main thing while reading is not to let the word "God" carry you off — substitute "the full necessity of nature" each time and the sentences come out straight.

The Spinoza outside this book

Read only the Ethics and you get the pure metaphysician, missing the half of him that shaped actual politics. The one book he published in his lifetime anonymously — and which caused an uproar — was the Theological-Political Treatise (1670), and it did three things that could have cost him his life.

First, he invented historical-critical Bible scholarship. Scripture, he argued, must be read as any ancient document is read — through its language, authorship, date and the circumstances of its audience — rather than by assuming in advance that every line is true. On that basis he argued that Moses did not write the Pentateuch (passages remarking that no one knows the location of Moses's grave "to this day" plainly come from a later hand) and suggested the surviving text was shaped by later compilers. This is generally credited as the starting point of modern biblical criticism.

Second, he demoted prophecy and miracles. What distinguished the prophets was not intellect but imagination: they received and conveyed moral teaching vividly, while knowing no more about nature than their contemporaries. Miracles, in his system, are self-contradictory: the laws of nature are the expression of God's own nature, so a miracle breaking them would be God contravening himself. People believe in miracles only because they do not know the causes — the sanctuary of ignorance again. He then compressed religion's core to a minimum: justice and charity; everything else is history and ritual, and settles nothing about truth.

Third, and most consequentially, he wrote the earliest systematic modern defence of freedom of thought. The treatise's subtitle states the thesis outright: freedom to philosophize can be granted without damage to piety or public peace, and abolishing it would in fact destroy both. The argument is entirely secular: minds cannot actually be coerced, so repression manufactures hypocrisy and conspiracy; and the purpose of the state was never to turn people into beasts or machines but to let them use their reason in safety — "the true aim of government is liberty" (paraphrased). Democracy he called the most natural form of government, being closest to the equal natural power people start with. The unfinished Political Treatise is colder still: political theory must begin from people as they actually are rather than as we wish them to be, and institutions should be built so that they keep working even when those in power are not admirable. That is nearly a sketch of modern constitutional thinking.

One small text deserves mention too: the early, unfinished Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect, which opens with a rare piece of self-disclosure (paraphrased): he found that the wealth, honour and sensual pleasure people chase are empty, and that chasing them is itself a source of suffering, so he set out to find a "true good" which, once found and possessed, would yield continuous and supreme joy. The emotional origin of all those glacial propositions lies in that paragraph: it is a man asking what is worth staking a life on — and the answer he gives is the thing the later book proves geometrically.

Ten sentences

① "God, that is to say, Nature." There is one substance; it is neither outside the world nor prior to it — it is the necessity by which the world runs. You are not a product it made. You are one of its crests: a wave is not in the sea, a wave is the sea, waving.

② This God has no will, makes no choices, answers no prayers: "strictly speaking, God loves no one and hates no one" (paraphrased). Things could have been produced in no other way and no other order — this is not the world selected from a shortlist; it is the only possible one.

③ Nature has no purposes, yet people see purpose everywhere, because we are born ignorant of causes and vividly aware of our appetites, so we project "what for" onto everything and then invent a god with our own tastes. The final "such is God's will" he named the sanctuary of ignorance — not an explanation but the shutting down of explanation.

④ Good, evil, beauty and ugliness are not properties of the world but ways of imagining it: what suits me I call good; what I cannot grasp I call chaos.

⑤ Mind and body are not two things pushing each other but one thing read under two attributes: "the order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things." The mind just is the idea of this body — like one computation described as logic and as voltage, two ledgers in permanent lockstep, neither driving the other.

⑥ People think themselves free because they are conscious of their desires and ignorant of the causes of them — the infant, the drunk, the furious child alike. Yet "freedom" survives, redefined: its opposite is not being determined but being determined from outside; free is what acts from its own nature alone.

⑦ Everything strives to persevere in its being, and that striving is its actual essence (conatus). Like a whirlpool: it does not exist and then work at persisting, it is the persisting. From which value is inverted — we do not pursue a thing because we judge it good; we judge it good because we pursue it.

⑧ There are only three primary affects: desire, joy (an increase in the power of acting) and sadness (a decrease); every other feeling is these plus an idea of a cause. Joy books the slope, not the balance — which is why someone improving is happier than someone merely well-supplied. And repentance and shame, on his ledger, are species of sadness: weakness, not virtue.

⑨ "An affect can be restrained or removed only by a contrary and stronger affect." So the exit is not reason suppressing feeling but understanding becoming a stronger feeling. Its companion proposition: a passion ceases to be a passion once we form a clear and distinct idea of it (paraphrased) — the energy is not destroyed, it is re-anchored from "someone is doing this to me" onto a process understood.

⑩ "Blessedness is not the reward of virtue but virtue itself; nor do we enjoy it because we restrain our lusts — on the contrary, because we enjoy it we are able to restrain them" (paraphrased). Salvation is not settled after death; it is paid out where understanding happens. And the book's last line concedes the odds for everyone: "All things excellent are as difficult as they are rare."
