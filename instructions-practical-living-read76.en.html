DEEP READING · READ 76

Instructions for Practical Living

Chuanxilu · Wang Yangming (Wang Shouren) · Ming China, published from 1518

中文 →

In one sentence

A young man follows Zhu Xi's method and spends seven days "investigating" the bamboo outside his window until he falls ill; thirty years old and then some, exiled to a malarial post in the mountains of Guizhou, he wakes one night having worked out the thing that will occupy the rest of his life: the principle of how to live is not sitting out there in things waiting to be researched — it is in the judgment your own heart-mind makes, right now, about what is in front of you; and what you lack is never more knowledge, it is doing without discount the small thing you already know. This book is the twenty years of answers he gave when students kept asking him: fine, but concretely, how?

Where it sits

Wang Shouren (1472–1529), known as Wang Yangming, is one of the very few figures in Chinese history credited with all three classical achievements at once — moral character, public deeds, and lasting doctrine. He was beaten with forty strokes at court, exiled, and hunted by a eunuch's agents; he also pacified a long-standing bandit insurgency in Jiangxi and captured the rebel Prince of Ning in thirty-five days; and he turned the Zhu Xi orthodoxy that had governed Chinese thought for three centuries — Neo-Confucianism in its official form — from investigating things outward to extending innate knowing inward, founding the school of mind (xinxue) that would later sweep East Asia, Japan included. The Chuanxilu is not a treatise he sat down to write; it is student notes of his conversations plus a handful of long letters. The title comes from the Analects — Zengzi's daily self-examination, "what my teacher transmitted, have I practised it?" — so the book is fragmentary and conversational, and, like the Analects, warm. Part One is the early record kept by Xu Ai and others; Part Two collects the letters (his most systematic writing is here); Part Three runs to his last years, including the famous night at Tianquan Bridge.

The core claims

  • Mind is principle (xin ji li): moral principle is not in things outside the mind. The principle of filial devotion is not located in your father, nor loyalty in your ruler — it lives only in what an unobstructed heart-mind demands of you when you face them. Seeking principle outwards is the wrong direction from the start.
  • Knowing and acting are one (zhixing heyi): not two things to be coordinated, but two faces of one thing. "To know and not to act is simply not to know." You say you know you should exercise and you don't — that is not weak will, it is that you never really knew.
  • Extend innate knowing (zhi liangzhi): everyone carries a faculty that judges right from wrong on the spot; the entire discipline is pushing it all the way out and never letting self-interest cut the wire. From which comes the most egalitarian and most brutal sentence in the book: the sage differs from you in quantity, not in purity.

The core ideas, one at a time

What he is reacting against: Zhu Xi, and those seven days of bamboo

Without Zhu Xi you cannot hear the urgency in Wang. Zhu Xi (1130–1200) read the Great Learning's phrase gewu zhizhi as "approach a thing and exhaust its principle." Every thing in the world has its own principle — why it is so, and how one ought to act regarding it. Investigate one today, another tomorrow, and after long accumulation understanding breaks through all at once. In practice this means: studying the principle of things is step one of self-cultivation; only once you understand may you act.

The young Wang believed this literally. He tells the story himself in Part Three (paraphrased): he and a friend named Qian agreed that becoming a sage meant investigating all things under heaven, so they might as well start with what was in front of them — he pointed at the bamboo in the courtyard and told his friend to investigate it. His friend stared and strained for three days and fell ill. Wang took over, lasted seven, and fell ill too. Their conclusion: sagehood is beyond us; we simply lack the strength.

This is not a comic anecdote; it is a serious falsification. Suppose you really did work out the principle of how bamboo grows. What does it have to do with whether you should be good to your parents, or tell your boss an unwelcome truth? The joint in Zhu Xi's system where accumulated knowledge is supposed to "break through" into moral understanding is exactly its vaguest joint. Sixteen years later, having petitioned on behalf of censors imprisoned by the eunuch Liu Jin, Wang was beaten and demoted to postmaster of Longchang in Guizhou — no shared language, malarial air, and servants falling sick one after another so that he ended up nursing them. He made himself a stone coffin, sat in it day and night, and put the question: "If a sage were here, what would he do?" One night, according to the chronological biography, he understood, leapt up and woke his servants shouting: "The way of the sage is complete in my own nature; seeking principle out in things was a mistake."

Note the conditions under which this happened. A man stripped of books, rank, prospects and safety has nothing left but his own mind. The school of mind was never a scholarly preference; it was the only thing a man at the bottom still had in his hands.

Mind is principle: the duty is not located in your father

"Mind is principle" invites the misreading "whatever I feel is right," so look at the actual exchange. His student Xu Ai (his brother-in-law and first disciple, who died young) objects: if we seek the highest good only within, surely we will get the details of the world's affairs wrong — serving one's father calls for warming his bed in winter and cooling it in summer, and don't these have to be studied?

Wang answers in two moves. First: "Is there, under heaven, any affair outside the mind, any principle outside the mind?" Go and look for "the principle of filial devotion" in your father. When he dies, does the principle die with him? Obviously not. So it was never in him; it is in you. Second, he closes the gap Xu Ai actually fears: of course the particulars must be learned — but they are branches growing out of that one devoted heart, not a list of rules you can memorise apart from it. If the mind is free of selfish desire and sincerely devoted, "then in winter it will naturally think of the parent's cold, and of itself go find out how to keep him warm" (paraphrased). Give it a root and the branches follow; learn only branches and you have stuck plastic flowers in the soil.

What this changes is the nature of moral knowledge. For Zhu Xi, principle is an objective norm sitting in things, waiting to be found and learned. For Wang, principle is what the mind generates in a concrete situation, so all you must do is stop obstructing it. The first is cultivation by addition; the second is cultivation by subtraction. Hence his rereading of gewu: "To rectify (ge) is to correct what is not correct and return it to correctness." Investigating things is not researching things; it is straightening a crooked intention. And "thing" is not bamboo or stone: "where the intention rests, that is a thing" — your intention rests on caring for a parent, and caring for a parent is a thing. He has moved the entire battlefield from the outer world to the instant a thought forms.

Knowing and acting are one — not "practise what you preach"

This is the most misread phrase he wrote. Almost everyone today takes it to mean "connect theory to practice." That is a motivational platitude, and it is precisely the frame Wang is dismantling: it assumes knowing and acting are two things that merely need to be kept in step. Wang's claim is harder: they were one thing all along, and seeing them as two is itself the pathology.

His formulation: "Knowing is the beginning of acting; acting is the completion of knowing." "There has never been anyone who knew and did not act. To know and not to act is simply not to know." To show what knowing actually looks like, he takes an example from the Great Learning, and it is the finest stroke in the book: "as when one loves a beautiful sight, as when one hates a bad smell." Seeing the beautiful thing is knowing; liking it is acting — but when did you ever see it first and then set up a separate intention to like it? The liking is already there in the seeing. Smelling the stench is knowing, recoiling is acting — and a man with a blocked nose neither smells nor recoils. There is no gap between them into which you could insert a "and then."

Apply that model to morality and it stings: you say you know late nights are wrecking you, that you should be more patient with your family, that this project should have been killed months ago — if you have not acted, Wang's diagnosis is not "he knows but can't do it," it is that you never knew; what you have is a sentence you can recite, like a man who has never smelled anything memorising that stenches are unpleasant. The line between real and counterfeit knowledge is not how much you can articulate but whether it is already moving you. That demolishes the modern consolation of "knowing so many truths and still living badly": there is no chasm between knowing and doing — the knowing you were holding was hollow.

There is a more radical layer in Part Three: "In teaching the unity of knowing and acting, I want people to understand that the moment a thought is set in motion, that is already acting." An evil thought is already a deed; you need not wait for the hand to move. Severe — but it explains why he stakes everything on the level of thought: if action begins in intention, the real battlefield is not remorse afterwards but the instant the thought surfaces. He also explained his motive (paraphrased): some people blunder into action without a shred of reflection, so one must say "know"; others hover in endless deliberation and never land, so one must say "act." The doctrine is a prescription for two diseases, not a metaphysical thesis.

Liangzhi: that bit of innate knowing is your own yardstick

"Extend innate knowing" is the formula he arrived at only around fifty. He said it came to him "out of a hundred deaths and a thousand hardships" (paraphrased) — it was not deduced, it was survived.

Liangzhi is borrowed from Mencius: "what one knows without deliberating." In Wang's hands it becomes concrete: liangzhi is the judgment in you that knows right from wrong on the spot, everywhere, always. "That bit of innate knowing is your own yardstick. Wherever your intention settles, it knows the right as right and the wrong as wrong; you cannot deceive it in the least" (Part Three). The evidence is ordinary: you say something cruel, and before you have assembled a single justification something in you has already flinched. That flinch is liangzhi; by the time you have three reasons why you were not wrong, the obscuring has begun. Wang goes further — even a thief knows he should not steal; call him one to his face and he still colours. Innate knowing does not vanish with a person's degradation; it only gets sat on.

Everything turns on the verb zhi, to extend: from the Great Learning's "extending knowledge," it means to push all the way out and carry through — not having liangzhi but bringing it to bear on every single thing without discount. So the practice is two motions fused: hear it; then act on it, above all when acting on it costs you. The hard part was never the first. You already knew the number should not be reported that way, that the sentence should not have been said — you simply had a hundred reasons to route around it. Wang's whole method is a technology for not letting you route around it.

It has one great practical advantage: it converts moral judgment from consulting a rulebook into judging on the spot. The Zhu Xi route fails on a situation the classics never described; liangzhi needs no precedent. It gives you no knowledge of the world — it has no opinion on whether you need bypass surgery — it only tells you, now, whether this particular intention is straight. Which is also exactly where it can be abused.

The gold analogy: you differ from the sage in weight, not in purity

If liangzhi is already complete in everyone, what does a sage have that you lack? Wang's answer is one of the most consequential images in Confucian thought: what makes a sage a sage is that his mind is pure heavenly principle with no admixture of selfish desire — just as what makes gold pure gold is its fineness, not how much of it there is. By capacity, Yao and Shun are gold of ten thousand yi (an old unit, twenty taels), King Wen and Confucius nine thousand, Bo Yi and Yi Yin four or five thousand. Wildly different weights — but if the fineness is the same, all are "equally called pure gold." So an ordinary person willing to do the work, whose mind becomes pure principle, "may also become a sage": an ounce of pure gold is incomparably lighter than ten thousand yi and not one degree less pure.

The analogy swaps out the entire racetrack. On weight, the gap between you and Confucius is hopeless. Wang says that was never the race: sagehood competes on fineness, and fineness is entirely within your control. He then turns on the scholars of his day: instead of working on fineness they compete on weight, spending a life on "knowledge must be broad, talent must be great" — which is adding copper and lead to the gold. The heavier it gets, the baser it gets, until there is no gold left at all.

It reads uncomfortably now. Our default unit for measuring a person is still weight — titles, résumé, assets, followers — and every one of those can be inflated by adulteration. Fineness is the only thing that cannot: it does not ask how large your achievements were, only whether, in each small matter, you discounted what your own knowing told you.

Nothing outside the mind: the flowering tree in the cliff

Here is the passage most often used to ridicule the school of mind. Walking in the hills, a friend pointed to a flowering tree growing out of a cliff and asked: "You say there is nothing outside the mind. This tree blooms and drops its flowers of itself, deep in the mountains. What has it to do with my mind?" Wang replied: "Before you looked at this flower, it and your mind were alike in stillness. When you came to look at it, its colours became clear all at once. So you know this flower is not outside your mind."

Modern readers have often filed this as subjective idealism — does the flower stop existing when unobserved? But Wang does not say it does not exist; he says it "returns to stillness," unlit. Read it against his own definition: "where the intention rests, that is a thing" — his "thing" is not a physical object but an affair that has entered someone's intention (this is the reading developed by the contemporary scholar Chen Lai). Unlooked at, the flower's physical existence is untouched; it simply has no meaning for you. Looked at, it "becomes clear all at once" and enters your world as an affair.

In modern terms: this is a claim about the world of meaning, not the world of matter. How many mountains and stars the universe contains has nothing to do with you; but your world — the one you feel glad about, guilty about, responsible for — consists precisely of what your mind has lit up. That is also the licence for the whole enterprise: if your world is lit by your mind, then cleaning that mind is the most fundamental engineering project available, not an escape from reality. Whether the passage is finally an intentional or an ontological claim remains unsettled — this is the school's most fragile joint.

Polishing on affairs: paperwork and lawsuits are the real learning

The easy misunderstanding at this point is that all this means sitting still with your eyes shut. Wang did teach quiet-sitting early on and then stopped, because his students "began to love stillness and hate activity, and drifted into withered dryness" (paraphrased — dry as dead wood, no life in it). What he said instead: "A person must be polished on affairs; only then does he stand firm, only then is he settled in stillness and settled in motion alike." Composure grown only in quiet scatters the moment something happens.

One exchange in the book could serve as the motto of every office worker alive. A minor official told him: your teaching is admirable, but my ledgers and court cases leave me no time to study it. Wang's reply (paraphrased): "When did I ever tell you to abandon your ledgers and lawsuits and study in a vacuum? Since you have official business, learn from the official business — that is genuine investigation of things." He then made it granular: hearing a case, do not grow angry because the man's manner is rude; do not soften because his words are clever; do not punish him harder because you dislike the person who recommended him; do not bend the judgment because he begs; do not decide carelessly because you are busy. Each of those "do nots" is a private impulse only you can see; examining and mastering them there is the actual work. "In the midst of ledgers and lawsuits there is nothing but real learning; to study apart from affairs is to grasp at emptiness."

This closes off the exit marked "I'll get serious about this when things calm down." Your job is not the obstacle to the practice; it is the practice, because favouritism, pique, corner-cutting and deference to power only surface inside real work — and those thoughts are what the practice works on. It also explains the man: he could suppress a rebellion, take a prince on Lake Poyang, and lecture on philosophy in camp, not because he knew more military theory than others but because in his framework fighting and teaching were never two different activities.

One body with all things: why this is not a selfish inwardness

Does putting all the work on your own mind end in self-absorption? In the long letter in Part Two known as "pulling up the root and stopping up the source," Wang answers: the sage's mind takes heaven, earth and the ten thousand things as one body. Every person's mind is originally no different from a sage's; it is severed by the partition of self and clouded by desire, so what was one gets cut into you and me. That is why, seeing a child about to fall into a well, a person is seized with alarm before any calculation — in that instant your mind and the child were never separate. Extended fully, innate knowing necessarily reaches outward to others rather than curling back on itself.

From that position he delivers his heaviest verdict on the scholarship of his time (paraphrased): breadth of memorised learning serves only to feed his arrogance; abundance of knowledge serves only to execute his malice; wide erudition serves only to license his sophistry; richness of style serves only to ornament his hypocrisy. Once learning is decoupled from fineness, it is merely weaponry issued to self-interest.

The Four Sentences: the argument he never settled

In the autumn of 1527, ordered south on campaign, Wang spent his last evening at home on Tianquan Bridge in Shaoxing, where his two senior students Qian Dehong and Wang Ji fell to arguing over his four-line teaching:

Schematic: a descent from a still mind, through the fork a thought creates, to the knowing that catches it and the work that straightens it — and the work, done long enough, polishes the mind back to stillness.

The fight was over line one. Wang Ji argued that if the mind in itself is beyond good and evil, then the intention, the knowing and the things arising from it must be too (the "four negations"); the four lines are only a device for beginners. Qian Dehong said no: people are clouded by ingrained habit, and without the concrete work of doing good and removing evil the whole thing evaporates. Deadlocked, they took it to their teacher.

His ruling is a model of proportion (paraphrased): "The two views complement each other; do not hold to one side. I have always received people in these two ways. A person of sharp endowment enters directly from the source; the rest have habitual minds, and their original mind is obscured, so for now teach them to do good and remove evil in their intentions — when the work has matured and the dross is gone, the original mind will be fully clear too." Sharp endowments exist and are almost never in the room; everyone else does the plodding work on their thoughts, and the clarity comes.

How should the grating first line be read? Not "there is no good or evil," but that the mind, before it moves, holds no prejudice or preference — a mirror with no stain on it, which shows beauty as beauty and ugliness as ugliness because it carries neither of its own. Wang glosses it himself elsewhere: what is beyond good and evil is principle at rest; good and evil come with the stirring of vital force. A mirror already tinted distorts whatever it reflects; the mind in itself must be "beyond good and evil" precisely so that its judgments are not contaminated by its own positions. But the ambiguity of that line seeded the school's later split.

The argument, condensed

The book has no structure, but its reasoning is a forced chain:

  • It starts with a failure: follow "approach a thing and exhaust its principle" and you get seven days of bamboo and an illness — the principle of things does not connect to how one should live.
  • The turn: principle is in the mind. So gewu must be reread — to rectify, not to research; and "thing" means an affair your intention rests on.
  • The test: everyone claims their heart is right, so how do you separate real from counterfeit? By action — no movement means what you held was an empty sentence. The unity of knowing and acting is thus not exhortation but a gauge of genuine knowledge.
  • The operation: what do you consult? Liangzhi; and the whole discipline is in the verb — extend it into every affair, undiscounted.
  • The ambition, placed: the gold analogy — you differ in weight, not purity, and purity is available to anyone; and one body with all things — extended fully, innate knowing runs outward, so this is not self-admiration.
  • The safeguard: do it on affairs. Not in quiet-sitting, not in books.
  • Finally, the self-limitation: at Tianquan Bridge he knew the "four negations" was the more beautiful doctrine and still insisted most people take the plodding road.

In one line: the book establishes that the resources for sagehood are already complete in everyone and that the practice must be cashed out on the spot; and its evidence is not metaphysical deduction but a plain psychological fact — if you really knew, you would already have moved.

Misreadings & honest criticism

The four standard misreadings

  • "Unity of knowing and acting" as "practise what you preach." That still treats them as two things merely kept in step. Wang is not diagnosing weak execution; he is saying your knowledge was counterfeit. The misreading licenses "I know but I can't" — the one position the original text refuses to grant.
  • "Extend innate knowing" as "follow your feelings." The dangerous one. Liangzhi is the voice that makes you uncomfortable, not the one that makes you comfortable, and the whole weight of "extend" is acting on it when it costs you. Equate it with intuition and first impulse and you have crowned self-interest as heavenly principle — exactly the error of the late-Ming epigones.
  • "Mind is principle" as "I decide what's true." His mind is not individual preference but the original mind common to everyone once selfishness is subtracted; he harps on self-examination and self-mastery precisely because the actual mind is obscured most of the time.
  • The school of mind as meditation. He taught quiet-sitting and personally stopped it; "polished on affairs" is the corrective, and retreat-and-introspection is the very "withered dryness" he diagnosed.

The strongest criticisms, stated honestly

  • From Zhu Xi loyalists: Confucian on the outside, Chan Buddhist underneath. The Ming scholar Chen Jian wrote a whole treatise arguing this, and it is not unreasonable: talk of the original substance, of immediacy, of not seeking outside, structurally resembles Chan's pointing directly at mind (compare read70 on the Platform Sutra). Wang's defence: Buddhism leaves the world and severs human relations, while liangzhi must land on serving parents, serving the state, governing people — the difference is not in the method but in where the method is required to land. Whether that line holds has been argued for four centuries.
  • The first of the four sentences was a landmine. Contemporaries asked whether "beyond good and evil" contradicts Mencius on the goodness of human nature, or slides toward Gaozi's view that nature is neither good nor bad. Wang's mirror analogy patches it — but the line escaped his control immediately: Wang Ji pushed the "four negations" to the point of dissolving the work of doing good and removing evil, landing on exactly the side his teacher had warned against that night.
  • The heaviest charge is consequential: the vaporous talk of the late-Ming followers is blamed for a share of the dynasty's fall. Liu Zongzhou's verdict is precise (paraphrased): among those who now speak of innate knowing, the reckless mix in their own appetites, so that everything they feel counts as "good knowing"; the fastidious dissolve it into mysticism, and so destroy the very thing they praise. Gu Yanwu went further in his Record of Daily Knowledge (paraphrased): empty talk of illuminating the mind and seeing one's nature replaced the practical learning of governing oneself and others — and the result was a country in ruins and the ancestral altars in rubble. The attribution is overdrawn (the Ming fell to fiscal collapse, climate, war and factional politics, not to a philosophy), but the underlying problem is real: a doctrine that grounds authority in inner self-certification has no institutional defence against being used for self-authorisation. All Wang offers is polishing on affairs and self-examination — personal discipline, nothing structural.
  • An epistemic blank. Liangzhi adjudicates right and wrong, not true and false: it will not tell you how to treat the disease or build the bridge. Elevating moral knowing over knowledge from seeing and hearing is internally consistent, but it left this tradition without much internal motive to pursue objective knowledge (some blame that for its missing an empirical-science turn — an attribution that is itself contested).
  • His own practice is not above dispute. His public deeds include large-scale "bandit suppression" in Jiangxi and Guangxi, a substantial part of it against peasants and non-Han peoples driven to revolt by taxation and the native-chieftain system; he used deception, feigned surrender and fire attacks without hesitation. A man who took "one body with all things" to its limit was simultaneously a highly effective suppressor of rebellion — not a contradiction to pass over lightly.

Ten sentences

1. The book begins with an honest failure: following "approach a thing and exhaust its principle," he investigated the courtyard bamboo for seven days and collapsed — the principle of things does not connect to how one should live. Exiled to Longchang, he worked it out in a stone coffin: "The way of the sage is complete in my own nature; seeking principle out in things was a mistake."

2. Mind is principle: the duty of filial devotion is not located in your father — it does not die when he does. Cultivation is therefore not adding knowledge but subtracting what obscures.

3. Hence gewu is reread: "to rectify is to correct what is not correct," and "where the intention rests, that is a thing" — the battlefield moves from the outer world to the instant a thought forms.

4. The unity of knowing and acting is not "practise what you preach": "to know and not to act is simply not to know." As one loves a beautiful sight, as one hates a bad smell — the liking is already in the seeing, with no room for an "and then." You didn't act; that isn't weak will, it's that you never knew.

5. Harder still: "the moment a thought is set in motion, that is already acting." So the work is not remorse afterwards but the instant the thought surfaces.

6. Liangzhi is the judgment that knows right from wrong on the spot and cannot be deceived — "your own yardstick." Even a thief has it; it is sat on, never lost. The difficulty is not hearing it but obeying it when obeying costs you. That verb, extend, is the entire practice.

7. The gold analogy: you differ from the sage in weight, not in fineness. Yao and Shun at ten thousand yi, Confucius at nine thousand, and an ounce of pure gold not one degree less pure. Titles, résumés and assets are all weight — and all can be inflated by adulteration. Fineness cannot.

8. "Polished on affairs" closes the exit marked "when things calm down": do not grow angry because his manner is rude, do not bend because he begs, do not decide carelessly because you are busy — each of those private impulses is visible only to you, and that is where the real investigation of things happens.

9. The four sentences: the mind in itself beyond good and evil; intention stirring into good and evil; liangzhi knowing which is which; doing good and removing evil as the practice. On the bridge he knew the more elegant reading was available and still sent most people down the plodding road — he understood that his doctrine was most easily hijacked by the clever.

10. And the fear was borne out after his death: "the reckless mix in their own appetites, so that everything they feel counts as good knowing" (Liu Zongzhou, paraphrased) — mistaking emotion for innate knowing is this tradition's fatal failure mode. So the correct posture for reading it: liangzhi is the voice that makes you uncomfortable, not the one that makes you comfortable. His last words were eight characters: my heart is luminous; what more is there to say.
