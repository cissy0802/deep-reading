The Power of Now

The Power of Now: A Guide to Spiritual Enlightenment · Eckhart Tolle · 1997 (folding in his 2005 book A New Earth: Awakening to Your Life's Purpose)

中文 →

In One Sentence

You are not your mind; you are the awareness in which thoughts appear, and nearly all psychological suffering comes from living in the past and the future while saying no to the way this moment already is. Tolle's way out points in a single direction: withdraw attention from thinking and bring it to the Now, say yes to this moment as it is, and then do whatever needs doing. Eight years later, A New Earth carried the same diagnosis from the individual mind out into relationships and societies, showing how the thought-built self he calls the ego keeps feeding itself on possessions, roles, grievances and conflict.

Where It Sits

Tolle was born in Germany, studied in England and has long lived near Vancouver. He belongs to no tradition and presents what he sees as the common core of all of them. The Power of Now is the most widely read book of the "presence" genre since the late 1990s: written as questions and answers, it quotes the Buddha, Jesus and Zen without belonging to any of them. In 2008 Oprah Winfrey picked A New Earth for her book club and co-hosted a series of online classes on it, which made Tolle a household name. Compare Singer's The Untethered Soul (Read 979) on this site.

The Core Claims

  • Humanity's basic illness is identification with the mind: taking the involuntary, unstoppable stream of thought in your head to be who you are. That false self, made of thought, is what Tolle calls the ego.
  • Suffering runs on psychological time. The ego can live only in the past (regret, resentment, the story of who I am) and the future (anxiety, hope, "it'll be fine once..."), because the present gives it nowhere to stand. All negativity, Tolle says, comes from an accumulation of psychological time and a denial of the present (paraphrased).
  • The way out is not better thinking but stepping out of thinking. Watch your thoughts, feel the body from inside, accept the "isness" of this moment completely, and you taste a peace that doesn't depend on conditions, which Tolle calls Being. Awareness itself is the remedy: pain and thought that are seen can no longer fully run you.

The Key Concepts, One by One

1. You are not your mind

What it is. Tolle's starting point is inseparable from his own story. In the book's introduction he says that until his thirtieth year he lived in almost constant anxiety broken by spells of suicidal depression. One night, shortly after his twenty-ninth birthday, a sentence kept repeating in him: "I cannot live with myself any longer." Then it stopped him. If "I" can't live with "myself", there are two of them, so which one is real? He says his mind went still, he felt drawn into a vortex of energy and heard words in his chest, "resist nothing"; the next morning he woke to birdsong in a peace he had never known, and spent a long stretch afterward sitting on park benches (all of this is his own account, paraphrased). It cannot be checked from outside, but it sets the book's question: is the one who can't stand himself the same as the one being stood?

The mechanism. Most minds, Tolle says, are in compulsive thinking: thoughts arise on their own, commenting, comparing, complaining, rehearsing, without pause. We assume we're doing the thinking; mostly it is happening to us. His image: the mind is a superb instrument when used rightly; the trouble is that you've stopped using it and it has started using you, as if you'd forgotten you could put a tool down and decided you were the tool (paraphrased). The cost is an opaque screen of concepts, labels and judgments between you and the world, other people and yourself. You don't see this tree; you see the word "tree" and its associations. You don't meet the person in front of you; you meet your old picture of them.

An example. You're washing dishes while, in your head, you're arguing with a colleague who annoyed you last week, heart rate climbing. Your body is in the kitchen; you are in a fight that isn't happening. Tolle would say that only hot water and plates are actually occurring; the argument is a film the mind is showing you, and you've taken it for reality.

How it changes your view. "I'm anxious" can be replaced with a more accurate report: "anxious thoughts are arising here, and I'm watching them." Thoughts stop being you and become things you see. Tolle insists this isn't a call to stop thinking, only to put thinking back in its place as a tool you pick up and set down.

2. The watcher and the gap between thoughts

What it is. How do you get out of the mind? Tolle's first move is to "watch the thinker": listen to the voice in your head often, as you'd listen to someone else, without judging, arguing or condemning. The moment you watch a thought, something deeper than thought is activated: the awareness doing the watching. You find, he says, that your sense of "I am" comes not from the thought but from what is watching it (paraphrased).

The cat at the mouse hole. One exercise is concrete: ask yourself, "I wonder what my next thought is going to be," then wait for it, very alert, like a cat watching a mouse hole (paraphrased). Most people who try it notice that while they wait with full attention, the next thought is slow to come. That pause is what Tolle calls a gap of "no-mind": you aren't drowsy, you're unusually awake, only nothing is being thought. The gaps last seconds at first and lengthen with practice.

Why it matters. For Tolle, awareness and thinking are different things, and awareness is the deeper one: thought is only a small part of consciousness, while intelligence, creativity, love and beauty come from the stillness beyond it (paraphrased). He offers everyday doorways too: give full attention to a routine act, such as each step and breath as you climb the stairs, or the water's temperature and the smell of soap as you wash your hands (paraphrased). When attention is wholly in what you're doing, the mind has no room to spin a story.

How it changes your view. You don't have to defeat your thoughts before you can be at peace. They can keep coming; you just stop following them. The turning point isn't in the content of thought but in your relationship to it.

3. Clock time and psychological time

What it is. This is the book's signature distinction. Clock time is practical: when the meeting starts, how the project is scheduled, what you learned from a mistake. Psychological time is identification with past and future: an identity propped up by old stories ("I'm someone people always let down") and a fulfillment postponed to later ("once I get promoted, married, retired, then"). Tolle keeps the first and asks you to drop the second.

The mechanism: problems are mind-made. Tolle asks: have you ever experienced anything outside the Now? What happened in the past happened in a now; whatever comes in the future will arrive as a now (paraphrased). The only moment you can actually deal with is this one. So he separates your "life situation" from your "life". The situation is a story stitched together from past and future, and it may well be a mess. But ask, "What problem do I have right now?", not next year, not in five minutes, but now, and you often can't name one. There may be something to handle; there's rarely a "problem". A problem is what the mind makes when it drags a situation into time and chews on it.

An example. You lie awake at night replaying tomorrow's negotiation, more panicked by the minute. Your actual situation: under a duvet, in a quiet room. The panic isn't in the room; it's in a scene that hasn't happened. Tolle would say: if tomorrow needs preparing, get up and write an outline; if nothing can be done, admit that nothing can be done right now and come back to your breath and the warmth of the blankets. Act or let go; what drains you is the third option, not acting and not stopping thinking.

How it changes your view. You notice how much of life you spend waiting for later: the weekend, the holiday, the moment things get sorted. Tolle isn't saying drop your goals. He's saying don't use the present as a stepping stone to the future; pursue goals in clock time, but don't hang your peace on them.

4. The pain-body

What it is. This is Tolle's most original and most quoted idea. Every emotional pain from the past that wasn't fully faced, accepted and released, he says, leaves a residue; the residues accumulate into an energy field living in you, which he calls the pain-body. It behaves like a semi-autonomous parasite: dormant most of the time, it wakes when triggered and takes over your thinking and feeling (paraphrased).

The mechanism: it feeds on pain. Tolle's most startling claim is that the pain-body needs more pain to survive (paraphrased). Once awake, it steers you toward thoughts that hurt, toward picking fights and saying wounding things, because the other person's counterattack is exactly its food. You may notice moments when you know you should stop talking and can't; whoever is talking then is no longer your usual self. Some people's pain-bodies are quiet most of the time; others' are almost always half awake.

An example. A couple is having a pleasant dinner. One says, offhand, "You forgot again," and the other suddenly ignites, dragging up five years of grievances; they're still at it at midnight. Tolle would say the forgetting didn't cause this: both pain-bodies woke at once and fed each other. He extends this to intimate relationships generally: the addictive love-hate kind is often two pain-bodies that need each other.

What to do. Tolle's method is surprisingly simple: watch it. When an unusually heavy or sharp emotion rises, recognize it as the pain-body, then put your attention on feeling it in the body rather than following its thoughts. The pain-body, he says, survives by your identifying with it; once you become its observer it can no longer feed on your thinking and slowly hands its energy back to awareness (paraphrased). This isn't suppression; it's feeling fully without acting it out or telling its story.

How it changes your view. You start to tell apart the reaction a situation deserves from an old wound using it as an excuse to flare. Someone else's outburst becomes legible too: an old pain has taken them over for the moment, and you don't have to step in, or answer with your own.

5. Presence

What it is. Presence is where the whole teaching is headed: being wholly in this moment, alert and still, with no thought standing in between. It isn't trance; it's more awake than usual. Tolle borrows a parable of Jesus: servants keeping watch for their master's return, not knowing the hour, and so staying alert the whole night (paraphrased). He reads that alert waiting as the shape of presence.

Why it matters. For Tolle, presence isn't a means to freedom; it is the freedom. You don't have to get your life in order before you can be present; presence is available in any situation, in a queue, a traffic jam, at the sink. He flags one common disguise: waiting. We spend our lives waiting for the end of the workday, the weekend, the right person, and waiting means wanting the future instead of the present (paraphrased). A present person stands in queues too, but isn't "waiting"; they're in the queue.

How it changes your view. The dead stretches you dread most, commuting, queuing, waiting for the lift, turn out to be the handiest places to practice.

6. The inner body

What it is. Tolle's most practical anchor is the body, not as it looks in a mirror but as it feels from inside. Close your eyes and put your attention in your hands: can you feel a faint aliveness there, a tingling or warmth? Then move to the feet, the belly, the chest, and finally sense the whole body as a single field of energy (paraphrased). He calls this the inner body and says it is the easiest door into Being.

The mechanism. The mind can live only in time; the body can live only now. You can't feel tomorrow's hands. So keeping part of your attention in the inner body drops an anchor in the present. Tolle suggests doing this through the day: while someone is talking, keep some attention inside your body and you'll find you actually listen instead of preparing your rebuttal; when provoked, sink attention into the body first, so the mind and the pain-body can't drag you off.

More than one door. Tolle calls such entry points portals into the Unmanifested, his term for the invisible source from which all things arise: the inner body, silence, space, conscious breathing, the cessation of thinking, surrender. When you hear a sound, notice the silence it rises out of and falls back into; when you look at a room, notice not just the furniture but the space between it. Each exercise is the same move: shifting attention from things to the background that holds them.

How it changes your view. The body stops being only an instrument and becomes a place you can always return to. You needn't understand "Being"; feeling the aliveness in your palms is enough.

7. Surrender

What it is. Surrender is the idea most easily mistaken for resignation, so Tolle spells out what it isn't. Surrender is ceasing inner resistance to the isness of this moment: not accepting that a situation will always be so, but accepting that right now it is so, because it already is, and resisting it only piles extra pain on a fact.

Three options. The book's most practical passage: if you find your here and now intolerable, you have three options. Remove yourself from the situation, change it, or accept it totally (paraphrased). The one to avoid is the fourth: neither leaving nor changing nor accepting, just complaining and resisting inside, which is the main source of misery. The example of being stuck in mud makes it sharper: surrender isn't "fine, I'm resigned to being stuck here"; it's first admitting "I'm stuck in mud," without cursing or asking "why me?", and then putting all your energy into getting out (paraphrased). Action that starts from presence is stronger and clearer than action that starts from resistance.

Illness and pain. Tolle applies the same stance to physical pain: don't label it "illness" or "misfortune" at first; just put attention on the sensation itself and stay with it. Pain that is fully accepted, he says, changes (paraphrased). That can't be verified as a general claim, but it describes a clear move: stop adding a second layer of pain, "this shouldn't be happening," on top of the facts.

How it changes your view. Acting and accepting stop being opposites. You can accept completely that you've lost your job today and be sending out applications tomorrow morning. The difference is that the person sending them isn't being driven by fear.

The Skeleton of the Argument

The book really argues one thing: suffering comes mainly not from your situation but from the layer of thought and time between you and it, and that layer can be seen through. The line runs in four steps. First, you've identified with the mind and taken thoughts for yourself. Second, the mind can only live in past and future, so you keep denying the present, producing anxiety, resentment and "problems", while undigested old pain builds into a pain-body that keeps fueling the machine. Third, awareness breaks the identification: watching thought, feeling the inner body, tasting Being in the gaps between thoughts. Fourth, from that awareness you say yes to this moment, and act, relate and face pain from there.

What does it rest on? Almost no outside evidence. Tolle cites no studies and gives no data. He relies on three things: his own account of awakening, a rereading of scripture (Jesus's and the Buddha's words read as pointers to presence), and, most important, experiments the reader can run on the spot: listen for your next thought now, feel your hands now. That's what the question-and-answer format is for. It isn't arguing; it keeps pulling the reader from understanding back to experience.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: "living in the now" means no planning or reflection. Tolle explicitly keeps clock time: planning, learning and memory are all to be used. What he rejects is the past as identity and the future as salvation.

Misreading two: surrender means putting up with anything. See the three options: leave, change or accept are all fine. What's ruled out is enduring while seething.

Misreading three: watching emotion means suppressing it. Tolle asks for the opposite, feeling fully without identifying or spinning a story. Suppression is a form of resistance, and it feeds the pain-body.

Criticism one: unfalsifiable and vague. Being, the Unmanifested, energy fields, a pain-body that lives on pain: none of these comes in a testable form, and any result can be explained as "not truly present yet". What readers can verify for themselves are experiential observations (thoughts can be watched; attending to the body tends to calm you), but the book promotes those directly into metaphysical conclusions without an argument in between.

Criticism two: the awakening story can't be checked and is easily romanticized. Tolle's transformation exists only in his telling. A sudden peace or a lasting sense of selflessness could have other explanations that can't be judged from outside, and turning one person's change into a universal path is itself a leap.

Criticism three: spiritual bypassing. In the 1980s the American psychotherapist and Buddhist practitioner John Welwood coined "spiritual bypassing": using spiritual ideas and practices to sidestep psychological wounds, unresolved feelings and real-world responsibilities. "It's just a thought" or "that's the pain-body" can easily become a way of not facing childhood trauma, not seeing a doctor, not admitting real problems in a relationship. For people with severe depression, trauma or dissociative symptoms, practices like these aren't enough on their own, and lines like "you are not your mind" can sometimes worsen a sense of unreality.

Criticism four: silence on structural injustice. When suffering is traced almost entirely to an individual's psychological time, poverty, discrimination and violence lose their weight. Tolle does allow for changing the situation, but the book's center of gravity stays inside, with little on when one must fight.

Criticism five: rewriting the traditions. Tolle quotes the Buddha, Jesus and Zen freely, reading them all as teachings of presence. Scholars of Buddhism note that its core is not-self and that it posits no deeper Being as a true self. The traditional practice of Advaita Vedanta, the Indian school of non-dualism, involves long study of scripture, discipline and a teacher, and contemporary "neo-Advaita" style teaching is often criticized for skipping all that and keeping only the conclusion "you already are it". Christian critics object to his recasting of Jesus's words as spiritual psychology.

Criticism six: unsupported claims and commercialization. Some specific assertions, such as the female pain-body being activated before menstruation and women carrying a "collective female pain-body" from millennia of subjugation (paraphrased), come with no evidence and an essentialist tinge. When Oprah's book club put A New Earth in front of millions in 2008, another criticism followed: a teaching about letting go of the ego became a large publishing and course industry, and some evangelical Christians publicly objected to Oprah promoting New Age spirituality through it.

Tolle Beyond This Book

Read only The Power of Now and Tolle can look like a teacher of personal calm. A New Earth supplies the other half: the ego doesn't live only in one person's head; it operates between people, through possessions, identities and groups, and much of humanity's collective madness is the ego writ large.

The ego is identification with form. Here Tolle broadens his definition: the ego is identification with form, with thoughts, the body, things, roles, opinions. Its smallest unit is the word "mine". To take an illustration: a stolen old watch may be worth little, yet its owner can't sleep, because what was lost feels like part of "me". The ego never feels it has enough, so it wants more: more things, more recognition, more attention.

The ego's usual moves. Complaint and resentment (casting others as wrong so you can stand on the side of right), the need to be right, the need for enemies, playing roles (teacher, patient, victim, success story), and turning an old story of hurt into an identity. Many arguments, Tolle says, aren't about the matter at all; both egos simply can't afford to lose (paraphrased). The moment you notice "I have to win this point," you've seen the ego.

Collective ego and collective pain-body. Nations, religions and political factions also form a "we" that defines itself against a "them"; Tolle sees the mass violence of the twentieth century as this collective ego's insanity (paraphrased). He extends the pain-body to the collective too: a people's historical trauma lives on in its descendants. These are his diagnoses; the book offers no historical argument for them.

Awakened doing. A New Earth says more about how to live afterward. Everyone has an inner purpose, awakening and presence, which is the same for all, and an outer purpose, the particular things you do, which varies and changes. Action from presence takes three modes: acceptance (you don't like this task, but it's needed now, so you do it calmly), enjoyment (full absorption in the pleasure of doing it) and enthusiasm (going all out for a goal without being captured by the ego's need to win) (paraphrased). If none of the three is available, he says, you're probably creating suffering for yourself or others. This is where Tolle's thought moves from how not to suffer to how to act.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · a wandering mind is an unhappy mind: In 2010 Harvard's Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert sampled over two thousand adults by phone and found minds wandering nearly half of waking time, with people less happy on average while wandering. Alike: both tie not being here to unhappiness. Unlike: it's correlational, and the causal story isn't settled.
  • Neuroscience · narrative self and experiential self: In 2007 Norman Farb and colleagues in Toronto found that thinking about oneself engages medial prefrontal regions; after eight weeks of mindfulness training, people asked simply to sense present experience showed less activity there and relatively more in lateral prefrontal cortex and the insula, which handles interoception, the sense of heartbeat, breath and other internal signals. Alike: it is almost a brain-scan version of Tolle's self-in-thought versus inner body. Unlike: it measures two modes of attention, not a deeper Being.
  • Western philosophy · the specious present: William James argued that the "now" we experience isn't a point but a short span holding the just-past and the about-to-come; Husserl described every present as carrying retention (the fading echo of what just was) and protention (anticipation of what's next). Unlike Tolle: for them, past and future are part of the present itself, not impurities to strip away.
  • Buddhism · the second arrow: In the Sallatha Sutta the Buddha says an untrained person struck by one arrow (bodily pain) shoots himself with a second (grief and resistance); the wise feel only the first. Alike: both separate a fact from resistance to it. Unlike: the sutta starts from not-self and points to no abiding Being.
  • Stoicism · only the present can be lost: In Book 2 of the Meditations, Marcus Aurelius writes that however long anyone lives, what they lose is only the moment they are living, since past and future are not theirs to lose (paraphrased). Alike: both bring you back to now. Unlike: the Stoic uses the present for reasoned judgment and duty, not for stepping out of thought.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. You are not your mind; you are the awareness in which thoughts appear.

2. The mind is a fine tool; the trouble is you can't put it down. You aren't using it; it's using you.

3. Ask "what will my next thought be?" and wait like a cat at a mouse hole: that gap is the first door out of the mind (paraphrased).

4. Use clock time, drop psychological time: don't make the past your identity or the future your salvation.

5. Too much future is anxiety, too much past is resentment and guilt; this moment usually holds a thing to do, not a "problem".

6. The pain-body is old hurt grown into an energy field that lives on fresh pain; watch it and it can't feed on your thinking (paraphrased).

7. The inner body is the nearest anchor to now: you can't feel tomorrow's hands, only these.

8. When a situation is intolerable, leave it, change it or accept it totally; the worst is the fourth way: stay, don't change it, and don't accept it.

9. A New Earth: the ego is identification with form; it wants more, needs to be right, needs enemies, and in nations and groups swells into collective madness.

10. None of it can be falsified, and it can be used to sidestep real wounds and real injustice: treat it as a practice to test daily, not a doctrine to believe.
