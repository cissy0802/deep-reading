DEEP READING · READ 94

The Origins of Totalitarianism

Hannah Arendt · 1951 (the chapter "Ideology and Terror" was added in 1958)

中文 →

In one sentence

Totalitarianism is not a dictatorship with the cruelty turned up. A tyrant wants you quiet, taxed and out of the way; this wants the whole person — and it gets it by combining an ideology that deduces the entire course of history from a single premise with a terror that never asks what you have actually done, until the space between people, where they could confer, band together and think their own thoughts, has been squeezed shut. And it succeeded not because some demon was unusually gifted, but because modern Europe had already manufactured the parts: superfluous money, superfluous men, and masses for whom belonging nowhere had become an ordinary daily condition.

Where it sits

Hannah Arendt (1906–1975), German-Jewish political thinker, student of Heidegger and Jaspers, was briefly held by the Gestapo in 1933, escaped Germany, passed through Paris and the French internment camp at Gurs, and reached the United States in 1941 — she was herself stateless for eighteen years, and that fact is built into this book. It appeared in 1951, written while the Nazi regime had just fallen and Stalin was still alive. She disliked the English title's promise of "origins." The German edition is called Elements and Origins of Total Domination: she was not tracing a causal chain but identifying components already scattered across modern Europe that finally crystallized into something new. (Her Eichmann in Jerusalem is READ 78: that book is about one cog in the machine; this one is about the machine.)

The core claims

  • Totalitarianism is a new form of government, not tyranny intensified. Tyranny wants obedience; totalitarianism wants you superfluous. It does not press people down with law — it removes the layer of law that keeps a boundary between one person and the next, and puts the movement there instead.
  • Its two pillars are ideology and terror. Ideology supplies a premise (all history is racial struggle; all history is class struggle) and a chain of freezing deductions from it. Terror does not ask about guilt: it clears away the category that the law of History or Nature has condemned.
  • The soil was grown by modern society itself. Surplus capital and surplus people drove imperialism's "expansion for expansion's sake"; the colonies produced race-thinking and rule by decree; the collapse of the nation-state after 1918 left millions of stateless people who were nothing but human; the dissolution of class society left atomized masses. The common denominator of terror is loneliness.

The key ideas, one at a time

Antisemitism: the hatred is not eternal — privilege without function is the gunpowder

She opens by dismantling two popular explanations. The first is eternal antisemitism: hatred of Jews as a two-thousand-year constant, almost a natural phenomenon. Arendt's objection is that this sounds like an indictment and works as an amnesty — if hatred is as permanent as the weather, then massacre is climate, and no person and no political arrangement has to answer for it. The second is the scapegoat theory, in which the victim is picked purely at random. But randomness explains nothing: why this group, why the end of the nineteenth century, why in the form of a political programme?

Her own accounting runs differently. Europe's absolute monarchs, and then the nation-states, needed financiers who were transnational, belonged to no class and had no state of their own — hence the court Jews. The state granted a small group privileges and protection in exchange for that group not organizing itself as a political power. By the late nineteenth century the state had turned to imperial finance and the stock market, and the function disappeared; the wealth remained, the conspicuous privilege remained, and the political power had never been there at all. Her judgement, in paraphrase: people will put up with exploitation and even oppression as long as the oppressor visibly does something — the landlord runs the land, the manufacturer runs the plant, you hate him but you know what he is for. Wealth detached from function becomes pure injustice, because nobody can say what it is doing there. (In any company, the most resented figure is rarely the boss; it is the person on the highest salary whose job nobody can describe.) Wealth plus privilege, minus function, minus power, is the most dangerous position a group can occupy.

She closes the volume with the Dreyfus affair — the Jewish French officer convicted of treason in 1894 and not fully exonerated until 1906. What interests her is not the miscarriage of justice but the ring of phenomena around it: the crowds shouting "Death to the Jews," the mass press that inflated a case into a civil war, and above all the alliance of the mob with the social elite. "Mob" is a technical term for her, and it does not mean the poor and certainly not the people: it means the residue that has dropped out of every class — ruined aristocratic sons, déclassé intellectuals, speculators — people who have left their class without entering another, and therefore resent the entire social structure. The most telling scene is the salon: writers and society ladies finding the street fever "crude but alive," the voice of what nobody dares say. The elite gave the mob its stamp of approval — a necessary step in the formation of every totalitarian movement since.

Expansion for expansion's sake: surplus money goes looking for surplus men

Part Two covers the thirty years of overseas imperialism after the Berlin Conference of 1884, and the ledger is blunt. Capital at home had accumulated past the point where reinvesting it paid, producing superfluous capital; the same industrialization had thrown off people with no place in the national economy, superfluous men. Ship both abroad together and you have imperialism. Arendt calls it the alliance of capital and mob: the money needed somebody to watch it far from home, and the mob, which owned nothing in Europe, arrived in the colonies as a master.

From this grows the section's central claim: expansion for expansion's sake. Cecil Rhodes supplies the best gloss, in a line she quotes — "I would annex the planets if I could." The nation-state is by definition bounded: one people, one territory, one law, and the border of the territory is also the border of the law. Expansion has no terminus. It is not a bigger state; it is a movement of power that has made the process itself its purpose, structurally identical to accumulation for accumulation's sake in business. Once politics stops aiming at a condition that could be reached and starts aiming at a motion that must be maintained, it is one step from totalitarianism — because an achievable aim implies a day when you stop, and a totalitarian movement never intends to stop. Worth carrying into ordinary life: of any organization that treats perpetual growth as its purpose, ask whether it has any moment at which it could say enough.

Race and bureaucracy: two devices built in the colonies, and the boomerang

Imperial rule invented two instruments. The first is race. Her case is the Boers in southern Africa: a handful of European settlers confronting a society they could not read treated the people in front of them neither as enemies nor as parties one could negotiate with, but as part of nature. Out of that grew a habit of describing differences between human beings as differences in kind. Its danger is not arrogance but the abolition of politics: nature does not negotiate, it is merely managed.

The second is bureaucracy — the Egyptian, Indian and Algerian pattern, with Lord Cromer in Egypt as the exemplar. Its core is rule by decree: never legislate, simply issue administrative orders as needed. Law is public, stable and given in advance, so you can plan a life around it; decrees can change at any moment, need no justification, and answer only upwards. And the great "advantage" of the arrangement is precisely that nobody can be held to account: the decision was not made by a person, it was made by a procedure, by "upstairs." Arendt would later call bureaucracy rule by Nobody — and unaccountability is not its defect but the source of its efficiency.

Hence the formulation that has outlived everything around it: the boomerang effect. What the metropole permitted itself in the colonies — administration without law, populations sorted by race, a supply of people who could be disposed of — eventually swung back home. The two organs of totalitarian rule, an ideology that defines people by category and an administrative machine unbound by law, were not invented from nothing in 1933; Europe perfected them on its own extraterritorial proving grounds and then brought them back for domestic use. What a country allows itself abroad is the draft of its own future at home.

The pan-movements and tribal nationalism: from party to movement

Running alongside overseas imperialism were the continental pan-movements, Pan-Germanism and Pan-Slavism. They resemble the patriotism of the nation-state and are its opposite. National identity in a nation-state is tied to territory and law — you are a citizen of this ground. Pan-movement identity is tied to blood and soul: a German is a German wherever he happens to live. It therefore spills over borders by nature, and by nature it is hostile to the state and to law itself: law has boundaries and blood does not. Arendt calls the result tribal nationalism — a secularized doctrine of chosenness that keeps "we are the chosen," discards the one who chose and the obligations that came with him, and distributes the sense of election to every member: you need not accomplish anything, birth already enrols you in the tribe that is going to win.

Here the totalitarian form appears for the first time. A party has to bargain in a parliament, so it must have a programme, and a programme can be tested, fulfilled and refuted. A movement has no programme, only a direction, and therefore can never fail — it is only ever still on its way. A movement can live without holding power, and having taken power it does not intend to settle down — which is why Nazi Germany produced so many offices with overlapping competences. That was not administrative chaos; it was a deliberate refusal to let power be located anywhere, so that the movement would not congeal into a state.

Statelessness and "the right to have rights"

After 1918 the empires broke up, new states appeared across central and eastern Europe, and with them came the Minority Treaties, millions of minorities, and an enormous new category of person: the stateless. The nation-state's default structure is the trinity of people, territory and state, and it quietly assumes that "human being" means "citizen of somewhere." When a person loses nationality, human rights ought to take over at exactly that moment — and the opposite happens: the instant someone is nothing but human, human rights turn out to protect nothing at all. The body that proclaims them is the state, the body that enforces them is the state, and the institution that has just expelled you is the only one that could honour the cheque. The stateless person is worse off than a criminal: a criminal has at least entered the field of law through a specific act, and is thereby a person the law recognizes.

Out of this comes the phrase that has outlasted the book's politics — the right to have rights: more fundamental than any particular right to speech, property or movement is the standing to belong to a political community, so that your opinion registers as an opinion and your action has consequences. Her formulation, paraphrased: the calamity of the rightless is not that they lack food or equality before the law, but that no community on earth counts them as members. They are like someone holding a passbook for a closed account — every clause intact, no bank that recognizes it. This chapter is the book's bridge to the camps: first you need people with no place in law, and only then people who can be disposed of at will.

The masses: not the people, not a class, and not the mob

Part Three changes protagonists. The mob gives way to the masses: what is left after class society dissolves — too many, sharing no common interest, and therefore impossible to organize into anything built on interest, whether a party, a union or a trade association. Their signature is not rage but indifference. Arendt notes the counter-intuitive fact that the largest reservoir of manpower for a totalitarian movement is the people who had never voted and whom every party had written off as irrelevant. Their arrival is not a tilt in the existing landscape; it is a continent surfacing.

The distinction from the mob matters. The mob has fallen out and wants to climb and to smash. The masses want admission to a whole that explains everything, and will sacrifice themselves for it. Arendt keeps returning to something more frightening than fanaticism — the selflessness of the totalitarian member: a person who does not care about his own interest or his own life cannot be bought with advantage, deterred by threat, or argued out of it by common sense, since common sense presupposes that everyone cares somewhat about himself. The mechanism is less dry tinder meeting a flame than weightlessness: a roomful of people each held to the ground by class, trade and neighbourhood, all of those ropes cut at roughly the same moment, and now any wind at all can carry the whole room off together.

Ideology: deducing all of history from one premise — the tyranny of logicality

Arendt's "ideology" does not mean an opinion or a position. It means what the word says: the logic of an idea — a premise used as a key from which the whole of the past, the present and above all the future is deduced. Her three marks of ideological thinking, in paraphrase: (1) it explains not particular facts but the total movement of history; (2) it emancipates thought from experience, positing a truer reality behind appearances, so that what you can see becomes the mere surface; (3) it proceeds by deduction, and once the premise is granted, no subsequent step requires — or will tolerate — checking against experience.

The last point is the whole of it. The compulsion does not live in the idea; it lives in the words "therefore," "and so," "necessarily." You can refute a conclusion with a fact, but it is very hard to stop someone who has accepted the premise from taking the next step, because formally the next step is correct. Arendt borrows Hitler's own phrase about ice-cold reasoning (paraphrased) and names the condition the tyranny of logicality: a person ruled by the deduction machine in his own head. Think of a program that needs one axiom as input and then generates every theorem on its own — once it is running, whoever runs it no longer needs to think, only to keep up; and if reality fails to match the deduction, that only shows reality has not caught up yet. What this gives you is the ability to tell "his conclusion is wrong" apart from "he has stopped checking conclusions against experience." The first can be argued with. The second cannot.

Terror: not a means but the form of government itself — and the "objective enemy"

Common sense says terror is a tool for crushing opposition, and should subside once the opposition is gone. The record says the reverse: both the Nazi and the Stalinist terror reached their peak long after organized opposition had ceased to exist. A tool that accelerates after its targets disappear is not a tool. Totalitarian rule understands itself as executing a law higher than any human law — the law of Nature (the racial struggle) or of History (the necessary succession of classes) — and terror is that law's enforcement arm. It does not punish the guilty; it clears away the category that is condemned to disappear. Guilt and punishment come apart completely: the more innocent the arrested person, the better the demonstration that the system does not look at individual conduct. That is not an excess in execution; it is a requirement of the design.

The matching concept is the objective enemy, which replaces the "suspect" of a legal order. A suspect is defined by what he may have done; an objective enemy by which category he falls into — kulaks, Jews, "socially harmful elements." The categories are defined by policy and can be swapped or widened at will; what you have done does not matter, what you are does, and what you are is not up to you and offers no path to reform. Obeying the law cannot save you, because it is not the law that defines you. Hence her most famous image, in paraphrase: laws are like walls that leave open ground between one person and another, and that open ground is freedom — the place where people confer, combine and act. Terror tears the walls down and replaces them with an iron band that presses everyone together until no gap remains between them, as though human plurality had vanished into a single enormous man. So the aim of terror is not a body count; it is the abolition of the space between people. Which gives you a usable test: to judge a regime, do not only count its victims — ask whether it still tolerates any connection between people that it did not organize.

Propaganda: the self-fulfilling prophecy, and believing everything and nothing

Arendt separates the two operations cleanly: propaganda faces outward, toward the world not yet ruled; once in power, the instrument turned on the ruled is indoctrination plus terror. The most poisonous move in totalitarian propaganda is to convert a claim into a prophecy. "The Jews will be exterminated" is not a threat but a forecast; carry it out and the sentence becomes retroactively true and the speaker becomes a prophet. The prophecy fulfils itself by being executed.

Why do the masses take it? Her diagnosis is that famous compound: extreme credulity together with extreme cynicism. They believe anything and simultaneously assume anything may be a lie. Reality has deceived them too often, so they conclude that every public statement is manufactured; and then the largest lie is no more suspect than any other, and is in fact more convincing, because it is big enough, consistent enough and capable of fitting every fragment into one story. Her most-quoted line, paraphrased: the ideal subject of totalitarian rule is not the convinced Nazi or the convinced Communist, but the person for whom the distinction between fact and fiction, between true and false, no longer exists. Totalitarianism need not make you believe what it says; it only needs you to believe that nowhere is anything true. Once truth and falsehood are worth the same, all that is left to decide where you stand is who is stronger, more consistent, and more willing to give you a place.

The camps: three steps to making a human being superfluous

She calls the camps the laboratory of totalitarian rule. What was being demonstrated there was not that certain people deserved to die but a proposition: everything is possible. She is careful to distinguish this from nineteenth-century nihilism's "everything is permitted," which still assumes a human being doing the permitting. "Everything is possible" means that the human being himself can be remade into something else. That is why the camps are economically absurd — at the worst moment of the war they consumed rolling stock, manpower and materials in order to transport and kill people who could have worked. They did not pay, because they were never a means. They were the point. Her analysis proceeds in three steps, each deeper than the last:

Schematic: Arendt's three stages of "total domination" (Part Three).

The first step strips nationality and severs arrest from any deed — it is precisely because the people taken are innocent that the rules there are decisively not law. The second makes death anonymous and traceless: no grave, no notice, nobody who knows on what day it happened, so that even martyrdom becomes unavailable (when a death cannot become anyone's memory, sacrifice has been abolished), and then imposed complicity — prisoner overseers, choices forced between family members — destroys solidarity and self-respect among the victims. The third uses prolonged hunger, arbitrary violence and unpredictable rules to grind a person down to a bundle of reflexes; what remains, in her phrase (paraphrased), is a set of marionettes with human faces whose reactions are entirely predictable — and complete predictability is exactly what is left when the human being has been removed. Spontaneity, the capacity to do something nobody could have foreseen, is what totalitarianism is actually out to destroy.

Here the three parts close on each other: superfluous money produced superfluous men, and superfluous men were used to prove that humanity itself can be manufactured into surplus. In this book she names it radical evil. Twelve years later, having watched Eichmann in the dock, she revised the verdict: evil has no root, it is only extreme (paraphrased). That revision is the most important correction in her thought, and the reason these two books have to be read together (see READ 78).

Isolation, loneliness, solitude: three words worth separating

The final chapter — "Ideology and Terror," added in 1958 — puts her answer inside three easily confused words. Isolation is political: people are cut off from the possibility of acting together. Every tyranny does this, but it only destroys the public realm; you still have a family, a craft, friends. Solitude is being alone with yourself, and it is where thinking happens; Arendt describes it as the two-in-one, in which I talk with myself and contradict myself. Solitude is not lonely, because you have company, and the company is you. Loneliness — in German Verlassenheit, literally being deserted — is the third and worst: abandoned by everyone else and by yourself as well, with even the self that would have argued back now missing. At that point only one thing still stands up: when experience can no longer give you a world and no companion can confirm what is real, "therefore, and so, necessarily" becomes the only certainty you can hold. The lonely have a special need for logic, and that need is the door ideology comes through.

Her conclusion, in paraphrase: loneliness is the common ground of terror and the essence of totalitarian government, and what prepares modern people for such rule is that loneliness, once the condition of the very old, has become an everyday experience. This explains something that otherwise makes no sense — why totalitarian rule is not content with obedience but also breaks up friendships, rewards denunciation, and makes people afraid to speak honestly to the person beside them in bed. That is not gratuitous cruelty; it is the central engineering. First empty the space between people, then separate a person from himself, and what remains will fit inside the movement.

The argument, distilled

The three volumes are a staircase going down. Step one (antisemitism): modern antisemitism is not the continuation of an ancient hatred but a new technique of political mobilization, fuelled by a structural position — wealth and privilege intact, social function gone — with the Dreyfus affair as the full dress rehearsal, mob-and-elite alliance included. Step two (imperialism): surplus capital and surplus men push Europe into expansion for its own sake, converting politics from a condition to be reached into a motion to be maintained; the colonies build two exportable machines, race-thinking and unaccountable rule by decree; the pan-movements supply the form of the "movement," which needs no programme and no border; and the post-1918 collapse of the nation-state leaves millions of stateless people to prove that human rights fail exactly when a person is nothing but human. Step three (totalitarianism): the dissolution of class society leaves atomized masses for the movement to absorb; ideology supplies the deduction and terror the enforcement; the camps serve as the laboratory in which people are unmade in three steps. The soil under all of it is loneliness turned ordinary.

What does the book actually establish? Not that two regimes were unusually wicked, but something harder to digest: totalitarianism is not barbarism invading the modern world from outside; it is a recombination of the modern world's own components. Which is why she ends (paraphrased) by warning that totalitarian solutions may well survive the fall of totalitarian regimes, as temptations that surface whenever it seems impossible to relieve political, social or economic misery in a manner worthy of human beings. And how does she establish it? Not with statistics or a tight causal chain, but by placing scattered elements side by side and showing how they crystallized. That is the book's power, and it is also where most of the criticism lands.

Misreadings, criticisms and live disputes

Misreading one: "totalitarian" as a synonym for "very harsh dictatorship." This is the most common reading and the one that does the book the most damage. Arendt's distinctions are fine-grained:

By her criteria most brutal regimes are not totalitarian at all; she reserved the fully developed form for Nazi Germany 1938–45 and the Soviet Union under Stalin (roughly 1930–53).

Misreading two: reading "origins" as causal history. She rejected that reading — the German title is Elements and Origins of Total Domination, and her word is crystallization. Turned around, this is also the standard historians' complaint: the joins between the three parts are rhetorical rather than demonstrable. Strung into a line the argument reads powerfully and verifies poorly.

Criticism one (heaviest against Part One). Her account of Jews "losing their social function" has been criticized by many historians of Jewish life as locating part of the cause of the hatred in the victims' social position; her source base was thin, and the internal variety of European Jewish society is handled coarsely. The storm twelve years later over the Jewish councils in Eichmann in Jerusalem belongs to the same lineage.

Criticism two (Part Two). Postcolonial scholarship values her for grafting colonial violence onto the history of Europe's catastrophe, and "boomerang" remains a keyword in that field. The same scholars fault her Eurocentrism: the colonial dead appear mainly as prehistory for a later European disaster rather than as their own history, and several passages on the Boers and on Africans carry the racial vocabulary of her period and read badly today. There is no point covering for her.

Criticism three (the central one): putting Nazi Germany and Stalin's USSR under one concept. This is the weak point of the whole totalitarian paradigm. Revisionist Soviet historiography from the 1960s onward, and the archives opened after 1991, describe a system far messier than a monolith: the Great Terror combined quotas from the centre with local zeal, institutional infighting and an enormous volume of neighbourly denunciation, and society was not simply atomized material that terror rolled over. Research on Nazi Germany points the same way — the Gestapo was astonishingly thin on the ground and depended heavily on ordinary people informing. The conclusion is not that she was wrong (nothing has replaced "ideology + terror + movement" as a characterization) but that the picture of an atomized mass crushed by a perfect machine needs the addition that a great many people put their own hands in — an addition that leads straight to her own later Eichmann book.

Criticism four: the concept's Cold War career. After the 1950s "totalitarianism" rapidly became an instrument of American foreign-policy rhetoric, and was for that reason abandoned by a whole generation of scholars in the 1970s. Once a concept becomes that useful for choosing sides, it is hard to keep using it as an analytic tool. A word in her favour: what has lasted best is probably not the typology but the components — statelessness and the right to have rights, ideology as the compulsion of logic, terror and the objective enemy, loneliness as a political condition. Even if you reject the pairing of the two regimes entirely, those parts remain sharp, and most of them work well outside the twentieth century.

Ten sentences

1. Totalitarianism is not tyranny intensified but a new form of government: the tyrant wants obedience, totalitarianism wants you superfluous — the first is satisfied when you shut up, the second will not let you be silent and still think your own thoughts.

2. Explanations that make hatred eternal sound like accusations and function as pardons — if hatred is as permanent as the weather, massacre is climate and nobody has to answer for it.

3. The most dangerous social position is neither wealth nor power but wealth and privilege that have outlived their function: people tolerate an oppressor who visibly does something, and cannot tolerate a privilege nobody can account for. (paraphrased)

4. Expansion for expansion's sake: once politics aims at maintaining a motion rather than reaching a condition, no moment can ever be enough — Rhodes's "I would annex the planets if I could" was not a joke.

5. The boomerang effect: administration without law and populations sorted by race were perfected in the colonies and then brought home. What a country allows itself abroad is the draft of its own future at home.

6. Statelessness exposes the fatal paradox of human rights: the moment a person is nothing but human, human rights protect nothing — since the state that proclaims and enforces them is the one that has just expelled him. Hence the most basic right of all, the right to have rights.

7. Ideology is not a position but a chain of deductions from a single premise; its compulsion lies not in the idea but in the words "therefore, and so, necessarily" — what she called the tyranny of logicality.

8. Terror is not a means but the form of government itself: it peaks after the opposition has vanished. Law is a set of walls leaving open ground between people; terror replaces the walls with an iron band and presses everyone into one giant — what it destroys is not people but the space between them. (paraphrased)

9. "The ideal subject of totalitarian rule is not the convinced Nazi or the convinced Communist, but the person for whom the distinction between true and false no longer exists." (paraphrased) It need not make you believe it; it only needs you to believe that nothing anywhere is true.

10. Isolation is having no one to act with, solitude is keeping yourself company, loneliness is when even that self is gone — and the lonely have a special need for logic. So breaking up friendships and rewarding denunciation is not surplus cruelty but the core engineering: empty the space between people, then separate a person from himself, and what is left will fit inside the movement.
