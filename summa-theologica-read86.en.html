Summa Theologiae

Thomas Aquinas · 1265–1274 (left unfinished)

中文 →

In One Sentence

This is not a book about what to believe. It is a machine for putting faith and reason on the same driveshaft: it takes an entire pagan philosophy — Aristotle's — and uses it to explain a Christian world, arguing that the two not only fail to collide but actually need each other. What it left to posterity are three things you can use without holding any religious belief at all: a standard of argument still unsurpassed (state your opponent's case at its strongest before you answer it), a way of deriving how people should live from what people are (natural law), and one sentence that sums up the whole medieval confidence in reason: grace does not abolish nature but perfects it.

Coordinates

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274) was the son of Italian nobility, a Dominican friar, and a teacher at the University of Paris. His century had just absorbed something like a technology shock: the complete works of Aristotle came back into Latin Europe through the Arabic world — above all through the commentaries of Averroes — carrying conclusions (an eternal universe, real doubt about personal immortality) that ran straight into Christian doctrine. Paris banned the teaching of Aristotle's natural philosophy for a time. Aquinas staked his career on a single bet: this pagan philosophy does not have to be burned, it can be absorbed. The Summa is the ledger of that bet — three parts, more than five hundred "questions," close to three thousand "articles," and a book he never finished.

Central Claims

  • Faith and reason cannot genuinely conflict, because they share a source. The stretch reason can walk unaided (that God exists, that mind is not merely matter) he calls the preambles of faith (praeambula fidei). The Trinity and the Incarnation are not anti-rational; reason simply cannot reach them. So an apparent conflict always means somebody argued badly — a principle that licenses a theologian to take a pagan's argument seriously.
  • Grace does not abolish nature but perfects it. Human nature was wounded by sin, not destroyed: reason still functions, virtue can still be trained, secular politics has standing of its own. This is the master key to the whole book.
  • Morality does not rest on a list of commands but on what a human being is. If people have an end set by their nature, then what counts as good is fixed by what makes a person flourish as a person. That line of thought is natural law, and its point is that ethics becomes arguable rather than merely announceable.

The Core Concepts, One by One

The article (articulus): a format that forces you to steelman

Start with the form, because in this book the form is the content. The whole work is assembled from nearly three thousand identically structured "articles," each answering one yes-or-no question ("Does God exist?" "Should human law repress every vice?"), and each locked into four moves:

MoveLatinWhat it does
1. ObjectionsVidetur quod… ("It seems that…")List the arguments against his own position — and list them at full strength
2. On the contrarySed contraOne authority pulling the other way (Scripture, a Church Father, Aristotle), just to tip the scale back
3. The bodyRespondeo dicendumHis own positive argument — usually the shortest part
4. RepliesAd primum…Go back and answer every objection individually. None may be skipped

The four moves. Not a layout convention — an enforced order of thinking.

Two things make this powerful. First, it turns "put the other side's case better than they did" into a compulsory writing step. In the article on whether God exists (Part I, question 2, article 3), the two opening objections are: the world contains evil, and an infinitely good God would not permit it; and natural causes explain natural things while human reason explains human affairs, so there is no need to posit God at all. That is the problem of evil and Occam's razor, written by Aquinas himself, placed at the most conspicuous spot in the book — before he answers. What English now calls steelmanning has been standard procedure here for eight hundred years.

Second, he almost never replies "you are wrong." He replies "in this sense yes, in that sense no." The move is called a distinction. To the problem of evil he answers, citing Augustine (paraphrased): God is so good that he would never allow evil to exist unless he were powerful enough to draw good out of it. You are free to reject that answer, but notice the shape of it — the objection is not denied, it is relocated. That habit is the most portable thing in the book: the output of a good argument is not a winner, it is a sharper set of distinctions.

Grace does not abolish nature: the master key

Gratia non tollit naturam, sed perficit. It appears in a reply in the very first question of the book, tossed off as if in passing, and it is the load-bearing wall of the entire structure.

What it says is that the supernatural layer does not replace the natural layer; it sits on top of it and carries it to completion. An analogy: curing a man born with terrible eyesight does not mean giving him somebody else's eyes. It means the eyes he already had start working properly. Same eyes, same optics, finally in focus.

The consequences run further than the sentence suggests. First, pagan philosophy gets a legitimate seat: in this book Aristotle is simply "the Philosopher" and Averroes is "the Commentator" — a Muslim and a fourth-century-BC Athenian treated as citable authorities by a Dominican friar. Second, secular life has standing of its own: since human beings are political animals by nature, Aquinas holds that people would have formed cities even without the Fall. The state is not a product of sin but a completion of nature — which promotes politics from necessary evil to natural good. Third, the body and the emotions are not things to be extinguished: he explicitly denies that the passions are evil in themselves; they are material waiting to be ordered by reason. If you still carry the assumption that religious thought is necessarily anti-intellectual, anti-worldly and anti-body, this book is the largest counterexample there is.

The Five Ways: the most famous arguments in philosophy, and the most often aimed at the wrong target

Five routes from ordinary experience to God: from change (whatever changes is changed by something else); from efficient causation (nothing brings itself about); from contingency and necessity (if everything might not have existed, something must exist that cannot fail to); from degrees of perfection (things are more and less good, so there must be a maximum); and from directedness (mindless things behave reliably toward outcomes).

What matters is the engine the first three share, and it is almost always missed. They are not tracing a chain backwards in time. They describe a chain of dependence operating right now — what the schoolmen called a per se ordered series. A hand moves a stick, the stick moves a stone: remove the hand and the whole chain stops in the same instant, because the stick's power to move is borrowed. That is nothing like "grandfather begat father begat me," where each link has causal power of its own and the earlier ones can be dead and gone. What Aquinas wants is the terminus of the first kind: a mover that borrows nothing.

Which brings a clarification that changes the whole debate: Aquinas himself held that philosophy cannot prove whether the universe had a beginning in time. He argued this against his contemporary Bonaventure, who thought it could be proved. Creation in time, for Aquinas, is accepted on faith, not demonstrated by reason. So "then who made God?" and "isn't the Big Bang the first cause?" are both aimed elsewhere: the "first" he is after is first in the order of being, not first on a timeline.

Don't over-defend him either. The Fourth Way is agreed on all sides to be the weakest: it assumes a Platonic premise, that wherever there are degrees there must be a maximum that causes them. "Some things are hotter than others" does not get you to a hottest thing. The Fifth Way is under pressure too: after Darwin, "mindless things behaving in a directed way" has a mechanism that requires no external mind. And even if all five worked, what they deliver is a necessary being — several hundred pages short of a God who hears prayers, which is exactly the distance Aquinas spends the rest of Part I covering.

The real distinction between essence and existence: why his God is not an item on the inventory

This is the hinge of his metaphysics and his most original departure from Aristotle. Essence (what a thing is) and existence (that it is) come apart in every created thing. You can know perfectly well what a phoenix is — a bird that burns and is reborn — and that tells you exactly nothing about whether any exist. Since "what it is" does not include "that it is," existence has to be given to it from outside.

Follow that all the way down and exactly one exception is possible: something whose essence simply is its existing. Aquinas's name for it is ipsum esse subsistens, subsistent being itself. So God is not the largest item on the inventory of things, not a supremely big being among beings; God is the sheer act of existing. That single move fixes the grammar of everything after it: pure act (actus purus — no unrealised potential, hence no change), simple (no parts, hence no such thing as "part of God").

Why this changes how you read the argument: a great deal of theist-versus-atheist debate is a dispute about whether to add one more entry to the universe's inventory. Aquinas's God was never on that list, so arguing for or against him as if he were is shadow-boxing on both sides. It is also why Aquinas says, with striking frankness, that we can know more securely what God is not — not a body, not composite, not in time — than what God is.

Analogy: what entitles us to say anything about God at all

The previous section leaves a trap. If God is utterly unlike everything else, then every word we apply to him is simply false and theology should shut up. But if the words mean exactly what they mean of us, God is just a large version of ourselves — which is idolatry. Aquinas's solution is to insert a third kind of usage between the two.

A word can be univocal ("this dog is alive," "that cat is alive" — one meaning); it can be equivocal (the bank of a river and the bank that holds your money — the same noise, unrelated senses); or it can be analogical. "A healthy man," "a healthy diet," "a healthy complexion": strictly, only the body is healthy; the diet is called healthy because it causes health, the complexion because it shows health. The three uses are neither identical nor coincidental — each is used according to its real relation to the core sense. "God is good" works this way: goodness in us is received and bounded, in him it is present at the source, in a higher mode.

The lesson travels far outside theology. Everything we say about what lies beyond direct experience — consciousness, infinity, quantum states — is analogy; the only variable is whether we notice. Unnoticed analogies smuggle the baggage of the image along with it: call the brain a computer and before long people are looking for its memory and its CPU. Aquinas's contribution was to name the practice and give it a rule: you must be able to say, at any moment, what the word keeps here and what it drops.

Happiness: why nothing in particular can fill you up

The five questions that open Part II are the stretch of the book that needs no faith to read. Aquinas treats the human final end as an elimination problem. Wealth? Its entire point is to be exchanged for something else; it is a means and cannot be an end. Fame? It lives in other people's heads, not in you. Power? It serves good and evil alike, so it is not itself a good. Pleasure? Pleasure is what accompanies getting a good, not the good itself — chasing pleasure for its own sake is driving by staring at the dashboard.

Then the mechanism: the object of the will is the good in general, and every particular good is bounded, so none of them fits. This is not a moral scolding; it is a statement about sizes — a vessel shaped to take infinity will not be filled by any quantity of finite things. That diagnosis stands whatever you believe: it is the earliest clean account of the most ordinary fatigue in a consumer society, the flatness of the few days after you finally get the thing. Aquinas's terminus is the beatific vision, the intellect seeing God's essence directly. You can decline the terminus and still find the diagnosis hard to refute.

The four laws: how ethics becomes arguable

The dozen or so questions on law in the middle of Part II are the most concretely influential thing in the book. Law comes in four layers:

LayerWhat it isHow it is known
Eternal law
lex aeterna		God's ordering of the whole of creationnot directly visible to us as a whole
Natural law
lex naturalis		the rational creature's share in the eternal law — not a tablet of rulesby reason, available to anyone
Human law
lex humana		actual legislation, derived from natural law by conclusion or by determinationpromulgated by those with authority
Divine law
lex divina		revealed law, supplying what reason cannot reachby revelation

Four layers. The work is done by the middle two, and by the gap between them.

The distinction between conclusion and determination is what lets natural law touch the ground. A conclusion follows straight from the principle: do no harm, therefore do not murder — and every legal system on earth agrees. A determination is a legitimate choice made where natural law is silent: natural law requires that traffic be safe, and has no view whatever on left or right — but once a jurisdiction picks one, it binds. That single distinction solves a genuinely hard problem: how to hold that some moral floor is cross-cultural while national laws may reasonably differ.

The first practical principle of natural law is that good is to be done and pursued, and evil avoided. It is not proved and cannot be; its standing in practical reasoning is what non-contradiction's is in theoretical reasoning — any piece of practical thinking is already standing on it. From there it unfolds through three natural inclinations: to preserve one's life, to have and raise children, to know the truth and live with others.

Two consequences still carry real weight:

  • An unjust law is no law. Aquinas's own phrasing is that it is not law but a corruption of law (legis corruptio) — which is not rhetoric, it is a claim that the obligation to obey is conditional. Martin Luther King Jr. cited exactly this passage in the 1963 "Letter from Birmingham Jail" to justify civil disobedience.
  • Law should not try to suppress every vice. He writes plainly that human law should forbid only the graver vices, chiefly those that harm others, since people cannot bear more and would be driven to worse evils. A thirteenth-century friar produced a strikingly modern principle of legislative restraint — one you can almost splice directly onto Mill's harm principle.

How it changes the way you see things: the question "is there a standard above enacted law?" gets a framework here that needs neither an appeal to feeling nor a prior religious commitment. Why "it was German law at the time" failed as a defence at Nuremberg traces back, genealogically, to this.

Virtue, and why courage alone is not enough

He splices two lists together: the Greek cardinal virtues — prudence, justice, fortitude, temperance — and the Pauline theological virtues of faith, hope and charity. The first set is built by repetition; the second, on his account, is given.

The one to get right is prudence, which the English word now badly misrepresents. It does not mean caution. It means the ability to see what should be done here, now, in this particular situation — the step that brings a universal principle down onto a single case (Aristotle's phronesis, practical wisdom). Why is it the hinge? Because without prudence every other virtue deforms: courage without it is recklessness, justice without it is doctrine applied until it wounds. Principles are always general; what you face is always a particular situation for which no principle has pre-written the answer.

One more mechanism worth stating: for Aquinas a virtue is a habitus — a stable disposition built by repeated acts, not a resolution and not a feeling. You do not become honest and then start telling the truth; you tell the truth enough times that telling it stops costing you anything. This is precisely where the twentieth-century revival of virtue ethics reconnected: Anscombe's "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958), charging modern ethics with keeping the notion of obligation after discarding the lawgiver, and MacIntyre's After Virtue (1981), which spends a whole book asking, alongside "what rules should you follow?", the older question — what sort of person should you become?

Double effect: the line between intending and foreseeing

In the article on killing in self-defence, Aquinas sets down a piece of reasoning that later broke loose and became a standalone tool of moral philosophy: one act can have two effects, one inside the agent's intention and one outside it but foreseen. What the defender intends is saving his own life; the attacker's death follows but is not intended, and so long as no more force is used than the situation requires, the act is not made evil by that side effect.

The load-bearing distinction is intending versus foreseeing: you can see a consequence coming clearly and still not intend it — it is neither your aim nor the means you use to reach your aim. The line is still holding weight today: the difference in the ethics of war between collateral damage and deliberately targeting civilians; in medical ethics, palliative sedation (giving enough analgesia to stop pain, foreseeably shortening life, as against giving a drug in order to shorten life); and an entire literature on trolley cases, where switching the track and pushing a man off a bridge differ precisely in whether the death is a side effect or the means.

In fairness, the line has been under attack for a century. Consequentialists argue it should carry no moral weight at all — the person is equally dead, and what was going on in your head does not change what happened in the world. It is a strong objection, but note the cost: almost every criminal code on earth separates murder from negligent homicide on exactly this basis. You can argue the distinction should not bear weight; you first have to admit that our whole moral and legal architecture is currently resting on it.

The Argument in Outline

The book's structure is a circle, which the schoolmen called exitus–reditus, going out and returning. Part I: who God is, and how everything proceeds from him — creation, angels, the structure of body and mind, how knowing happens. Part II, the longest by far, nearly half the work: how rational creatures return to him — human action, the passions, habits, virtues, sin, law and grace. Part III: Christ as the road back, and the sacraments. Out from the source, through free human action, back to the source. Everything else is elaboration of that one line.

So what does it establish, and on what? In a sentence: that the world as fully explained by reason and the world as accepted by faith are the same world. The method is to bolt Aristotle's framework of causes, ends and virtues onto a doctrine of creation — Aristotle supplying "what is operating and how," revelation supplying "toward what," and the master key serving as the joint. That is also where the structure is most exposed: one side of the joint is an Aristotelian physics that science has replaced, the other is a revelation that cannot be tested.

One biographical fact belongs here rather than in a footnote: he never finished. On 6 December 1273, after Mass, he stopped writing and told his secretary (paraphrased) that everything he had written seemed to him like straw. He never touched Part III again and died three months later, not yet fifty. The "Supplement" printed at the end of modern editions was assembled by his secretary Reginald from Aquinas's much earlier commentary on the Sentences; it is not his own composition. The largest work of rational theology ever attempted was abandoned by its author in mid-sentence.

Misreadings and Serious Criticism

Misreading 1: "The Five Ways are trying to prove the universe had a beginning." They are not. They concern a dependence chain operating now, and Aquinas states in black and white that philosophy cannot settle whether the world began — he argued the point against Bonaventure. "Who made God?" and "the Big Bang was the first cause" both miss.

Misreading 2: "He thought reason could prove the whole of the faith." The opposite: one of his central projects is drawing the boundary. The Trinity, the Incarnation, creation in time — reason cannot establish any of them, only show they are not self-contradictory. Reading him as a rationalist apologist is as wrong as reading him as an anti-rational dogmatist.

Misreading 3: taking the Fifth Way for Paley's watchmaker. That is 1802, and it is an argument from analogy (complex structures resemble artefacts, so there is an artificer). Aquinas is talking about immanent directedness. The two differ — but do not overdo the defence: natural selection supplies exactly what the Fifth Way says requires a mind, namely a mechanism for directed behaviour in mindless things.

Misreading 4: "Medieval theologians argued about angels on the head of a pin." The jibe has no traceable source and is not in Aquinas. His questions on angels are doing hard metaphysics: how are individuals distinguished if they have no matter and occupy no space? How does a mind know anything without senses? Philosophy of mind is still asking both under other names.

What follows is the criticism that actually lands, and it should not be softened:

  • Hume: causal necessity is not read off the world but produced by habitual association; and the very notion of a necessary being is suspect, since whatever can be conceived to exist can be conceived not to exist. That cuts at the foundation of the first three Ways.
  • Kant, twice over. First, every cosmological proof smuggles in the ontological one, because pinning "necessary being" onto something requires the claim that its essence contains existence — and existence is not a predicate. Second, categories like cause and necessity are valid only within experience; applying them to the universe as a whole, or to God, is a use they cannot support. This is where most modern philosophers get off the train, and they have not got back on.
  • The system is built on a physics that has been abandoned. Act and potency, substantial forms, natural ends — that is Aristotle's natural philosophy, and science discarded it. So the live question becomes: can the metaphysics be detached from the physics? Contemporary Thomists (Edward Feser, Eleonore Stump) say yes; critics say it is defending a skeleton with no body left. The fight is not over.
  • The is–ought problem in natural law. "People have a natural inclination to preserve their lives" does not yield "you ought to preserve your life" — Hume's gap, and what Moore called the naturalistic fallacy. The New Natural Law school (Grisez, Finnis) repairs it by saying the basic goods are self-evident starting points of practical reason rather than inferences from facts about nature. The repair is strong; it is also accused, by sympathisers as well as opponents, of no longer being Aquinas.
  • The positions of his that are hardest to stomach are in the text, not in his critics' caricature. He holds that an obstinate heretic, twice warned, may be handed to the secular power and put to death, on the reasoning that corrupting the faith is graver than counterfeiting money. Following Aristotle's biology, he calls the female a defective male (mas occasionatus), though he adds that with respect to nature as a whole woman is intended. Deleting these turns a thirteenth-century man into a portrait. Worth noticing at the same time: those conclusions rest on biological and political premises taken as fact at the time — and by his own method, in which natural law depends on reason's reading of nature, changed premises ought to change the conclusions.
  • The cost of becoming official. In 1277 Bishop Tempier of Paris condemned 219 propositions, some touching Aquinas's positions; the relevant condemnations were withdrawn after his canonisation in 1323. Then Leo XIII's 1879 encyclical Aeterni Patris made Thomism the quasi-official philosophy of the Church — which preserved the tradition at the price of freezing a living body of argument into a manual. Most of the twentieth-century revivals (Gilson and Maritain, Rahner's transcendental Thomism, and the analytic Thomism running through Anscombe, Geach, MacIntyre and Stump) have essentially been attempts to thaw it.

Ten Sentences

1. The master key is a line short enough to miss: grace does not abolish nature but perfects it — the supernatural layer does not replace the natural one, it carries it to completion. The whole building stands on that sentence.

2. Nearly three thousand articles hold one format: state the case against yourself at its strongest, cite an authority the other way, give your own argument, then answer every objection one by one. Steelmanning was standard procedure eight hundred years ago.

3. He almost never tells an opponent they are wrong. He distinguishes — in this sense yes, in that sense no. The output of a good argument is not a winner but a sharper set of distinctions.

4. The Five Ways describe a dependence chain operating right now (hand, stick, stone), not a regress in time — and Aquinas himself held that philosophy cannot prove whether the universe began, so "who made God?" never touches him.

5. The Fourth Way is the weakest by common consent, and the Fifth is under pressure after Darwin; even if all five worked, they deliver a necessary being, several hundred pages short of a God who hears prayers.

6. In anything created, what it is and that it is come apart — you know what a phoenix is without knowing whether any exist. The one exception is the thing whose essence is its existing, so his God is not the biggest item on the inventory but existence itself, and arguing about him as an inventory item is shadow-boxing on both sides.

7. A healthy man, a healthy diet, a healthy complexion: analogy is how language reaches past experience without lying or merely punning. We do it whenever we talk about consciousness, infinity or quantum states — the only variable is whether we notice.

8. Wealth is a means, fame lives in other people's heads, pleasure only accompanies the good — the will's object is the good in general, and every particular good is bounded, so none of them fits. Not a scolding about greed; a statement about sizes.

9. Natural law's two most durable consequences: an unjust law is "not law but a corruption of law" (King quoted precisely this from a Birmingham jail); and law should not try to suppress every vice, since people cannot bear it and are driven to worse.

10. The line to keep: the largest work of rational theology ever attempted was abandoned by its own author. On 6 December 1273 he stopped writing, said (paraphrased) that all he had written seemed like straw, and died three months later. Part III was never finished.
