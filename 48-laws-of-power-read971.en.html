The 48 Laws of Power

The 48 Laws of Power · Robert Greene · 1998

中文 →

In One Sentence

Power is a game everyone plays and almost no one admits to playing. Greene combs three thousand years of courts, battlefields and markets for the moves that keep recurring and hardens them into 48 cold "laws." The book does not teach you to be good; it tells you something uncomfortable: who rises and who falls in a group is decided less by talent and sincerity than by appearances, reputation, dependence and emotional control. Its most valuable use is not as a playbook but as a field guide: a way to recognize the moves being run on you.

Where It Sits

Robert Greene trained in classics and drifted through dozens of jobs (by his own count around eighty), including stints in Hollywood and magazine journalism, before producing this book in 1998 with the Dutch book packager Joost Elffers. He went on to write The Art of Seduction, The 33 Strategies of War, Mastery and The Laws of Human Nature. The book stands at the popular end of a cold-eyed tradition that runs through Machiavelli's The Prince, Gracián's The Art of Worldly Wisdom and La Rochefoucauld's Maxims: a long-running bestseller and one of the most contested self-help books ever written.

Core Claims

  • Power is a game you cannot opt out of. In the preface Greene compares modern life to a royal court. The court demands surface grace, modesty and loyalty while a cutthroat struggle for favor runs underneath; today's companies, social circles and even families are courts of the same kind. His conclusion is chilly: people who announce that they don't play power games are usually playing a subtler version. Posing as pure and harmless is itself a strategy for winning trust and the moral high ground.
  • Power is won indirectly. Openly displaying ambition, ability or intent invites envy, suspicion and counterattack. So most of the 48 laws teach the oblique route: hide your aims, let others believe your idea was theirs, give credit upward, let someone else do the dirty work.
  • The laws are tools, not morals, and every one of them can fail. Each law follows the same template: a one-line Judgment, a historical Transgression (someone who broke the law and paid), an Observance (someone who kept it and won), an Interpretation, the "Keys to Power," an Image, an Authority (quotations from the old masters) and finally a Reversal: when the law backfires. What Greene is really training is not obedience to 48 rules but a way of seeing: treating every social situation as a board on which each player's interests, vanities and fears are laid out.

1. Appearance is reality: reputation is the capital of power

Law 5 reads "So much depends on reputation—guard it with your life." The mechanism is plain: other people have neither the time nor the means to check you out, so they judge you by reputation and bearing, and your reputation fights battles on your behalf before they begin. Someone known to be tough rarely has to prove it; someone known to be reliable is believed by default.

Greene's favorite illustration is Zhuge Liang's "empty fort." With his army away and his city undefended, Zhuge Liang sees Sima Yi's troops approaching. Instead of fleeing, he throws open the gates and sits on the wall burning incense and playing the zither. Sima Yi, who knows Zhuge Liang as a man who never takes reckless gambles, concludes there must be an ambush and withdraws. Zhuge Liang did nothing; a lifetime of reputation won the battle for him. (Note that the story comes from the tradition of the novel Romance of the Three Kingdoms, not from the official histories, a point that matters in the critique below.)

Reputation also compounds. A good one gets every action read charitably; a bad one gets everything read as malice. So Greene urges you to repair any crack immediately, and warns that the cheapest way to defeat a rival is often not to beat him but to wreck his reputation. Next door sits Law 6, "Court attention at all costs," starring the showman P. T. Barnum, who understood that in a world short on attention being talked about, even badly, beats being ignored; and Law 37, "Create compelling spectacles," because images and rituals bypass reason and go straight to emotion.

How it changes your view: you start separating what someone has done from what he is seen to have done. Much of the time, you are judging not the person but a carefully managed image.

2. Hide your intentions and say less than necessary

Law 3, "Conceal your intentions"; Law 4, "Always say less than necessary." The logic is information asymmetry: once your aims are visible, the other side has time to defend; the more you talk, the more you reveal your weaknesses, your ordinariness and what you actually want. Greene doesn't recommend clumsy silence but a "smoke screen": a sincere, ordinary, even noble front that hides the real target and draws attention to the wrong place.

The cautionary tale for Law 4 is the Roman general Coriolanus (told by Plutarch in his Lives, and dramatized by Shakespeare). A decorated war hero, he campaigned for consul by showing the plebeians his scars, as custom required, and seemed certain to win. Then he spoke publicly with open contempt for the common people, opinion swung against him, and he lost; later, after further speeches such as opposing grain distribution to the plebs, he was driven into exile. Silent, he was a legend; once he opened his mouth, he was just an arrogant man.

Greene's extension: people who say little acquire mystery, because others project their own imaginings into the silence, while people who say a lot eventually say something they cannot take back. This applies doubly to the powerful: the less they say, the more each word weighs.

How it changes your view: when people talk, you start listening for what they leave out; and when someone seems unusually candid and harmless, you ask whether the candor is itself the smoke screen.

3. Never outshine the master

Law 1 sets the tone for the whole book: "Never outshine the master." The deepest insecurity of anyone above you is being eclipsed by someone below. The more talented you are, the more your talent needs to look like it is polishing the master's glory rather than competing with it.

The negative example is Nicolas Fouquet, Louis XIV's superintendent of finances. In August 1661 he threw a staggeringly lavish party for the young king at his newly finished château of Vaux-le-Vicomte: gardens, fireworks, a new comedy by Molière, gold and silver tableware. Fouquet meant to display loyalty and taste. The king saw a subject whose splendor outshone the monarch's. Less than three weeks later Louis had the musketeer captain d'Artagnan arrest him; Fouquet was convicted of embezzlement, imprisoned for life, and died in the fortress of Pignerol in 1680. The irony: Louis then hired the same architect, painter and garden designer who had built Vaux to create Versailles.

The positive example is Galileo. In 1610 he discovered four moons of Jupiter with his telescope and, instead of trumpeting his own brilliance, named them the "Medicean Stars" in honor of the Medici of Tuscany, turning his discovery into a cosmic emblem of his patrons' destiny. Soon afterward he was appointed chief mathematician and philosopher to the Medici court, and his livelihood was secure.

In the Reversal, Greene concedes (paraphrased) that you cannot worry about offending everyone, and that if the master is already falling, there is no need to keep shielding him.

How it changes your view: it explains a familiar workplace puzzle, why the most capable person is often not the one promoted fastest. In a hierarchy, ability is only half the story; the other half is whether your ability makes those above you feel flattered or threatened.

4. Dependence and interest: be needed, not thanked

Law 11, "Learn to keep people dependent on you"; Law 13, "When asking for help, appeal to people's self-interest, never to their mercy or gratitude." Both rest on one judgment: gratitude fades and affection changes, but interest and dependence are structural.

The classic case for Law 13 comes from Thucydides' History of the Peloponnesian War. Around 433 BC, Corcyra was at war with its mother city Corinth, and both sent envoys to Athens for help. The Corinthians spoke of past favors and obligations. The Corcyraeans were blunt: we have the largest navy in Greece after yours; an alliance with us serves you, and letting that fleet fall to your enemies would hurt you. Athens chose Corcyra. Greene's lesson: translate any request into what the other side gains. People respond coolly to "you owe me" and warmly to "this is good for you."

Law 11 is the long-run version: better to be needed than to be thanked (paraphrased). Command a skill others lack, information others don't have, relationships others can't reach, and you go from a replaceable part to a load-bearing wall. Greene adds that the dependence should feel like need, not hostage-taking; a patron who feels held hostage will look for a way to get rid of you.

How it changes your view: when you assess your place in an organization, the question is not "do they like me?" but "what breaks if I leave tomorrow?" And before you ask anyone for help, figure out why they would want to give it.

5. Distance, scarcity and unpredictability: emotion is your weak point

Law 16, "Use absence to increase respect and honor"; Law 17, "Keep others in suspended terror: cultivate an air of unpredictability." The mechanism is scarcity and uncertainty: what is always available is cheap, and whoever is predictable can be exploited.

Law 16 draws on Herodotus' story of Deioces the Mede. He won great prestige as a fair judge, and people flocked to him to settle disputes. At the height of his fame he suddenly announced he would judge no more. Lawlessness followed, and the people made him king. Once crowned, he built walls within walls, withdrew into his palace, rarely appeared, and let his subjects communicate with him only through messengers. The king nobody could see seemed all the more a king.

For Law 17 Greene turns to the 1972 world chess championship in Reykjavik. Bobby Fischer arrived late, kept making demands about prize money, lighting and cameras, and even forfeited a game by refusing to show up. The Soviet champion Boris Spassky, a creature of method, was thrown off balance and lost the title. Greene's reading: Fischer used unpredictability to unsettle an opponent who depended on everything going to plan.

This line connects to Law 39, "Stir up waters to catch fish": stay calm yourself and provoke your opponent, because angry people act against their own interests, and your anger hands the steering wheel to whoever provoked it.

How it changes your view: in the moment you are provoked, ask who profits from your reaction. And if you are always on call and endlessly accommodating, consider whether you have made yourself too cheap.

6. Selective honesty: one staged kindness buys all their trust

Law 12, "Use selective honesty and generosity to disarm your victim." The mechanism: once people decide you are honest, they stop checking. A single unexpected confession or gift is the price of buying off their vigilance.

Greene's story stars the 1920s con man Victor Lustig, famous for "selling" the Eiffel Tower. Lustig approached Al Capone and promised to double $50,000 in sixty days. He put the money in a safe deposit box untouched, and two months later returned it to Capone, explaining that the deal had fallen through. Capone had seen plenty of swindlers but never one who gave money back. Moved by this "honesty," he handed Lustig $5,000 on the spot, which was exactly what Lustig had been after all along.

Its neighbors are Law 14, "Pose as a friend, work as a spy," and Law 21, "Play a sucker to catch a sucker": seem dumber than your mark so he lowers his guard. All three share a mechanism: manipulation works best precisely when the target feels safest.

How it changes your view: when someone is unusually honest or generous where you expected nothing, there is no need for instant suspicion, but it is worth asking what the kindness is meant to buy.

7. Formlessness: no law is always right

The final law, Law 48, is "Assume formlessness," and its image is water: having no fixed shape, it flows into any gap and cannot be grasped. It is the book's meta-law: any fixed pattern will be studied, predicted and exploited by opponents, so the true master clings to no single law and changes shape with circumstance. Law 47, "Do not go past the mark you aimed for; in victory, learn when to stop," is the flip side: success breeds rigidity and arrogance, turning one winning move into a supposed eternal truth.

This also explains why the laws so often contradict each other. Law 6 tells you to court attention; Law 16 tells you to withdraw. Law 25 tells you to re-create yourself and stand out; Law 38 says "Think as you like but behave like others." Greene's answer is that the laws bow to timing (Law 35, "Master the art of timing"), and every Reversal is a reminder of when a law stops working.

How it changes your view: power is not a set of moves you memorize and deploy but a continuous ability to read the situation, which is also why people who apply this book by rote are usually the first to be seen through.

The Argument in One Line

The whole book compresses into one chain: people crave a sense of control almost instinctively, and open competition invites retaliation → so the power game is necessarily played indirectly and in disguise; society is a court → in a court, others can only judge you by appearances, so reputation and image become the capital of power → therefore control information (hide intentions, say less), manage your relations with superiors and rivals (don't outshine the master, crush enemies completely, make people depend on you), and manage your own emotions and availability (absence, unpredictability, calm) → and all of it must shift with circumstance, ending in formlessness.

How does Greene "prove" it? Almost entirely through history: the court of Louis XIV, Renaissance Italy, ancient Greece and Rome, China's Warring States and Three Kingdoms, nineteenth-century European diplomacy, twentieth-century swindlers and businessmen. Each law gets a loser and a winner, plus quotations from Machiavelli, Gracián, Sun Tzu, La Rochefoucauld and others as "Authority." This is argument by illustration: its persuasive force comes from vivid stories, not from anything resembling statistical testing, which is both why it is such a page-turner and why it is so vulnerable.

Common Misreadings & Criticism

Misreading 1: It's a self-help book; follow it and you'll climb. The laws contradict each other and each comes with conditions under which it fails. Applied out of context, say, "concealing intentions" and "never trusting friends" in a team built on collaboration, they will cost you trust fast. Greene himself has said repeatedly in interviews that the book is first of all about understanding power, not an exhortation to do evil (paraphrased).

Misreading 2: Greene thinks people are evil. His stance is closer to amoral description: he does not judge, he describes how power actually operates in groups. The trouble is that the line between describing and prescribing blurs easily in a book written as imperative "laws."

Criticism 1: An unapologetic manipulation manual. Several laws openly teach using others: Law 7, "Get others to do the work for you, but always take the credit"; Law 15, "Crush your enemy totally" (one of Greene's examples is Xiang Yu letting Liu Bang slip away at the Hongmen banquet, only to be destroyed by him later); Law 33, "Discover each man's thumbscrew." Critics argue that the book turns other people into pure instruments and, used over time, corrodes the user's own relationships. It has been banned in prisons in a number of U.S. states, on the grounds that it could encourage manipulation and gang organization, even as it circulates widely behind bars and in hip-hop culture; the rapper 50 Cent later co-wrote The 50th Law with Greene.

Criticism 2: The history is cherry-picked and often unreliable. Each law gets only the stories that support it, and the same historical figure can play hero in one law and villain in another. Some stories don't hold up at all: the empty fort comes from a novel rather than the histories; the anecdote behind Law 7, Edison promising Tesla $50,000 and then telling him he didn't understand "American humor," rests mainly on Tesla's side of the story, with little independent corroboration. Events that historians see as complex and multi-causal are trimmed into clean moral fables, except that the moral is an anti-moral.

Criticism 3: Survivorship bias, and near-unfalsifiability. We read about people who used a move and won; those who used the same move and failed, got caught, or got purged mostly never make it into the book. Worse, every law comes with a Reversal: if things worked, the law was right; if they didn't, it was time for the Reversal. A system that can explain every outcome predicts nothing.

Criticism 4: A poor fit for modern cooperative organizations. Greene's world is mostly one-shot, zero-sum court intrigue. Most work today is long-term collaboration in a highly transparent "repeated game," where colleagues deal with each other again and again and reputations are recorded and passed around. In that environment a single exposed manipulation can sink your whole reputation, which, ironically, violates Law 5. Critics often cite the organizational psychologist Adam Grant's findings in Give and Take as a counterpoint: givers are overrepresented at both the bottom and the top of career success, and smart givers often end up going further than takers.

The honest verdict: as an observation of human nature, the book catches a lot that is real; reputation, envy, vanity and dependence really do shape who rises in organizations. As a guide to action it is badly overrated and morally expensive. Its most defensible use is defensive: understand it, then recognize which law someone is running on you.

Greene Beyond This Book

Read only The 48 Laws of Power and it is easy to take Greene for a pure tactician of intrigue. Twenty years later, The Laws of Human Nature (2018) turns the lens from the game outside to the people inside it. If the 48 laws are the moves, The Laws of Human Nature explains why those moves work on people, and much of it asks you to see yourself first. It has eighteen chapters, each a "law"; here are the most central.

The Law of Irrationality. Greene's starting point: we believe we decide by reason, but most of the time emotion decides first and reason supplies the justification afterward. He holds up Pericles of Athens as the model of the rational person, someone able to master his own impulses and the crowd's; after his death, Athenians driven by fear and greed slid into one disastrous decision after another. Rationality is not innate; it is a capacity built over years of noticing how your emotions bend your judgment.

The Law of Narcissism. Greene treats narcissism as a spectrum. Everyone needs some self-love to hold a self together, but "deep narcissists" have a hollow core that must be filled with constant attention from others, and they cannot truly see anyone else; at the healthy end, attention turns outward and becomes empathy. His advice: convert attention to yourself into close observation of others, which is the precondition for reading people at all.

The Law of Role-playing. Everyone wears a mask, playing a more agreeable character than the real self. Greene teaches you to read beneath the mask, since facial expression, tone of voice and posture are often more honest than words, while admitting that you too must play your role well. It is the psychological upgrade of the 48 laws' principle of appearances.

The Law of Compulsive Behavior: character is destiny. Character forms early and then repeats its patterns almost compulsively. So to judge someone, ignore what they say and look at what they have done over and over, especially under pressure and after setbacks.

The Law of Repression: confront your shadow. Here Greene borrows Carl Jung's concept of the Shadow: the parts of ourselves we refuse to acknowledge (aggression, envy, selfishness) get pushed into the unconscious, but they don't disappear. They leak out as projection (seeing our own flaws in other people), sudden emotional outbursts, or an exaggerated display of the opposite trait. One of Greene's tells: the harder someone performs a virtue, the more you should watch for its opposite. His aim is not to destroy the shadow but to acknowledge it and integrate its energy.

The Law of Envy. Envy is the most hidden emotion, because admitting it means admitting inferiority, so it rarely appears openly. It disguises itself as criticism, gossip or insincere praise. This lays bare the psychology behind Law 46 of the earlier book, "Never appear too perfect."

The remaining chapters cover shortsightedness, defensiveness, grandiosity, conformity, how leaders earn a following, aggression, generational perspective and, last, death denial: facing our mortality frees us from petty struggles and lets us live with focus. That arc from intrigue to self-knowledge, from using people to understanding them, is the complete Greene. Mastery (2012) traces a third path, in which power comes from real skill built over a long apprenticeship; it has its own entry on this site, so it is not unpacked here.

Crossovers

  • Game theory · Robert Axelrod's repeated prisoner's dilemma tournaments: in 1980 Axelrod had scholars submit programs to play the repeated prisoner's dilemma against each other. The winner was the simplest, tit for tat: cooperate first, then copy the opponent's last move; nice, retaliatory, forgiving and transparent. The likeness: both treat interaction as strategy. The difference: in repeated games the winner is precisely the player who does not hide its intentions; Greene's laws read as if written for one-shot games.
  • Primatology · Frans de Waal, Chimpanzee Politics: at the Arnhem zoo, de Waal watched chimpanzees win the alpha position through alliances, courting and dividing rivals rather than brute strength. The likeness: power is social and indirect from the start. The difference: de Waal also documented abundant reconciliation and consolation; cooperation and repair are the other half of primate politics.
  • Distributed systems · the Byzantine Generals Problem (Lamport et al., 1982): nodes must reach agreement even though some may be traitors sending false messages; tolerating f traitors requires at least 3f+1 nodes. The likeness: both assume allies may lie (Law 2, "Never put too much trust in friends"). The difference: engineers answer by designing rules that let an honest majority win, not by learning to lie themselves.
  • Psychology · the Machiavellianism scale (Christie and Geis): in 1970 Richard Christie and Florence Geis turned endorsement of manipulation into a measurable trait, the Mach-IV scale, later grouped with narcissism and psychopathy as the "Dark Triad." High scorers did best in face-to-face, loosely structured, emotionally charged situations, exactly Greene's court. The difference: such advantages are not stable in long-term relationships.
  • Buddhism · the eight worldly winds (lokadhamma): early Buddhist texts name eight worldly conditions (gain and loss, fame and disrepute, praise and blame, pleasure and pain) that toss the mind about. The 48 laws are close to a manual for riding those winds; Buddhism aims the other way, at a mind the eight winds cannot move.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. Power is a game you cannot opt out of; those who claim not to play are often playing a subtler version.

2. Society is a court: graceful and modest on the surface, an endless struggle for favor beneath, so power is mostly won indirectly.

3. Others can only judge you by appearances; reputation is the capital of power. Zhuge Liang's empty fort was held by his name alone.

4. Hide your intentions and say less than necessary: silence keeps mystery, talk exposes ordinariness.

5. Never outshine the master. Fouquet threw one party and spent his life in prison; Galileo named Jupiter's moons for the Medici and gained a patron.

6. When asking for help, appeal to interest, not gratitude; better to be needed than to be thanked.

7. What is always available is cheap, whoever is predictable can be used, and your anger hands the wheel to whoever provoked it.

8. One staged act of honesty can buy off all of someone's vigilance; the money Lustig returned to Capone was bait.

9. No law is always right; the final law is formlessness, changing shape with the moment.

10. The history is cherry-picked, the system nearly unfalsifiable, and in a world of long-term cooperation manipulation backfires; the best use is defensive: recognize which law is being run on you.
