Democracy in America

De la démocratie en Amérique · Alexis de Tocqueville · 1835 / 1840

中文 →

In One Sentence

A French aristocrat watching his own class get swept off the board sailed to America to see what a society of equals would look like — and came back with a conclusion nobody has managed to get around since: equality is coming and cannot be stopped, but it leads just as easily to a strange new servitude as to freedom, and what decides which way it goes is not the text of a constitution but how people actually live — what restrains them, what they belong to, and whether they can be bothered with anything beyond their own front door.

Where It Sits

Tocqueville (1805–1859) came from old Norman nobility; his parents barely escaped the guillotine, and his great-grandfather Malesherbes was executed for defending Louis XVI in court. In 1831, aged 26, he sailed to the United States with his friend Gustave de Beaumont on the official pretext of studying its prisons. He stayed nine months, filed the prison report — and wrote this. Volume One (1835) made him famous across Europe; Volume Two (1840) is more abstract and considerably darker. The thing to hold onto: he is not writing about America. He is using America to write about democracy, the social form then swallowing the West, and his intended reader is French. That move — asking not what government ought to be but what kind of people and politics a given social condition produces — makes him one of the founders of political sociology.

The Central Claims

  • Equality of conditions is the generative fact of the modern world, and it is irreversible. It is not a form of government but a social state, one that reaches into law, language, family, religion, art, ambition — everything.
  • Equality does not guarantee freedom and in fact endangers it. Equals withdraw into their private circles; public life empties out; and a mild, tutelary state moves in to fill the vacancy. Not a tyrant — a nanny. And people walk in willingly.
  • America kept its freedom inside equality not because of a clever constitution but because of its mœurs — township self-government, the habit of association, religion, juries, lawyers: the daily arrangements that force ordinary people to handle public business. Institutions can be copied. Habits cannot.

The Core Concepts, One by One

"Democracy" is a social condition, not a form of government

First thing to unlearn: Tocqueville's démocratie does not primarily mean voting. It means equality of conditions (égalité des conditions) — a society with no hereditary ranks, where no one is born naturally above anyone else. Elections are a flower that grows out of that soil, not the root.

His claim is that this has been running for seven centuries: the Church opening the priesthood to commoners, gunpowder letting a peasant kill a knight, printing ending the monopoly on knowledge, commerce making fortunes mobile. Nobody planned any of it — often the aristocracy itself pushed it along for short-term advantage — and every step ground the ranks flatter. Hence his word for it: providential. Universal, durable, beyond anyone's power to arrest. He is not casting a vote for or against; he is saying that since you cannot stop it, you had better learn to steer it.

Change this one definition and your view of the world shifts: democracy stops being a machine you can import, install and debug, and becomes a soil condition — whether or not people instinctively bristle at the thought that anyone stands above them. Wherever that instinct is general, whatever the regime is called, Tocqueville's effects begin.

Tyranny of the majority: what gets crushed is thought, not votes

The most quoted idea in the book and the most consistently flattened. It is not mainly about 51% outvoting 49% — that is only the legal shell. What frightened Tocqueville is this: in a society of equals, if nobody is born wiser than me, why should I defer to any particular authority? The only authority left standing is "what everyone thinks." The majority thereby acquires a moral weight impossible under aristocracy: it is not merely numerous, it becomes a synonym for being right.

Hence his blunt line (paraphrased): "I know of no country where there is so little independence of mind and real freedom of discussion as in America." The mechanism he describes is unnervingly precise. Under the old tyranny, the prince struck at the body and left the soul alone — and you became a martyr, and people admired you. Under the tyranny of the majority nobody lays a finger on you; they simply leave you exactly where you are, in full possession of your rights, and stop speaking to you. Publishers won't print you, voters won't have you, and even those who privately think you are right keep their distance in case the silence spreads to them. You are not killed; you are muted (paraphrased). Tocqueville's verdict: a more refined method, and a more complete one — it leaves the body and goes straight for the soul.

Why this has aged so well: it explains something no legal text can. Free speech is written down in black and white, and people still don't say what they think — because the pressure isn't coming from the government, it's coming from the anticipated social cost. The internet raised the RPM of this machinery by orders of magnitude: a majority opinion forms in hours, and social exile is one click. His remedy is not to abolish majority rule but to manufacture counterweights to it: an independent judiciary, local self-rule, associations, the conservative temper of the legal profession, juries that drag ordinary people into legal reasoning — so that "everyone thinks so" has to pass through several non-majoritarian gates before it lands on anyone's head.

Mœurs (habits of the heart): the layer beneath the law, where it is actually decided

Tocqueville asks a hard question: why does American democracy hold together? He answers in three ranked layers. (1) Physical circumstance — a continent of land, no dangerous neighbours, no standing army needed. (2) Laws. (3) Mœurs. And the ranking runs backwards: mœurs matter most, laws second, circumstance least. His evidence is almost experimental: the South American republics copied the U.S. constitution nearly word for word and got coup after coup, while New England townships with far scruffier legal texts ran beautifully. Same rules, different soil, entirely different plant.

What are mœurs? He uses the word broadly — not just morals but "the whole moral and intellectual state of a people": what they believe, how they habitually settle disputes, whether they regard public business as theirs or the government's, whether they instinctively call a meeting. The standard English rendering, habits of the heart, later became a term of art in sociology. Concretely, he names the pillars:

  • The township. "Municipal institutions are to liberty what primary schools are to knowledge" (paraphrased). Paving a road, running a school, maintaining a well — trivia that forces ordinary people to argue, compromise, and live with the consequences. Freedom isn't learned by reading a constitution; it's drilled by handling small dull things. Hand a ballot to someone who has never managed anything near him, and he will not know what it is for.
  • Religion. His argument is functional, not devotional. Democracy hands people limitless freedom of action; if nothing inside them limits desire, freedom rots into license, and people eventually prefer a master. Religion is the thing that neither votes nor legislates but still says some things are not done. And a counterintuitive observation: American religion is stronger precisely because it is separated from power. European churches strapped themselves to thrones, and went down with them.
  • The jury. Less a judicial institution, he says, than a free school of citizenship: it compels a private man to step out of his own affairs, judge someone else's, carry the responsibility, and pick up the habits of legal reasoning on the way.
  • The legal profession. Lawyers love precedent, form, and a jargon outsiders can't enter — aristocratic vices that in a democracy work as brakes. He calls them the only aristocracy a democratic society has (paraphrased).

The lasting effect on how you think: from here on, assessing any institutional reform anywhere, you'll reflexively ask one more question — have the daily habits that would carry this grown up alongside it? If not, the rulebook is paper.

Individualism: a pejorative he coined

Today "individualism" sounds like a compliment — self-reliance, a mind of your own. But the modern usage was essentially fixed by Tocqueville in Volume Two, and he coined it to name a disease. He carefully separates it from egoism: egoism is a passionate, exaggerated love of self, as old as humanity; individualism is "a calm and considered feeling" — reasonable, even decent: I withdraw into the circle of family and friends, and leave the larger society to look after itself.

Why must democracy breed it? Because equality cuts the vertical links. In an aristocracy you are born embedded in a chain: a lord above, tenants below, ancestors behind, heirs ahead — you could not attend only to yourself, because you were not a free-standing unit. Equality melts the chain and leaves grains of sand who owe each other nothing. His diagnosis is cold: democracy throws a man back forever upon himself alone, and threatens to confine him entirely within the solitude of his own heart (paraphrased) — it "makes every man forget his ancestors, hides his descendants from him, and separates him from his contemporaries."

The danger is sequential. Individualism first drains public virtue; then the drained space gets filled. A man who tends only his own household will not tend the town's business; the town's business, untended, must be taken up by someone; and that someone is the state. Individualism is not a road to independence. It is a road to being managed. His remedy is again a habit, not a law: association. Americans form a society for everything — hospitals, schools, missions, temperance. Where in France the government would act and in England a great lord would, he marvels, in America you will invariably find an association (paraphrased). The point isn't what the associations accomplish; it's that they are the only known way of gluing sand back together — they let people discover in practice that their fate is tied to other people's. Which is why he says that in democratic countries the science of association is the mother of all sciences (paraphrased): whether democracy survives depends on whether that craft is learned.

Self-interest rightly understood: the only workable democratic ethic

So what makes people in a democracy exert themselves for the common thing? His answer is practical to the point of deflating: don't count on classical self-sacrifice; l'intérêt bien entendu — self-interest rightly understood — will do. Americans, he notes, make no speeches about dying for the republic; they cheerfully explain that they do good because it suits them. Help your neighbour because you'll need help; fund the school because your children are in it; obey the rules because the rules protect your business.

The doctrine, he says, "does not produce great virtues, but it forms a multitude of orderly citizens" (paraphrased). No saints, no monsters — the average of an entire people hauled steadily above the pass mark. Why that is an insight and not a shrug: it concedes that a democratic ethic has to be scalable. A virtue only heroes can practise cannot hold up a society of equals. Everything then depends on the words "rightly understood" — on people seeing far enough to book the entry that says: if the public thing collapses, my private thing goes with it. Self-interest that can't see that far collapses back into plain individualism.

Soft despotism: the most startling forecast in the book

Near the end of Volume Two he announces that he must describe a form of oppression for which the old vocabulary has no name — tyranny, despotism and the rest all miss it. The picture he paints (paraphrased): an innumerable crowd of similar and equal men, each spinning around in pursuit of the small, vulgar pleasures with which he fills his soul, withdrawn into himself, indifferent to the fate of everyone else; they are still there, but he no longer sees them. Above this crowd rises an immense tutelary power, which alone takes charge of assuring their enjoyments and watching over their fate — absolute, detailed, regular, far-seeing and mild. It resembles paternal authority, except that a parent's aim is to raise a child into an adult, and this power's aim is to keep people in childhood permanently.

Then the sentence that does the real damage (paraphrased): this power does not break wills; it softens them, bends them, directs them. It rarely compels action but constantly obstructs it; it destroys nothing but prevents things from being born; it does not tyrannize, it hinders, represses, enervates, extinguishes — until the nation is reduced to "a flock of timid and industrious animals, of which the government is the shepherd." He is careful to show how this coexists with elections: every few years people emerge from their tutelage to choose a master, then withdraw into it again. The ballot does not cancel the guardianship; it lets people accept it with a clear conscience.

Why this is rated among the sharpest political forecasts of the 19th century: it describes no dictator at all, but the natural swelling of administrative centralization — an accumulation of permits, regulations, provisions and subsidies, each individually defensible, which together turn citizens into permanent dependants. And he draws a distinction that is still constantly missed: governmental centralization (who decides the nation's great affairs) and administrative centralization (who runs local detail) are entirely different things. The first any strong state needs; the second is what kills freedom. Once local matters are all drawn upward, people lose the arena in which self-government is practised — and therefore the capacity to resist.

His conclusion: the real risk in a democratic society is not that someone will seize power, but that people will hand it over gladly, in exchange for not having to bother.

The passion for equality, and restlessness amid plenty

Two final ideas explain why people would hand it over. The first is that the passion for equality runs deeper than the passion for freedom. Tocqueville sees why: freedom's benefits are indirect, delayed, and expensive — meetings, arguments, responsibility — while equality's pleasures are immediate and felt every day. Hence (paraphrased): "They want equality in freedom, and if they cannot have it, they want it in slavery still." Most people can bear everyone being low together; what they cannot bear is the man beside them standing higher.

The second is his famous observation about the American temper: why are men who inhabit the freest and most prosperous condition on earth so strangely melancholy and restless in the midst of abundance? His explanation is a side effect of equality. Aristocracy came with a ceiling, and a ceiling is a form of "enough" — you did not measure yourself against a duke. Equality removes the ceiling; in principle anyone may rise; so everyone is now measuring against everyone. Hope becomes unlimited while capacity stays finite, and the gap that never closes is the modern person's permanent dissatisfaction. Written in 1840 — a century and a half before any book about status anxiety or social-media comparison, and cutting deeper than most of them.

The Argument, Condensed

The whole book compresses into one line of reasoning:

(1) Equality of conditions is an irreversible, providential movement — the premise everything else rests on. → (2) Equality dissolves every intermediate rank, turning a society of chains into a heap of sand. → (3) That produces two consequences. Horizontally: since no one defers to any particular authority, everyone defers to majority opinion, and independent thought suffocates. Vertically: everyone retreats into a private circle (individualism), and public space empties. → (4) The emptied space is necessarily filled by the state, which grows into an unprecedented, mild, tutelary despotism — and people, loving equality more than freedom and comfort more than trouble, walk in of their own accord. → (5) The way out cannot be a return to aristocracy (irreversible) and cannot be a well-drafted constitution (South America tried). It can only be the deliberate reconstruction of intermediate layers under conditions of equality: townships to practise on, associations to re-bind the sand, religion to set limits on desire, juries and lawyers as brakes, and self-interest rightly understood to make public participation an account that ordinary people can balance.

So the book proves a conditional, not an anthem: equality is coming; freedom is not a gift that comes with it, but something that has to be re-invented, laboriously, under the new conditions. America is worth studying not because it is admirable but because at that moment it was the one place where the invention had actually been made to work. Volume One is mostly about how the machinery runs — federalism, localities, courts, parties, the press. Volume Two is about what equality does to people — their ideas, feelings, families, literature, ambition and unease. Volume Two is where his thought goes deep.

Misreadings & Honest Criticism

  • Misreading 1: that this is a book praising America. It is a warning written for Frenchmen. His position is that democracy is inevitable and must therefore be prevented from destroying liberty; the weight of the book is diagnostic. America is the laboratory, not the idol.
  • Misreading 2: reading "tyranny of the majority" as vote-counting. His real target is non-coercive social pressure smothering thought — the law grants you speech and you still don't speak.
  • Misreading 3: taking "individualism" as praise. He minted the word to name a pathology: letting go of public life calmly and respectably.
  • Misreading 4: the most-quoted line of all. "America is great because she is good; if America ceases to be good, she will cease to be great" — this is not in the book. It is a 20th-century misattribution, long since documented by Tocqueville scholars. Which is itself the best evidence that this book is quoted far more often than it is read.
  • Criticism 1: thin evidence. Nine months, mostly in the Northeast, with conclusions drawn largely from conversations with elites — lawyers, clergy, politicians — and from his own deduction. What he depicts is Jacksonian, white, New England-inflected America of the 1830s, not America as such.
  • Criticism 2: the "two Tocquevilles" problem. Scholars (Seymour Drescher's well-known argument among them) note that the 1835 volume is empirical, concrete and comparatively hopeful while the 1840 volume is abstract, deductive and dark — almost two different books. Much of Volume Two derives sweeping claims from the single premise of equality, which makes them hard to falsify.
  • Criticism 3: slavery and the native nations. He does devote a long chapter to the "three races," condemns slavery outright, foresees with accuracy that it will tear the Union apart, and leaves a cold-eyed record of Indian removal. But he still treats all of it as adjacent to his account of American democracy rather than structural to it — a point modern historians press hard.
  • Criticism 4: aristocratic nostalgia. Marxists and many social historians argue that his anxiety about equality blinded him to the genuinely new inequality of the 19th century: industrial capital. (In fairness, he has one strikingly prescient passage warning that manufacturing could breed one of the harshest aristocracies in history — he simply did not pursue it.)
  • Criticism 5: weaponized by both sides. The right quotes him against big government; the left quotes him on civil society and association; each takes its half. His own position is more awkward and more honest: a man who believed neither that the old order could return nor that the new one would come right by itself.

Tocqueville Beyond This Book

Read only this book and you miss the major blow of his later life: The Old Regime and the Revolution (1856). It takes the same problem from the other end. Where Democracy in America asks how a society of equals can stay free, The Old Regime asks why France failed to. Two of its findings are still quoted constantly:

First: centralization was not produced by the Revolution — it was inherited by it. Everyone assumed the Revolution had swept the old order away. Tocqueville went through the administrative archives and found that the machinery — nobles stripped of function, local power drawn upward, everything run by officials in Paris — had been built piece by piece by the monarchy itself. The Revolution merely took possession and reinforced it. That is why France could change regimes so many times while its administrative structure barely moved. It is also the historical specimen of exactly the thing he feared most in Democracy in America.

Second: what later writers named the Tocqueville effect — revolutions break out not when oppression is heaviest but when it starts to lift. The regions that rose first, he observed, were the least burdened and most prosperous. Because suffering endured as the natural order is endured; the moment someone shows it could be otherwise, the same suffering becomes intolerable. Expectations rise faster than conditions, and the gap is the fuel. It is the same mechanism he used in Democracy in America to explain restlessness amid plenty — which is the real key to him: his lifelong subject was never America or France but a single question, what equality does to the human heart, with political upheaval and private disquiet as the same phenomenon developed at two different scales.

Ten Sentences

1. His "democracy" is not a voting system but equality of conditions as a social state — irreversible as fate, and it remakes law, religion, family, art, ambition and unease alike.

2. Equality is not freedom and can be its enemy: it dismantles every layer standing between the individual and the state, turning a society of chains into a heap of sand.

3. The thing to fear in a democratic age is not a tyrant but majority opinion — the old tyranny struck the body and made you a martyr; this one never touches you, it just ensures no one speaks to you again (paraphrased).

4. "I know of no country where there is so little independence of mind and real freedom of discussion as in America" (paraphrased) — the law can grant speech while social fear keeps mouths shut.

5. Of the three things that sustain a democracy, circumstance matters least, laws next, and mœurs most: South America copied the U.S. constitution word for word and harvested coups.

6. "Municipal institutions are to liberty what primary schools are to knowledge" (paraphrased) — freedom is drilled by managing roads and schools; hand a ballot to someone who has never handled anything near him, and he won't know what it's for.

7. "Individualism" is his coinage and it is an accusation: not passionate selfishness but a calm, considered retreat — I'll mind my own circle and leave society to itself; and what is vacated always gets occupied.

8. The antidote is association: in democratic countries the science of association is the mother of all sciences (paraphrased) — the only known craft for gluing sand back together and letting people see their fates are joined.

9. He forecast an oppression the old vocabulary couldn't name: a tutelary power, absolute, detailed, regular, far-seeing and mild, that keeps a people in permanent childhood; it does not break wills, it softens them (paraphrased) — while every few years they come out to choose a master and go back in.

10. People end up there because they love equality more than they love freedom: "they want equality in freedom, and if they cannot have it, they want it in slavery still" (paraphrased). Freedom was never equality's gift — it has to be invented all over again, the hard way, under the new conditions.
