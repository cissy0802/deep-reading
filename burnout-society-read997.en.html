The Burnout Society

Müdigkeitsgesellschaft · Byung-Chul Han · 2010 | with Psychopolitics (Psychopolitik, 2014) folded in

中文 →

In One Sentence

What wears people down today is no longer an outside power telling them "you may not," but the voice inside saying "you can": once the command takes the form of freedom, each of us becomes our own overseer, exploiter and exploited fused into one, and depression, burnout and scattered attention become the signature illnesses of the age.

Where It Sits

Han was born in Seoul in 1959, studied metallurgy, then moved to Germany to study philosophy and theology, wrote his doctorate on Heidegger, and taught for years at the Berlin University of the Arts. He writes in German. The Burnout Society runs under a hundred pages in the original and made his name; since then he has published a similarly slim book almost every year, and Psychopolitics is the one that carries this line of thought into political economy. The best companion is Deep Read 12, Discipline and Punish: Han's starting move is to declare Foucault's "disciplinary society" obsolete.

The Core Argument

  • The illnesses of the age have changed: from immunological illness, caused by an invading Other, to neuronal illness, caused by an excess of the Same. The twentieth century's enemy came from outside; the twenty-first century's sickness comes from internal overload: too many stimuli, too much information, too many options and demands on oneself.
  • Disciplinary society has become achievement society, and "you should" has become "you can." This is not looser but harsher: a prohibition has limits, "can" has none; exploitation by others provokes resistance, while exploitation of oneself leaves nobody to resist.
  • Neoliberalism no longer represses freedom; it rules by exploiting it (Psychopolitics). Power no longer needs to forbid. It only needs to seduce, motivate, and get you to hand over your data and your energy willingly. The moment you feel freest is the moment you are being managed most thoroughly.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Neuronal violence: from the immunological paradigm to an excess of positivity

What it is. The book opens, roughly, by saying that every age has its signature afflictions. Antibiotics ended the bacterial age; immunology was the answer to the viral age; but the early twenty-first century is defined, pathologically, neither by bacteria nor by viruses but by neuronal illness. He names depression, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), borderline personality disorder and burnout syndrome. These, he says, are not infections but infarctions: nothing foreign has broken in; something internal has seized up.

The immunological paradigm. The immune system runs on a binary: self versus non-self. Identify the foreigner, then attack it. Han argues that the whole twentieth century, from the two blocs of the Cold War to the vocabulary of borders, spies and "defense," was organized on this logic. He calls it negativity: the Other provokes a reaction precisely because it is "not me."

Why the paradigm no longer fits. With globalization, the unsettling Stranger is increasingly replaced by the consumable Exotic: the tourist travels the world without ever really encountering the Other, simply absorbing difference as a commodity (paraphrased). Difference no longer triggers an immune response. So the new violence comes not from the Other but from an excess of the Same, which Han calls the violence of positivity: overproduction, overachievement, overcommunication. An immune system can fight a foreign enemy, but it has no defense against too much of a good thing, just as the body mounts no immune response to too much sugar.

How it changes the way you see. We tend to ask "who is oppressing me?", as if the cause of suffering must be an enemy. Han points to another possibility: what crushes you may be no single bad thing, but a pile of things that are each perfectly fine: one more opportunity, one more group chat, one more course worth taking. None of them is an enemy, so there is nothing to push against.

2. From disciplinary society to achievement society: "should" becomes "can"

What it is. Foucault's disciplinary society was made of hospitals, asylums, prisons, barracks and factories, producing "docile bodies" through prohibition, norms and surveillance (see Deep Read 12). Han's verdict is that this world has long since been replaced by another, roughly a society of fitness studios, office towers, banks, airports, shopping malls and genetic labs. He calls it achievement society (Leistungsgesellschaft), and its inhabitants achievement-subjects (Leistungssubjekte): no longer "obedience-subjects" but entrepreneurs of themselves.

The mechanism: the modal verb changes. The grammar of disciplinary society is negative: "may not," "should," "must." The grammar of achievement society is positive: "can." Han takes Obama's campaign slogan, "Yes, we can," as the emblem of the age: projects, initiative and motivation replace prohibition, commandment and law (paraphrased). Disciplinary society produced madmen and criminals; achievement society produces depressives and losers.

The key step: "can" doesn't abolish "should"; it swallows it. Han is explicit that the achievement-subject still carries a disciplinary substrate: the "should" has been internalized and is now issued by the self to the self. Why switch grammars at all? Because past a certain level of productivity, prohibition itself becomes a brake on output: someone under orders does what is required; someone who believes "I can" keeps raising the bar. The move from "should" to "can" is not a rupture but an upgrade.

How it changes the way you see. If nobody is pushing you and you still can't stop, that may not be "self-discipline." It may be discipline installed inside you.

3. Self-exploitation: master and slave in one person

What it is. The achievement-subject feels free; no boss stands behind it with a whip. But Han says that here freedom and constraint coincide: the achievement-subject surrenders to the compulsion to maximize performance, and that compulsion wears the face of freedom. The exploiter is simultaneously the exploited; perpetrator and victim can no longer be told apart (paraphrased). He borrows Hegel's master-slave dialectic: here master and slave are the same person, a master who is himself a slave working for himself.

The mechanism: why exploiting yourself beats being exploited. Han's claim is that auto-exploitation is more efficient than allo-exploitation (exploitation by another), because it comes with a feeling of freedom. Do the accounting: external exploitation needs supervisors, costs money to monitor, and meets foot-dragging and revolt. Self-exploitation has zero monitoring cost and zero friction; the worker puts in the overtime, learns the new skill and answers weekend email unprompted, calling all of it "investing in myself." The most efficient control is the kind the controlled experience as self-realization.

The consequence: resistance loses its target. If your boss squeezes you, you can strike, quit or hate him. If the squeezing comes from your own "I could do better," whom do you protest against? Psychopolitics pushes this into politics: those who fail no longer question the system; they feel responsible and ashamed. So instead of revolution, there is depression (paraphrased). Class struggle becomes each person's civil war with themselves.

How it changes the way you see. Once the line between employee and entrepreneur blurs, many people audit their own hours with a capitalist's eye: sleep must be high-quality, leisure must be productive, hobbies should ideally monetize. It is worth asking: am I living freely, or freely managing myself?

4. Depression: no longer being able to be able

What it is. Han's central diagnosis is that depression is the sickness of a society suffering from an excess of positivity (paraphrased). His interlocutor here is the French sociologist Alain Ehrenberg, who argued in The Weariness of the Self (La Fatigue d'être soi, 1998) that the spread of depression tracks a shift in what society demands, from "obey the rules" to "be yourself, take initiative"; depression is the exhaustion of someone who must become their own master and finds the task too heavy.

What Han accepts and where he pushes further. He agrees with Ehrenberg's direction but faults him for staying at the psychological level of "being oneself" and missing the economic logic underneath: what makes people ill is not the demand to be oneself as such, but being oneself once it is plugged into the logic of achievement, meaning the self has to become a project of continuous value growth. A "can" with no ceiling means it is never enough; there is always a little more.

The mechanism: "no longer being able to be able." When the achievement-subject has spent itself in "can," depression arrives as, roughly, no longer being able to be able. And then it blames no one but itself: in a world where everyone "can," not being able is your personal failure. The depressive is the achievement-subject at war with itself, finally defeated by itself. Burnout is the more visible form of the same process: someone who used themselves as fuel finally runs out.

The doping society. Near the end Han notes that achievement society drifts toward a doping society: once drugs that boost cognition and focus are normalized, the person is treated as a machine whose performance must be optimized, and even fatigue becomes a malfunction to be medicated away (paraphrased). For him this is no solution, only the same logic pushed further: not being allowed to be tired is itself the root of the illness.

How it changes the way you see. The usual advice to someone depressed is "exercise more, adjust your mindset, get more efficient," all of it still spoken in the grammar of "can." Han's angle is that at least some depression is not individual maladjustment but a social structure projected onto an individual as pathology. That does not replace medical treatment, but it changes how you understand your own collapse.

5. Hyperattention and profound boredom: multitasking as regression

What it is. On the level of attention, the excess of positivity shows up as what Han calls hyperattention: rapid switching among tasks, information sources and processes. Multitasking is usually praised as an advanced skill; he argues the opposite: multitasking is not civilizational progress but regression. Animals in the wild must multitask: an animal eating has to keep watch for predators, and cannot sink into any one thing (paraphrased).

The contrast: deep attention and profound boredom. Against this stands deep attention, a contemplative attention that can stay with one object for a long time. Han credits humanity's cultural achievements, philosophy, art and science, to this kind of attention, and one of its seedbeds is profound boredom. He quotes Walter Benjamin (the German critic; the line comes from his essay "The Storyteller"): "Boredom is the dream bird that hatches the egg of experience." A bird needs stillness to brood, and the slightest rustling in the leaves drives it off; Benjamin was lamenting that such still moments were vanishing from modern life. Han adds that today's hyperattention leaves us no tolerance for boredom, so the bird never settles.

Nietzsche: learning to see. Han draws on Nietzsche's three tasks of education in Twilight of the Idols: learning to see, learning to think, learning to speak and write. Learning to see means accustoming the eye to calm and patience, letting things come to it; not reacting immediately to a stimulus, but mastering the instincts that inhibit and exclude (paraphrased). For Nietzsche, the inability not to respond to every stimulus is itself a sickness, a symptom of exhaustion. From this Han distinguishes two kinds of power: positive potency, the power to do something, and negative potency, the power not to do: to refuse a stimulus, to stop. Achievement society recognizes only the first, and a person who has only "can" and no "can not" is like a car with an accelerator and no brakes.

How it changes the way you see. Scattered attention is not just a self-control problem; it is the output of a cultural form: an environment that will not tolerate boredom is bound to produce people who cannot think deeply. "Doing nothing" stops looking like waste and starts looking like a precondition for deep thought.

6. Two kinds of tiredness: Handke and the tiredness that trusts the world

What it is. The German title means "the tiredness society," yet Han's ending is not pure indictment; he also tries to turn tiredness into a positive image. He borrows from the Austrian writer Peter Handke (Nobel laureate in literature, 2019) and his Essay on Tiredness (Versuch über die Müdigkeit, 1989), which distinguishes different tirednesses.

The first: tiredness that divides. The tiredness of achievement society is solitary and divisive: the exhausted person withdraws into themselves, has nothing to say, pushes even those nearby away. Handke describes lovers too tired to speak to each other. Han says this is the tiredness of spent positive potency: it leaves you unable to do anything and cut off from others (paraphrased).

The second: the tiredness that trusts the world. Against it stands what Handke calls fundamental tiredness: you are tired, but not hollowed out, rather loosened. The self shrinks, the world grows, and you can "see" again: a child, a leaf, the person sitting next to you. Handke calls it a tiredness that trusts the world. This is the tiredness of negative potency: no longer gripping, no longer chasing, and so, for the first time, making room to simply be with others. Han links it to the Sabbath: a day of not-doing, not a rest taken so as to be more productive next week (paraphrased).

Why it matters. This step moves the book from diagnosis toward a way out, and an unusual one: not better time management, not "work-life balance" (which still aims at working more effectively), but restoring the value of not-doing itself. Rest is not for recharging; the recharging metaphor already treats you as a battery.

7. Psychopolitics: ruling through freedom (from Psychopolitics)

What it is. The Burnout Society is about the individual illness; Psychopolitics asks what kind of rule produces it. Han's answer, roughly: neoliberalism is a highly efficient, indeed intelligent, system for exploiting freedom. It does not oppose freedom; it turns everything that belongs to freedom, emotion, play, communication, self-expression, into productive force.

From biopolitics to psychopolitics. Late Foucault introduced biopolitics: the modern state takes a population's birth rates, health and lifespan as objects of government, so power acts on bodies and populations. Han thinks this no longer suffices. Neoliberal productivity comes less from the body than from the mind, so power's target shifts from the body to the psyche, and biopolitics becomes psychopolitics. The factory wanted your muscle; today's economy wants your motivation, emotions, creativity and attention.

Smart power. Disciplinary power says "no"; it works by forbidding and punishing, and so it provokes resistance. Psychopolitical power is friendly: it does not forbid but seduces; it doesn't silence you but encourages you to express, share, comment and like. It does not oppose your will; it shapes it, so that you want what it wants you to want. Han has a barbed line to the effect that the "like" is the digital amen.

The dictatorship of transparency and the digital panopticon. Foucault discussed the panopticon (Bentham's ring-shaped prison, where inmates could be seen at any moment from a central tower and so policed themselves). Han says the digital panopticon goes further: its inmates are not forced into visibility but display themselves eagerly, volunteering their lives, locations and moods. Transparency is hailed as a virtue, but for Han the demand that everything be transparent is itself a dictatorship, abolishing secrecy, distance and whatever cannot be calculated (a theme he had already developed in The Transparency Society, 2012).

Big Data as a psychopolitical instrument. Han argues that Big Data does more than record the past: it lets power read desires and tendencies that people are not even aware of themselves, and so predict and steer behavior. Power can thereby intervene before consciousness does (paraphrased).

The way out: idiotism. The book's final chapter is called "Idiotism." The Greek root of "idiot" originally meant a private person, someone who stays out of public affairs. Han's point, roughly, is that philosophy's great moments are often a kind of playing dumb, like Socrates professing that he knows nothing; today's idiot is the one who is not networked, does not communicate, is not transparent and cannot be fully computed, who refuses endless communication and the demand to be optimized, and keeps, in silence and solitude, a space that cannot be read (paraphrased). It is a stance, not a program.

The Skeleton of the Book

Compressed to a single line across both books: negative power (prohibition) → positive power (motivation) → power internalized as self-demand (self-exploitation) → the individual spent in an endless "can" (depression, burnout, fragmented attention) → domination completed in the form of freedom (psychopolitics) → the only gap lies in not-doing (negative potency, profound boredom, fundamental tiredness, idiotism).

How is it established? Mostly not with data, but with three resources. First, conceptual contrast: Foucault's disciplinary society, Hegel's master and slave, Benjamin's boredom, Nietzsche's learning to see and Handke's tiredness are each brought in and turned around. Second, pathological metaphor: the vocabulary of immunology, infarction and neurons is used to diagnose a society. Third, everyday recognition: readers find themselves in "I volunteer for overtime" and "I can't stop scrolling." The core insight really comes down to one sentence: when the external prohibition is replaced by an internal "can," pressure loses its limits, and it loses anyone to resist. Everything else, from depression to Big Data, unfolds that sentence on different levels.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: Han is telling you to stop trying and lie flat. What he criticizes is not effort but limitless self-exploitation carried out in the name of freedom. The vita contemplativa he champions (the contemplative life, the long-standing counterpart to the active life) also demands intense concentration; its direction is simply seeing rather than output.

Misreading two: all depression is socially caused. Han offers cultural diagnosis, not etiology. Depression has genetic, physiological and biographical causes; reading it purely as "a symptom of neoliberalism" may persuade someone who needs treatment that understanding is enough.

Criticism one: speculation over evidence. This is the most common and most forceful objection. The book has almost no data, no fieldwork, no epidemiology; it runs on aphoristic assertion and conceptual reversal. It can name a feeling, but it cannot tell you how widespread that feeling is or for whom it holds. Many reviewers call it a philosophical poem rather than a sociological report.

Criticism two: has depression actually increased? This is a live epidemiological debate. On one side, depression diagnoses and antidepressant prescriptions have risen sharply in many countries. On the other, estimates from the Global Burden of Disease study (a large international project that tracks disease prevalence across countries) suggest that age-standardized depression prevalence has stayed roughly flat over recent decades, with growth in total cases driven mainly by population growth and ageing. The sociologists Allan Horwitz and Jerome Wakefield argued in The Loss of Sadness (2007) that loosened diagnostic criteria have relabeled some normal sadness as depressive disorder. As for burnout, the World Health Organization's ICD-11 (the eleventh International Classification of Diseases) defines it as an "occupational phenomenon," not a medical condition. If the "age of depression" is partly a change in diagnosis and naming, Han's pathological starting point has to be discounted.

Criticism three: has "should" really disappeared? His achievement-subject looks like an urban professional or freelancer. For delivery riders, ride-hail drivers, factory workers and low-wage service staff, external coercion has not diminished at all: platform algorithms set routes and deadlines, a bad rating docks pay, and surveillance is finer-grained than anything in Foucault's factory. Calling self-exploitation the general form of the age risks passing off one class's experience as society's; for many people "you can" is just a sugar coating on "you must," and they know exactly who is giving the orders.

Criticism four: an oversimplified Foucault. In his 1979 Collège de France lectures, The Birth of Biopolitics, Foucault himself had already analyzed neoliberalism and described its subject as "an entrepreneur of himself"; Deleuze's short 1990 essay "Postscript on the Societies of Control" had likewise announced that disciplinary societies were giving way to societies of control. Critics therefore argue that "going beyond Foucault" mostly means targeting early Foucault, when late Foucault had already got close.

Criticism five: a romance of contemplation and tiredness. Profound boredom, fundamental tiredness and idiotism are lovely, but they require time, safety and financial slack, close to a luxury of the leisured. For someone working two jobs, "learning not to do" is not an option. And his ways out are almost all personal stances, with no institutional proposals on working hours, labor protections or platform regulation.

Criticism six: the author's own tension. A philosopher who publishes a book a year, lectures worldwide and is translated into dozens of languages, criticizing performance and overproduction, invites the charge of irony; his slim books are also perfectly suited to quick consumption in fragmented time. This doesn't refute the argument, but it is a reminder that a critique of achievement society can become one of its better-selling products.

Han Beyond This Book

Beyond The Burnout Society and Psychopolitics, his run of short books circles two themes.

First, the vanishing of the Other. The Transparency Society (Transparenzgesellschaft, 2012) argues that transparency, which looks like a democratic virtue, actually makes everything comparable, calculable and displayable, draining things of depth and distance. The Agony of Eros (Agonie des Eros, 2012) holds that love needs a genuinely different Other who cannot be possessed, while the achievement-subject sees only its own reflection in others, so love shrinks into consumption and comparison. The Expulsion of the Other (Die Austreibung des Anderen, 2016) generalizes the line. All of these grow from the same trunk as this book's diagnosis of an excess of the Same.

Second, the East and not-doing. Early on he wrote Philosophy of Zen Buddhism (Philosophie des Zen-Buddhismus, 2002), setting Zen ideas such as emptiness and no-self against Western thinkers like Heidegger and Hegel; in The Scent of Time (Duft der Zeit, 2009) he ties the fragmentation of time to the loss of the contemplative life. The negative potency and contemplation this book champions are of a piece with his long interest in the "nothing" of East Asian thought.

Crossovers

  • Eastern philosophy · Zhuangzi's "usefulness of the useless": In the Zhuangzi, a sacred oak escapes the axe because its wood is worthless, and so grows enormous. Alike in treating "not being usable" as self-preservation; unlike in that Zhuangzi aims at preserving life, while Han is critiquing a socioeconomic order.
  • Buddhism · Craving (taṇhā): The Four Noble Truths trace suffering to craving, a grasping that is never satisfied. Alike in that "can" has no end and each gain sharpens the thirst; unlike in that Buddhism sees craving as a universal habit of mind, while Han assigns it to a specific historical regime.
  • Neuroscience · The default mode network: Around 2001 the neurologist Marcus Raichle and colleagues described a set of brain regions that become more active when we are off-task and mind-wandering: the default mode network, involved in self-reflection, memory and imagining the future, and linked by later studies to creative association. It looks like a neural footing for profound boredom: the brain is not idle when we "do nothing." The disanalogy: an overactive default mode network is also tied to rumination in depression, so idleness is not automatically good; what matters is its texture.
  • Psychology · Self-determination theory: Deci and Ryan distinguish external regulation from introjected regulation: acting out of guilt or pressure on self-esteem, where the rule has moved inside but is still not genuinely endorsed. That is almost exactly an internalized "should"; the difference is that the theory is empirically measured and holds that real autonomy is attainable.
  • Distributed systems · Backpressure: A service without backpressure accepts every request until its queues overflow and the whole thing cascades into failure; a healthy system must be able to refuse, rate-limit and shed load. That is negative potency in engineering form. The difference: a server's overload comes from outside traffic, while most of the achievement-subject's requests are sent to itself.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. Every age has its illness: the twentieth century's was immunological, an invading Other; the twenty-first's is neuronal, an excess of the Same.

2. What crushes people is often not an enemy but a pile of things that are each "fine": too many stimuli, too much information, too many opportunities and demands on oneself.

3. Disciplinary society says "you should," achievement society says "you can"; the second is harsher, because a prohibition has limits and "can" has none.

4. The achievement-subject is its own master and its own slave; self-exploitation beats exploitation by others because it comes with a feeling of freedom.

5. Exploited by others, you can resist; exploited by yourself, you are left with shame. So instead of revolution, there is depression (paraphrased).

6. Depression is the sickness of a society suffering from too much positivity: where everyone "can," no longer being able counts as personal failure.

7. Multitasking is regression, not progress; boredom is the dream bird that hatches the egg of experience, and an environment that won't tolerate boredom produces no deep thought (Benjamin).

8. We need the power not to do: not reacting at once to a stimulus is where seeing and thinking begin (Nietzsche's "learning to see").

9. One tiredness isolates; another loosens you and lets you see the world and the people beside you again, and that one is worth keeping (Handke).

10. Neoliberalism doesn't repress freedom, it rules through it: the power that never needs to forbid you is the hardest power to notice.
