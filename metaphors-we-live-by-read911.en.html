Metaphors We Live By

Metaphors We Live By · George Lakoff & Mark Johnson · 1980

中文 →

In one sentence

You think metaphor is a flourish for poets and copywriters, decoration on the surface of language — this book says the opposite: metaphor is first a matter of thought, not of wording. The whole conceptual system we rely on to grasp abstract things (time, argument, love, ideas, emotion) is itself largely built out of a more concrete, more bodily concept; the metaphors you utter are just the tip of that hidden conceptual structure breaking the surface. Once you see it, you suddenly notice the skeleton of thought inside the most ordinary everyday speech.

Where it sits

One author is a linguist (Lakoff, UC Berkeley), the other a philosopher (Johnson, University of Oregon) — and this slim 1980 book is the founding text of "cognitive linguistics" and of the later "embodied cognition" movement. Its "conceptual metaphor theory" hauled metaphor out of the scrap-heap of rhetoric and set it in the middle of cognitive science and philosophy. The book is thin, example-packed, almost jargon-free, and effortless to read — yet what it overturns is not small: it directly challenges the two-thousand-year-old Western default that reason is abstract and disembodied. Lakoff later pushed the insight all the way into politics (his theory of "framing," Don't Think of an Elephant), and its root is here.

The core claims

  • Metaphor is a mode of thought, not a decoration on language. "The concepts that govern our thought are metaphorical in nature" (paraphrased) — we don't just use metaphor to talk about things, we use it to think about and experience them.
  • Abstract concepts are built out of concrete bodily experience. Intangibles like time, argument, and emotion can only be grasped through things we can see, touch, and do — "war," "money," "up/down," "a journey" — which is why metaphor is indispensable.
  • Every metaphor highlights one facet and hides another — and can, in turn, shape reality. The metaphor you frame something with determines which side of it you see, which side you miss, and even how you treat it.

The core concepts, one by one

Conceptual metaphor and "systematicity": ARGUMENT IS WAR

The book's first cornerstone is redefining "metaphor" as a conceptual metaphor — not a pretty phrase but a whole mapping that "understands one conceptual domain in terms of another." Their signature case is "ARGUMENT IS WAR." Look at how we ordinarily talk about arguing: your claims are indefensible; he attacked every weak point in my argument; his criticisms were right on target; I demolished his argument; I've never won an argument with him; you disagree? OK, shoot. Defense, attack, targets, winning, losing, positions — all the vocabulary of war.

The key insight is systematicity: these aren't a few coincidental turns of phrase but a whole structured mapping from the source domain "war" onto the target domain "argument" — the expressions arrive in flocks precisely because, in our heads, we really do treat an argument as a war. And it shapes not just wording but conduct: you cast your opponent as an enemy, you think in attack, defense, and winning, you feel wounded when you "lose." Lakoff and Johnson imagine: if a culture's metaphor were "ARGUMENT IS DANCE" — participants are partners, the goal is to perform a balanced, graceful dance — they would experience and carry out "argument" so differently that we might not even count it as arguing. The move is ruthless: it shows that the way of thinking we take as self-evidently given is quietly dictated by a metaphor.

Three kinds of metaphor: the three scaffolds that build the abstract world

They sort everyday metaphors into three rough types. One table makes it clear:

TypeWhat it doesExample
OrientationalGives an abstract concept a spatial orientation (up/down, in/out), anchored straight to the bodyHAPPY IS UP / SAD IS DOWN: my spirits rose, feeling down, sank into depression; MORE / GOOD / POWER is up
OntologicalTreats a shapeless abstraction as a discrete, countable "thing" or "container" you can act onTHE MIND IS A MACHINE (rusty today, wheels turning); treating "inflation" as an enemy to fight
StructuralUses one richly structured concrete concept to organize a whole abstract oneTIME IS MONEY (spend / save / waste / invest time); LOVE IS A JOURNEY; IDEAS ARE FOOD

From "give it an orientation" to "make it a thing" to "structure it wholesale" — each type builds a richer conceptual structure than the last.

Orientational metaphors sit closest to the body: HAPPY IS UP, SAD IS DOWN — spirits rise, moods lift, but you also feel down, low, sunk. Why up and down? Because it's grounded in bodily experience: a drooping posture goes with sadness, an erect one with vitality. The same family gives MORE IS UP (prices rise), GOOD IS UP, HAVING POWER IS UP (climb to the top, at the peak of power). Not arbitrary pairings — direct gifts of the body and the physical world.

Ontological metaphors solve "how do I get a grip on a thing with no shape?" — by treating it as a thing. The classic is THE MIND IS A MACHINE — "my mind isn't operating today," "I'm a little rusty," "the wheels are turning now" — or THE MIND IS A BRITTLE OBJECT — "he cracked up," "I'm going to pieces." Once inflation is spoken of as an entity, you can "fight it," "combat it," have it "back you into a corner" — it turns from an abstract economic phenomenon into an enemy you can declare war on.

Structural metaphors are the most powerful, because they import a whole structure. TIME IS MONEY: you're wasting my time, this gadget will save you hours, how do you spend your day, I've invested a lot of time in her, you're running out of time, is it worth your while. Lakoff and Johnson point out this metaphor isn't universal: it's a cultural product of industrial capitalism pricing labor by the hour — treating time as a finite, spendable resource, which in turn reinforces that way of living. Kin to it are LOVE IS A JOURNEY (look how far we've come, we're at a crossroads, this relationship is going nowhere, spinning our wheels, on the rocks) and IDEAS ARE FOOD ("food for thought," "raw facts," "half-baked ideas," let me "digest" that, a claim I "can't swallow").

A deeper mechanism hides here, called metaphorical entailment: a metaphor imports not just a few words but the source domain's whole logic of inference. Because TIME IS MONEY, and money is a finite, valuable, exhaustible resource, we automatically infer that time too is finite, that time is "precious," that time can "run out," that wasting it is a "loss" — inferences that are not our independent observations of time but are piped in from the source domain "money." This is metaphor's real power: it doesn't hand you a phrase, it quietly hands you a whole set of default rules for how to reason about and keep accounts on the concept. You think you're reasoning about time on its own terms; in fact you've been costing out time on money's ledger all along.

Highlighting and hiding: every metaphor is a lens with a prescription

This is the book's most practically lethal point. Any metaphor, in lighting up one facet of a thing, necessarily hides another. ARGUMENT IS WAR highlights the adversarial, win-or-lose side of arguing and completely hides the side where it could be "cooperation, jointly figuring something out." Wear that lens long enough and you genuinely forget that an argument could be anything but combat.

They give a subtler example — the conduit metaphor (named by the linguist Michael Reddy): we nearly always think of communication as transferring objects — ideas are objects, words are containers that hold them, communicating is sending those containers. So we say "it's hard to get that idea across," "your meaning came through," "pack more thought into fewer words," "the meaning is right there in the words." What does it hide? It hides that meaning depends heavily on context and must be reconstructed by the listener — it fools us into believing that "if the words are clear, the meaning is automatically packed inside them and delivered intact to the other head," which is exactly why we get indignant at "I said it perfectly clearly, how could you misread it?" Seeing what a metaphor hides is often more useful than seeing what it highlights.

But "hiding" is not a "prison." Lakoff and Johnson also note: precisely because you can see what a metaphor hides, you get the chance to change lenses — deliberately reframe a disagreement from "war" to "solving a problem together," and the participants turn from opponents into teammates, and what can be heard at the table shifts with them. This is the book's most actionable lesson: you can't think without metaphor, but you can choose which metaphor — and that choice is often the watershed between seeing another possibility and not.

Metaphor is rooted in the body: a hard blow to "objectivism"

Here is the book's real philosophical ambition. If even the most abstract concepts are built out of bodily experience (up/down, hot/cold, containers, paths, force), then meaning is not a free-floating "objective truth" independent of humans; it is rooted in this body and this culture's experience. From there they fire in two directions at once: at objectivism — the mainstream Western myth that "truth is absolute, independent of human understanding, and the world comes with one correct, perspective-free description" — and at naive subjectivism — "it's all just personal feeling, there's no true or false." They propose a third road, experientialism: truth is relative to a conceptual system, and that system is not arbitrary — it is firmly anchored in shared human bodily experience. Neither a God's-eye absolute truth nor "anything goes" — and this step is the philosophical starting gun for the whole later current of embodied cognition.

Worth noting: they don't hold that all metaphors are equally "solid." One like HAPPY IS UP has a direct experiential basis — rooted in a bodily feeling everyone shares (dejection is the whole body slumping), so it's highly consistent across cultures and nearly inescapable; one like TIME IS MONEY, by contrast, plainly bears the stamp of a particular society, culturally chosen and changeable. This distinction is crucial: it keeps the theory from sliding into "it's all arbitrary convention" relativism — some metaphors are forced on us by the body, others merely selected by a culture; the former are near-universal, the latter can be swapped.

New metaphors can create new realities

If metaphor can shape how we see and act, then changing the metaphor can change reality. Their example: when a society frames "labor" as a "resource" (minable, measurable, exhaustible like coal or oil), we comfortably attend only to its quantity and ignore whether the people doing the labor are living well — the metaphor hides the human beings. Conversely, once a group adopts a new metaphor, it really does change their actions and institutions. Lakoff later grew this thread into the political theory of "framing": political fights are really fights over "whose metaphorical frame defines the issue" — the moment you accept your opponent's metaphor (even to argue against it), you're already fighting on their turf ("Don't think of an elephant" — the more you try not to, the more you do). So metaphor never merely describes the world; it is quietly in the business of making it.

And the most easily overlooked, most crucial thing: the metaphors that govern us are mostly "dead" — so dead we don't feel they're metaphors at all. "He attacked my argument" carries no sense of "figure of speech" for you; it's simply the literal, "of course you'd say it that way" phrasing — and that very invisibility is what makes it most powerful: a frame you can't even detect is the frame you're least able to question and most likely to swallow whole. A poet's fresh, striking metaphor you spot at once and pause to savor; what actually rules your everyday thinking are these dead metaphors, sunk below the surface of language, long since invisible to you. And that is the whole point of this book: to fish them out one by one and force you to "see" again how you think.

The load-bearing skeleton

Here's the one thing this slim book keeps circling, said once and clearly: what it argues, and what it argues from.

Its claim is a common-sense-overturning line: the vast majority of abstract thought is metaphorical — we systematically build the intangible concepts out of concrete, bodily ones; and these metaphors, sourced from the body, highlight one facet and hide another, and reshape our perception and action in return. What does it argue from? From the sheer systematicity of everyday language. Its method is as simple as it is bold: line up, string after string, the expressions we take for granted under concepts like "argument," "time," and "love," and let you see with your own eyes that they aren't isolated dead metaphors but a living system pouring out of a single source domain — the systematicity of the language exposes the metaphorical structure of the thought behind it. The book's force is exactly this: the evidence is in the words you reach for every day, and once it's pointed out, you can never again pretend not to see it.

Common misreadings & criticism

  • Don't read it as a rhetoric manual on "how to write good figures of speech": its claim is the opposite — metaphor is first a cognitive structure and only second a linguistic phenomenon; it cares not about clever tropes but about the everyday expressions so dead you never noticed they were metaphors at all.
  • The methodological soft spot (the thing to know most): the whole book rests on the authors introspectively citing language examples, not systematic experimental data; critics note the examples can look cherry-picked, the claim is hard to falsify, and there's a "direction of causation" problem — does the metaphor shape the thought, or merely reflect / describe it? Language examples alone can't settle that.
  • Pinker's rebuttal: Steven Pinker (The Stuff of Thought) grants that metaphor is real and pervasive but rejects the strong version — he argues that beneath the metaphors lies a layer of non-metaphorical, abstract conceptual scaffolding (time, cause, intention); and the fact that we can evaluate, reject, and remake a metaphor itself shows we aren't wholly imprisoned by it. Pushing "all reason is metaphor" to the limit overshoots.
  • To be fair, though — later experiments partly caught it: cognitive scientists such as Boroditsky have shown that metaphors do change reasoning — frame crime as a "beast" and people lean toward catching and punishing; frame it as a "virus" and they lean toward tracing social roots and prevention — even though both groups read the same data and afterward believe they were being purely "objective." Such work suggests that the line this book shouted in 1980 on the strength of linguistic intuition — "metaphor shapes thought" — was broadly right; only the missing half of the evidence was supplied later.

The distillation in ten sentences

① Metaphor is first a matter of thought, not wording: we don't just use it to talk about the world, we use it to think about and experience the world.

② Abstract concepts (time, argument, love, emotion) are intangible, so we can only build them out of concrete bodily experience (war, money, up/down, a journey) — which is why metaphor is indispensable.

③ The signature case, ARGUMENT IS WAR: indefensible claims, attacking weak points, demolishing, winning/losing — this whole set arrives systematically because we really do treat argument as war.

④ Swap cultures to "ARGUMENT IS DANCE" and the whole experience of arguing changes — the thought we take as self-evident is quietly dictated by a metaphor.

⑤ Three types: orientational (happy is up / sad is down, anchored in the body), ontological (treat inflation as an enemy to fight), structural (time is money: spend / save / waste / invest).

⑥ "TIME IS MONEY" isn't a universal truth but a product of industrial capitalism pricing labor by the hour — and the metaphor reshapes that way of living in return.

⑦ Every metaphor highlights one facet and hides another: ARGUMENT IS WAR hides that arguing could be "cooperating to figure something out"; seeing what it hides is often more useful than seeing what it highlights.

⑧ If even abstract concepts are rooted in bodily experience, meaning is no human-independent "objective truth" — a blow that fells both objectivism and naive subjectivism, and the philosophical starting point of embodied cognition.

⑨ Change the metaphor and you change reality: framing "labor" as a "resource" hides the people doing it; political fights are really fights over whose metaphorical frame defines the issue.

⑩ The honest ledger: it runs on language examples, not experiments, and Pinker faults it for pushing "all is metaphor" too far; but later framing experiments (crime as beast vs. virus) show that its 1980 cry — "metaphor shapes thought" — pointed the right way.
