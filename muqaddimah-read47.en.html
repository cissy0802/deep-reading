The Muqaddimah

The Muqaddimah · Ibn Khaldun · 1377

中文 →

In One Sentence

This book does not ask who beat whom in which year; it asks why every regime eventually falls—and Ibn Khaldun's answer is that the thing which binds a group into a single striking force (he calls it ʿaṣabiyya) is spent by victory itself, so that rise, conquest, comfort, softening, and replacement by the next unblunted band form a curve that can be written down like a law of nature.

Where It Sits

Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406) was born in Tunis into a family of officials whose roots ran back to Seville. He spent his life moving between the courts of the Maghreb and al-Andalus as adviser, envoy, and judge: he was jailed, he switched patrons, he served as Granada's emissary to Castile, he ended his days as chief Maliki judge in Cairo, and he spent weeks in conversation with Timur outside the walls of a besieged Damascus. In 1377, sick of court life, he withdrew to a hilltop fortress in what is now Algeria and—by his own account—wrote the Muqaddimah in roughly five months. The word means simply "the introduction": it was the opening volume of a vast universal history, the Kitāb al-ʿIbar (Book of Lessons), and the introduction has long outlived the history it introduced. Its place is easy to fix: this is the first time on record that anyone announced that human society has laws of its own, that those laws can be studied as an independent science—and then actually wrote that science. Arnold Toynbee later called it the greatest work of its kind ever created by any mind in any time or place.

The Central Claims

One: a historian's real job is not to relay but to audit. Old chronicles are full of impossibilities not because liars are common but because historians only checked who reported the story to whom and never asked whether the event was possible given how human societies actually work. To ask that second question you need a theory of society—so history must be built on a new science.

Two: the power of every regime comes from ʿaṣabiyya—group feeling, the willingness of people to put themselves at risk for one another. Not virtue, not divine favor, not noble blood. Whoever has more of it beats opponents who are richer, more numerous, and better equipped.

Three: the fatal part is that ʿaṣabiyya destroys itself by succeeding. Victory brings settlement, wealth, and luxury; luxury makes people fond of their lives and careful with their money; rulers switch from kinsmen to paid soldiers and salaried officials; the willingness to die for one another rots from the inside. Dynasties are not knocked over by enemies—they lose the thing that held them up, and the enemy merely arrives to collect the body. Civilization is what group feeling is for, and it is also where group feeling goes to die.

The Core Concepts, One at a Time

1. ʿUmrān: he is inventing a discipline that does not exist

The Arabic word ʿumrān means roughly "the state of being inhabited, settled, cultivated"—think of it as "the fact of human beings living together" taken as a subject in its own right. That is what Ibn Khaldun proposes to study: why people must live in groups, what organization grows out of grouping, how organization hardens into power, how power grows cities and crafts, and how the whole thing rots. He knew perfectly well he was doing something nobody had done, and says so in the book: this is an independent science, with its own object (human social organization) and its own problems.

An analogy: before him, writing history was like keeping a weather diary—rain today, wind tomorrow, someone struck by lightning last year. Ibn Khaldun wanted meteorology. Not a record of which days it rained, but an account of the pressure and temperature conditions under which rain must fall. He even fenced off his new field: it is not political philosophy (he is not describing the ideal city, which was Plato's and al-Farabi's business) and it is not jurisprudence (he is not saying what ought to be). It is description—how societies in fact run.

Why it matters: this is the first full appearance of the idea that society has laws of its own, explicable without appeal to divine will or personal virtue—roughly four centuries before Montesquieu and five before Comte coined "sociology." It changes how you think in one specific way: the next time you hear that a country collapsed because its last ruler was a fool, you will instinctively ask whether the fool caused the collapse, or whether a state that far along the curve was bound to end up with a ruler like that.

2. ʿAṣabiyya: the thing that makes people willing to die for each other

This is the engine of the whole book and the word most easily wrecked in translation—rendered as "group feeling," "social cohesion," "solidarity," "esprit de corps." Its root has to do with binding and tying together. The accurate reading is: the automatic reflex within a group by which your business is my business and an attack on you draws my fist—strong enough that members will accept the risk of death for the group. Note that this is not "identification," which is a much weaker thing; identification gets you a repost, ʿaṣabiyya gets you someone stepping in front of a blade with nobody watching.

Where does it come from? Kinship is the classic source: cousins raised together, where an injury to one is known instantly to all. But Ibn Khaldun states plainly that blood is only the cheapest source, not the only one—long alliance, clientage, a foreign family adopted into a tribe, shared service to a house: all of these can generate the same bond, because prolonged close contact produces the effect that kinship produces (paraphrased). That point rescues the concept from being a theory of race or lineage and makes it a theory of how a human group is structured.

Why it matters: it yields a counterintuitive but extremely durable rule for reading conflicts. What decides a fight is not numbers, money, or equipment but how many people in a formation will hold their ground one second longer for the person beside them. Ibn Khaldun watched a few thousand tribal horsemen break dynasties with heavy tax revenues and standing armies, precisely because those armies were hired: mercenaries fight for wages, and wages do not buy that extra second. Modern military sociology keeps rediscovering the same fact—soldiers do not die for the flag, they die for the squad. Ibn Khaldun made it the first principle of political rise and fall in the fourteenth century.

How it changes your seeing: you begin auditing every organization for whether its group feeling is rising or falling—why a startup is most dangerous at thirty people and unable to move at three thousand with ten times the resources; why a department that has just been reorganized suddenly contains nobody willing to do one thing beyond their remit. You stop counting what a group has and start counting how much "I'll carry a bit extra for you" it has left in reserve.

3. Badāwa and ḥaḍāra: not good people and bad people, two sets of constraints

Ibn Khaldun divides human life into two modes. Badāwa—usually "desert life" or "rural life"—means groups living off herding and rough agriculture at the level of necessities (not only Bedouin, but also mountain people and villagers). Ḥaḍāra—"sedentary" or "urban civilization"—means city life with surplus, division of labor, crafts, and luxuries.

The crucial point is that this is not a moral contrast but a comparison of the pressures two environments apply. In the desert there are no walls, no government, no police; a person's safety is supplied entirely by kin—so those people necessarily maintain a high level of mutual defense and hardness, or they die. It is precisely the absence of order that forces the strongest group feeling into existence. In the city there are walls, garrisons, and courts; safety has been outsourced to institutions—so the capacity to die for one another atrophies from disuse, the way an unused muscle wastes. The city does not corrupt people; it stops requiring their hardness, and so the hardness goes.

The two are not opposed values but the two ends of a one-way street: one produces force, the other consumes it.

One implication is easy to miss: he thinks urban civilization is the thing worth wanting. Science, crafts, architecture, refinement all live in cities; the desert offers only scarcity. He does not romanticize nomads for a moment. The tragedy is exactly there: you need desert hardness to take the city, and once you live in the city that hardness is certain to be spent by the city itself.

4. Mulk: royal authority is where group feeling arrives, and its first killer

Mulk means royal authority—the power of one man to compel obedience from everyone. Group feeling, Ibn Khaldun says, naturally aims at it: a bonded group pushes upward until it has produced a ruler and a dynasty. Up to here the story is ascent.

The reversal comes the day power is won. A new ruler faces a structural trap: the kinsmen and comrades who carried him to the throne are precisely the only people capable of pulling him off it—they share the same group feeling, so why him and not one of them? Every founder therefore does the same thing: he weakens, purges, or sidelines his own original following and promotes rootless men who owe everything to him personally—slave soldiers, clients, eunuchs, hired bureaucrats. In the short run this is intelligent self-preservation. In substance it severs his own blood supply: the old following brought willingness to die for one another; the new one brings a monthly salary. From here the dynasty looks more centralized and more magnificent, and is hollow.

Why it matters: this converts "why founders always kill their generals" from a moral story (ingratitude) into a structural necessity (the same cohesion is both his strength and his threat). It also explains a recurring oddity: a regime is often at its most impressive exactly when it is most fragile—because the things making it impressive (standing armies, bureaucracies, monumental building) are substitutes bought to replace a force that has already gone.

5. Luxury and the four generations: a dynasty's actuarial table

Taraf means luxury, the habit of ease. Ibn Khaldun's treatment of it is not a sermon but an accounting exercise. The real damage luxury does is not that it "corrupts hearts" but that it raises the fixed costs of the whole ruling group while lowering the risk each member will accept. People who have grown used to comfort have too much to lose, so they run sooner in battle, evade taxes more, and haggle with each other more—while sustaining that comfort demands more money, more troops, more taxation. Revenue needs rise while the supply of people willing to bleed falls; the moment those two lines diverge, the countdown starts.

From this he derives his famous—and most contested—rule of thumb: a dynasty rarely outlasts four generations, with a generation reckoned at roughly forty years, hence about a hundred and twenty years.

GenerationIts relation to the founding forceHow it shows
First (the builders)Possesses it directly—brought in from the hard countryRough, tireless, staking their lives on one another
SecondSaw it firsthand, heard it from the foundersAlready settled and comfortable, but still remembers the cost
ThirdOnly tradition—a set of rules to be observedKeeps up appearances by imitation, fights by hiring others
FourthGone entirely—believes glory is inheritedDespises the ancestral discipline, spends until the end arrives

The four generations are Ibn Khaldun's actuarial table; he admits the span may be longer or shorter—the slope is the claim, not the number.

He also breaks the process into five stages (paraphrased): seizing power; the ruler taking sole control and purging his old following; ease and monument-building; contentment and imitation of predecessors; squandering and collapse. The sharpest judgment in it: the last ruler does not lose the realm because he is unusually incompetent—he is simply sitting at the end of the curve. Put the founder in the fourth generation's seat and he would likely lose it too.

How it changes your seeing: you start doing a generational audit of any institution. Are the people running it now the ones who built it, the ones who watched it being built, or the ones who only ever grew up inside the finished structure? A third-generation leadership that has never faced an existential threat is missing something no amount of intelligence can substitute for.

6. The fiscal death spiral: the curve he drew six centuries before Laffer

This is the passage modern economists quote most, and it is the financial engine of everything above. Ibn Khaldun's line runs roughly: "At the beginning of a dynasty, small assessments yield large revenues; at the end, large assessments yield small revenues."

The mechanism is plainly stated and not at all mysterious. Early rulers still carry desert habits: few wants, low expenses, light taxes—and light taxes leave farmers and merchants a margin, so they plant more and trade more, the base widens, and total revenue is high. Later, court expenses, mercenary payrolls, and official salaries all swell; the ruler can only raise rates; higher rates eat the producers' margin, so some stop planting, some stop trading, some leave; the base shrinks; and to cover the hole he raises rates again—collecting less with each increase. The last stage is the ruler entering commerce himself, monopolizing trades and competing with his own subjects (Ibn Khaldun devotes a section to how a trading ruler wrecks the market).

Supply-side economists adopted him as an ancestor in the 1970s, and Laffer's supporters have publicly credited him. But honesty requires a qualification: what he gives is a qualitative mechanism about extraction destroying the thing extracted, not a curve with an optimum you can compute—calling him the author of the Laffer curve is an upgrade he did not ask for.

In the same vein, he offers a strikingly modern judgment (paraphrased): profit is the value realized from human labor—the crafts and wealth of a city depend finally on how many people are concentrated there and how fine a division of labor that concentration can feed. Bigger city, finer craft; when the dynasty fails and the population scatters, the crafts are lost with it. He even uses this to explain why certain skills vanish from a region and never return: not because people got stupider, but because the social scale that could support the skill is gone.

7. Religion: a multiplier of group feeling, not a substitute for it

Ibn Khaldun was a devout Muslim judge, and his analysis of religion's political function is startlingly cool. A religious call (daʿwa), he argues, can do what no tribe can do alone: fuse several mutually hostile group feelings into one enormous one. Arab tribes raided each other for centuries; within decades of Islam's rise they overran two empires. For him this demonstrates a mechanism, not a miracle: religion removes internal envy and rivalry, points everyone the same way, and makes death weigh less.

But he attaches a decisive limit: religion is a multiplier, not an addend. Without an existing group feeling underneath it, preaching accomplishes nothing—he says explicitly that religious movements with no tribal force behind them typically fail. And when religious fervor cools across generations, the force it temporarily amplified falls back to its own level, sometimes lower.

Why it matters: this is a rare piece of clear-sightedness from an insider—he declines to explain the rise of the Islamic empire by divine favor and instead files his own faith's founding event inside his own sociological model. The tool it hands you: no ideology—religion, nationalism, corporate culture, revolutionary faith—can manufacture cohesion out of nothing. It can only amplify cohesion that already exists, and the amplification decays with time.

8. "A child of custom" and the internal critique of sources

One of his most quoted lines runs, in substance: "Man is a child of the customs and things he has become used to, not the product of his nature and temperament." You are what you are mostly because of what has repeatedly trained you, not because of who you were born to. This is both the key to his account of why desert people are hard and city people soft, and the foundation of his whole method: behavior is shaped by circumstance, circumstance is lawful, therefore behavior can be reasoned about and predicted.

The direct payoff is the technique he pioneers: internal criticism of sources. Before him, Islamic historiography tested a report mainly by its chain of transmission (isnād, the list of who reported it to whom): if every transmitter was reliable, the report stood. Ibn Khaldun says that is nowhere near enough. You must also ask whether, given how societies actually work, the thing could have happened at all. His demonstration is hard-edged: chronicles report that Moses led six hundred thousand fighting men out of Egypt, and he simply runs the numbers—could that land feed them, how long would that column be, could the descendants of Jacob's sons multiply to that figure in four generations? They could not, so the report fails, however clean its chain. He takes apart the legendary "City of Brass" in the desert the same way, and does not spare his great predecessor al-Masʿūdī either.

Why it matters: this is the first systematic attempt to admit common sense and social regularity as evidence in historical criticism, and it is the book's most durable methodological legacy—when a historian today doubts an ancient census or the reported size of an army, the move is still his: calculate first, believe second. It also changes your habits directly: faced with any figure or claim, before asking where it came from, ask whether it is physically and humanly possible.

The Argument, Distilled

Compressed into one line of reasoning, the whole book runs like this.

Humans cannot live alone—one person can neither produce enough food nor fend off beasts and other people—so they must form groups; that is the starting point of society. Groups require something to bind them: that is ʿaṣabiyya, and it is strongest where external order is thinnest (desert, mountains, frontiers), because there people have nothing but each other. Past a certain strength, group feeling pushes upward—producing a chief, then expansion, then royal authority. Which is why power is nearly always seized from the periphery, not generated at the center.

The day power is won, everything reverses. The ruler must guard against his own comrades, so he purges them and hires outsiders: cohesion is replaced with salary. Settled life brings luxury; luxury raises costs and dulls courage. Rising costs force heavier taxes; heavier taxes crush producers; the tax base shrinks, which forces still heavier taxes—the fiscal death spiral. The army goes from brothers to unpaid mercenaries, and the frontier starts to leak. Three or four generations on, the dynasty still holds the splendid capital, the regalia, and the legitimacy—but the willingness to die for one another is at zero. Then some group still living in the hard country, its group feeling intact and often amplified by a religious call, walks up and pushes; the thing falls over. The victors move into the same city and begin the same curve.

What has he actually established? That the rise and fall of dynasties needs no appeal to divine mandate, moral decline, or the personal quality of rulers—it can be derived from a set of mechanisms about groups, environments, public finance, and generations. On what evidence? Three things: firsthand observation of the endless carousel of North African and Andalusi regimes (he served in them and watched them fall), a critical reworking of the sources available to him, and a self-consistent chain of causal reasoning. The weight of the book lies less in whether its conclusions are right than in its proof of concept: that social phenomena can be analyzed as causal natural processes at all.

Misreadings, Criticisms, Honest Objections

Misreading one: that it is a hymn to the noble nomad. The reverse. Ibn Khaldun holds that civilization, crafts, learning, and refinement exist only in cities and that desert life is scarcity. What he admires is the hardness the desert forces, not the desert. He is describing a one-way street—you get in by being hard, and getting in costs you the hardness. That is tragic structure, not a ranking of values.

Misreading two: that ʿaṣabiyya means blood, or nationalism. He says repeatedly that alliance, clientage, and long cohabitation generate it as well; kinship is merely the cheapest implementation. It is a concept about the structure of a human group, not about race. Reading it as modern nationalism is worse still: nationalism is manufactured by print and state schooling, whereas ʿaṣabiyya is built out of face-to-face shared risk.

Misreading three: that "luxury destroys dynasties" is a moral sermon. His argument is about costs and risk appetite, not divine punishment for decadence. The difference matters practically: the moral reading prescribes a frugal king; the mechanical reading tells you that even a frugal king cannot stop it once a ruling group has spent three generations safe inside a city.

And now the serious objections, which deserve to be stated plainly:

One: a cyclical model has no room for progress. His world turns in a closed loop—rise, peak, decay, fall, rise again. It cannot accommodate the idea that a society might accumulate its way into being permanently different, and so it entirely failed to anticipate what Europe was about to do. Intriguingly, he was not wholly unaware: at one point he notes hearing that the philosophical sciences are flourishing in the Christian lands to the north, and adds that God knows best what goes on there (paraphrased)—then moves on. His model explains his world's past and does not survive contact with the next five hundred years.

Two: it may be a regional model promoted to a universal law. The cycle of "peripheral tribe replaces decayed center" fits the medieval Maghreb, where cities and tribes fed on each other, extremely well; as a law of human history it strains badly. The Ottoman state lasted six centuries; in the Chinese dynastic cycle the bureaucratic-gentry structure mattered far more than tribal cohesion. When the twentieth-century anthropologist Ernest Gellner used a Khaldunian model to explain "Muslim society" as such, he drew exactly this criticism: a local mechanism inflated into an essentialized picture of the East.

Three: "four generations, one hundred twenty years" does not survive the data. It reads as a rhetorical order of magnitude rather than a testable regularity, and he himself allows that it may run longer or shorter. But once the span is elastic, and given that group feeling cannot be measured independently, the theory drifts toward unfalsifiability: any collapse whatsoever can be attributed after the fact to exhausted ʿaṣabiyya—which is the most dangerous comfort zone a theory can have.

Four: technology ended his mechanism. There is a forceful modern rebuttal: the Khaldunian cycle presupposes that a cohesive band of horsemen can defeat a larger paid army. Gunpowder, artillery, and drilled standing infantry erased that premise—after which the wealthy, bureaucratic, cannon-casting center reliably beat the tribal periphery, and the road from margin to throne effectively closed. What he discovered may be less a universal law of history than the law of one particular military-technological regime.

Five: the climatic determinism, and the racial prejudice inside it, must be said out loud. He explains the temperaments of peoples by climatic zone, holding that those of the far south and far north stand further from a balanced humanity; one passage describes sub-Saharan Africans as close to animal in disposition and naturally submissive to slavery (paraphrased). Those passages are real, they are wrong, and they should not be waved away as "a man of his time." Two facts belong alongside them, to avoid a different distortion: he explicitly rejected the then-common genealogical explanation (the "curse of Ham"), insisting on environment rather than descent; and this is precisely where his system cracks open—a man who wrote that people are children of custom rather than of lineage had, by his own principle, the tools to refute his own conclusion.

Six: "the father of sociology" needs discounting too. The Muqaddimah also devotes long stretches to prophecy, dream interpretation, letter mysticism, and magic, all of which he takes to be real phenomena—though he dismantles astrology and alchemy with a fairly empirical hand. He is not a secular social scientist born early; he is a fourteenth-century Maliki judge who did something extraordinarily ahead of its time from inside his own framework of belief. Modernizing him actually obscures what he achieved.

Ten Sentences

1. The surface of history is stories; underneath is the science of how societies organize themselves. Whoever lacks the second cannot tell true from false in the first.

2. Fights are decided not by numbers, money, or equipment but by how many people will hold one second longer for the person beside them. That force is ʿaṣabiyya; it cannot be bought, only grown.

3. It is strongest where order is thinnest, because there people have nothing but each other. Scarcity is the delivery room of cohesion.

4. Civilization is what that force is for and where it is buried: you need hardness to take the city, and the city immediately starts spending your hardness.

5. Every founder ends up purging the men who carried him up, because the same cohesion that raised him is the only thing that can unseat him. That cut severs his own blood supply.

6. The damage luxury does is not moral but actuarial: it raises fixed costs and lowers the risk anyone will accept, at the same time.

7. Early in a dynasty small assessments yield large revenues; late in a dynasty large assessments yield small ones (paraphrased). The final stop is a ruler who goes into business against his own subjects.

8. Religion multiplies cohesion, it does not add it: it can fuse feuding groups into one force but cannot conjure force from nothing—a call with no strength behind it dies quietly.

9. A man is a child of his customs, not a product of his lineage (paraphrased). So never ask what a people is "by nature"; ask what circumstances have trained it, over and over.

10. The last ruler does not lose the realm for being unusually foolish—he is sitting at the end of the curve. Seat the founder in the fourth generation and he would probably lose it too.
