What Is Life?

What Is Life? The Physical Aspect of the Living Cell · Erwin Schrödinger (1887–1961) · 1944

中文 →

In One Sentence

A physicist notices that life's signature trick — copying itself faithfully for centuries — ought to be physically impossible, unless hereditary material is a handful of atoms locked in place by quantum mechanics and arranged like a code, an "aperiodic crystal", and unless the organism survives by constantly "feeding on order" (negative entropy) to stave off decay. Nine years later, the DNA double helix turned out to look remarkably like what he described.

Where It Sits

Schrödinger was one of the founders of quantum mechanics (the wave equation bears his name; Nobel Prize in Physics, 1933). In February 1943 he gave three public lectures at Trinity College Dublin and turned them into this slim book the following year. It contains almost no original biology, yet it became the spark for molecular biology: Watson, Crick and Wilkins all said it was what pointed them toward the gene.

The Core Argument

  • Nearly all the order physics knew about was statistical order — the averaged-out behavior of enormous numbers of atoms. Yet the order of heredity is stored in very few atoms and survives intact for centuries. Classical statistical physics cannot account for that.
  • Quantum mechanics can: the gene is a molecule, kept stable by discrete energy levels and energy barriers, and a mutation is a "quantum jump" from one molecular configuration to another. Hereditary material must therefore be an "aperiodic crystal" whose atomic arrangement is itself a "code-script."
  • Thermodynamically, what makes an organism special is that it "feeds on negative entropy": it keeps drawing order from its surroundings and dumping disorder back, holding itself far from the dead stillness of equilibrium. Life runs on "order from order," not the "order from disorder" physics was used to.

Put together: life = a molecular code sealed by quantum mechanics + a thermodynamic engine that keeps swallowing order. The book's achievement isn't the details of its answers. It's that it recast "what is life?" as a question a physicist could actually get to work on: how is the information stored, where, and why doesn't it fall apart?

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Why are atoms so small? Statistical order and the √n law

What it is. Schrödinger opens with an odd-sounding question — why are atoms so small? — and promptly flips it: the real question is why we (and anything else that perceives and thinks) have to be so much bigger than atoms. The answer lies in a basic fact of statistical physics. Almost every "exact law" in physics — gas pressure, diffusion, magnetization, heat flow — isn't the result of individual atoms behaving themselves. It's what you get when countless atoms jostle at random and their behavior averages out. A single molecule's thermal motion is pure chaos; ten quintillion of them together behave like clockwork. Schrödinger calls this "order from disorder."

The arithmetic: the √n law. Averages come with error, and the error follows a rule. If a system has n particles, random fluctuations are typically about √n in size, so the relative error is about 1/√n. With n = 100, you're off by around 10 — ten percent. With a million, the error drops to a tenth of a percent. To make a "law" accurate to one part in ten million, you need an astronomical number of particles. Precision is bought with numbers: the smaller the system, the less reliable it is.

An analogy. Think of an opinion poll. Ask ten people and the result is close to worthless; ask ten thousand and you're within about a percentage point. Physical laws are opinion polls of atoms. Schrödinger's counterexample is Brownian motion: a grain of pollen small enough that the molecules hitting it don't average out gets knocked about in a jittery dance — a live demonstration that tiny means unreliable. A creature built from just a few atoms would live in permanent, unpredictable noise; stable perception and thought would be out of the question.

Why it matters. This is the setup for the whole book. It establishes a piece of common sense — reliable means many — and everything dramatic that follows comes from the fact that life breaks it.

2. The paradox of the gene: a few atoms, unchanged for centuries

What it is. Genetics already knew genes sat on chromosomes, and there were two rough ways to estimate a gene's size: the width of bands visible on chromosomes under the microscope, and the size of the "target" that an X-ray had to hit to cause a mutation. Pulling together the estimates of the day, Schrödinger concluded that a gene held at most a few million atoms, and by the X-ray estimate perhaps only around a thousand. By the √n law, something that small should be tossed about by thermal motion as helplessly as a pollen grain.

The signature example: the Habsburg lip. Yet genes are astonishingly stable. Schrödinger points to the "Habsburg lip" — the pronounced jutting jaw and drooping lower lip of Europe's Habsburg dynasty, visible in family portraits from the sixteenth century to the nineteenth. Whatever tiny cluster of atoms set that trait was pummeled by thermal motion at body temperature, about 37°C, for centuries, and still got copied down the generations almost untouched. Something a fraction of the size of a pollen grain outperformed any precision-made clock.

Why it matters. This is the book's central puzzle. Life shows an orderly, law-like behavior of a kind we meet nowhere else, and it rests on a tiny number of atoms (paraphrased). That move turns a biology question into a physics question: not "what chemical is the gene?" but "what kind of physical structure lets a handful of atoms resist thermal agitation and stay exact for centuries?"

How it changes the way you see. It makes stability itself something to explain. At the molecular scale, everything is falling apart by default; any small structure that stays the same for a long time is a mystery in need of an answer.

3. Mutations as quantum jumps: Delbrück's model and the energy barrier

What it is. Schrödinger's answer came from a 1935 paper by three men: Timoféeff-Ressovsky (a Soviet geneticist), Zimmer (a physicist) and Max Delbrück (a physicist who later pioneered phage research and shared the 1969 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine). Chapters four and five are essentially an explanation of their model for a general audience, and Schrödinger openly calls it "Delbrück's model."

The mechanism. Quantum mechanics says atoms don't join into molecules in some smooth, continuous way; a molecule can only sit in one of a set of discrete energy states (energy levels). To change from one arrangement of atoms into another — chemists call the alternatives isomers — a molecule has to get over an "energy hill," an energy barrier. The energy needed is the activation energy, which Schrödinger writes as W. Thermal motion keeps handing the molecule random kicks of energy, but the chance of collecting enough to reach W falls off exponentially with the ratio of W to kT, the typical thermal energy (k is Boltzmann's constant, T the temperature).

Schematic: the gene is held in its configuration by an energy barrier. Now and then a thermal fluctuation or an X-ray photon pushes it over the top into another stable configuration — that is a mutation.

The arithmetic. The book gives a set of estimates: raise W from 30 times kT to 50 times, and the configuration's average lifetime jumps from about a tenth of a second to more than a year; at 60 times kT it's roughly thirty thousand years. A modest rise in the barrier buys a stability increase of many orders of magnitude. That solves the Habsburg lip. The gene has few atoms, but its stability doesn't come from averaging; it comes from the hill between quantum states. It's a molecule, not a statistical cloud.

It explains mutation too. Hugo de Vries, the Dutch botanist, had observed around 1900 that mutations come in jumps: either nothing changes, or a new stable trait appears in a single step, with no in-between. Schrödinger points out that this is exactly what a quantum jump looks like: a molecule can't change halfway; it can only leap whole from one stable configuration to another. And he stresses that mutations have to be rare. If genes changed constantly, most of the changes would be harmful and a species would drown in them. The height of the barrier strikes the balance: stable enough to inherit, occasionally changeable enough to evolve.

How it changes the way you see. You start to see reliable information as a matter of energy landscapes. Information keeps because there's a high enough wall between two states. Magnetic domains on a hard drive and charge traps in flash memory work the same way: an energy barrier separates the 0 from the 1. Schrödinger was the first to apply that engineering intuition to life.

4. The aperiodic crystal and the "code-script"

What it is. If the gene is a stable structure held together by chemical bonds, physically it's a kind of solid — a crystal. But ordinary crystals (salt, ice, metals) are periodic: one small unit repeated over and over in three dimensions. They're stable, but they have nothing to say; know one unit and you know the whole thing. Schrödinger argued that the gene must be an aperiodic crystal: just as stable, just as firmly bonded, but with units that don't repeat, so every position can be different.

His own analogy. The difference, he says, is like that between ordinary wallpaper, where one pattern recurs with dull regularity, and a masterpiece of embroidery — a Raphael tapestry, say — which shows no tedious repetition but an elaborate, coherent, meaningful design (paraphrased). Wallpaper has order but no information; the tapestry has both.

The code-script. The chromosomes, he wrote, contain in some kind of code-script the entire pattern of the individual's future development. How could a small molecule hold the design of a whole organism? He does the sum with Morse code: two symbols, dot and dash, in groups of no more than four, already give thirty distinct letters; allow more symbols and longer groups, and the number of possibilities grows exponentially. Information capacity doesn't need bulk, only combinations. The arrangement of a modest number of atoms is enough for an astronomical number of different instruction manuals.

What happened next. In 1953 Watson and Crick published the structure of DNA: four bases (A, T, G, C) strung in a non-repeating sequence along a sturdy sugar-phosphate backbone — practically a textbook realization of "aperiodic crystal plus four-symbol code." The genetic code cracked in the 1960s, where three bases specify one amino acid, follows the same principle of encoding many instructions with short combinations. The book was written when most scientists assumed genes were probably made of protein (Avery's paper showing that DNA carries hereditary information also came out in 1944). Schrödinger didn't guess the molecule, but he guessed the physical properties it had to have.

How it changes the way you see. This is one of the first clear statements of the modern idea that life is information: an organism isn't just a heap of remarkable chemicals but a text that is stored, copied and read. Schrödinger also said the code-script is both "law-code and executive power" — the architect's plan and the builder's craft in one. That later looked like an overreach (see the critiques below), but the question it raises — how does information become a body? — is still at the heart of developmental biology.

5. Feeding on negative entropy: how life holds off decay

What it is. Start with entropy: a measure of how disordered a system is. The second law of thermodynamics says the entropy of an isolated system only rises or stays put. Leave things alone and rooms get messy, coffee goes cold, buildings crumble, and everything heads toward a uniform, changeless state — thermodynamic equilibrium. Being alive, Schrödinger says, means evading that decline. A stone reached equilibrium long ago; an organism stays highly ordered, far from equilibrium, for a long time. How?

The arithmetic. He borrows Boltzmann's formula: entropy = k log D, where D measures the system's disorder. Simply by living — metabolizing, moving, making heat — an organism keeps producing entropy, and left unchecked it would soon reach maximum entropy, which is death. Its only way out is to keep extracting "negative entropy" from its environment (which he defines as k log(1/D), a measure of order). Hence the book's most famous line: what an organism feeds upon is negative entropy.

A concrete example. Do we eat for energy? Not exactly, he says. An adult's energy budget roughly balances; whatever goes in comes back out as heat. What matters is the quality of the energy. Food is made of highly ordered large molecules (proteins, starches); what we excrete and exhale are broken-down simple ones. We take in order, give out disorder, and use the difference to pay off the entropy we keep generating. Plants get their negative entropy straight from sunlight — energy concentrated in light from a very hot source.

An honest footnote. Fellow physicists objected to this chapter, and in a note at its end Schrödinger concedes that for an audience of physicists he should have talked about free energy (the part of a system's energy available to do work, which accounts for both energy and entropy). He chose "negative entropy" because it was easier for lay readers to grasp. So "feeding on negative entropy" is best read as a vivid heuristic, not a precise thermodynamic quantity.

How it changes the way you see. It turns life from a thing into a process. An organism isn't a still building; it's a whirlpool holding its shape in a moving stream. The whirlpool persists while the water passing through it is constantly replaced. What makes something alive is not its material but the organization that keeps wringing order out of its surroundings to maintain its form. Stop the flow and the form collapses back into equilibrium.

6. "Order from order": the clockwork and the new laws of physics

What it is. Finally Schrödinger ties the two threads together. Ordinary physical order is "order from disorder" — statistical averaging. Life's order is "order from order": one highly ordered molecule (the gene) directly shapes an entire ordered organism, and passes that order on intact when it is copied. To him this was a mechanism physics had barely studied.

The clockwork analogy. Is there any precedent in physics for "order from order"? Yes: the running of a clock, the motion of the planets. These systems look "dynamical" — each step precisely determined by the last, with no statistical averaging. But a clock is also made of atoms jiggling with heat; why does it run as reliably as a dynamical system? Because it's a solid, its parts held firmly by chemical bonds (what he calls Heitler–London forces, the quantum-mechanical account of the covalent bond), and at room temperature thermal motion can't shake its structure. At room temperature a clock behaves almost like a system at absolute zero — and so do the key parts of an organism, the gene above all. Hence his well-known remark that the living organism is the finest masterpiece ever achieved along the lines of quantum mechanics (paraphrased).

"Other laws of physics." He went further: living matter, while not eluding the laws of physics as established so far, is likely to involve "other laws of physics" hitherto unknown, which, once revealed, will be just as much a part of physics (paraphrased). That line lured a cohort of young physicists into biology.

How it changes the way you see. It gives you a general way to ask how reliable any system is. Reliability comes either from averaging over large numbers (statistical order — actuarial tables, polls) or from rigid structure that's immune to noise (structural order — clocks, digital circuits, DNA). Life uses both: structural order to store information, and the statistics of vast numbers of molecules to make its physiology steady.

The Skeleton of the Argument

The book is one clean chain of reasoning:

① Every precise law physics knows is statistical and needs huge numbers of atoms to average out, so small systems are bound to be unreliable (the √n law).

② But the facts of genetics say the gene has very few atoms and still passes down unchanged for centuries (the Habsburg lip), mutating only rarely, in jumps.

③ Classical physics can't explain this; quantum mechanics can. The gene is a molecule locked into a discrete configuration by an energy barrier, its stability rising exponentially with the barrier's height; a mutation is a quantum jump to another configuration.

④ A molecule that is both stable and able to carry vast amounts of information must be an aperiodic crystal: its atoms don't repeat, and the arrangement itself is the code-script.

⑤ The organism as a whole resists the second law by feeding on negative entropy: taking in order, giving off disorder, staying far from equilibrium.

⑥ So life's order is "order from order." It doesn't break physics, but physics may have new laws to learn from it.

How does he make the case? Hardly with new data at all — with a physicist's reasoning from constraints. First ask what physical conditions something must meet to do what it demonstrably does, then work back from those conditions to what it must look like. That habit of reasoning from constraint to structure is the book's real legacy. It told a generation that the secret of life could be attacked at the molecular scale, and that the key words were information, stability and replication.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "Schrödinger discovered or predicted DNA." He didn't. He never mentions DNA or suggests that hereditary material is a nucleic acid — the prevailing view leaned toward protein. What he predicted were the physical properties the hereditary material had to have (stable, non-repeating, able to encode), not which molecule it was. The credit divides cleanly: the principled guess was his; the proof belongs to Avery, Franklin, Watson, Crick and others.

Misreading 2: "The physics in the book is Schrödinger's own." The central model comes from Delbrück and colleagues' 1935 paper, and Schrödinger says so. His contribution was synthesis, distillation, and putting the model — with a great physicist's name and exceptionally clear prose — in front of an entire generation of scientists.

Critique 1: Perutz's "not new and not true." Max Perutz, who solved the structure of hemoglobin and shared the 1962 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, delivered the sharpest verdict in a 1987 essay: roughly, what was true in the book was not original, and much of what was original was known not to be true even when it was written. Above all, he argued, Schrödinger all but ignored biochemistry. Enzymes had long been shown to be the molecular machines that maintain order in living things, the chemistry of metabolism was already well understood, and life needed no mysterious "new physics."

Critique 2: "Negative entropy" is muddy. Linus Pauling, the chemist and two-time Nobel laureate, among others, called the notion loose and misleading. What organisms actually use is free energy, of which entropy is only one part. And "organisms eat negative entropy" invites the misreading that life defies the second law. It doesn't: it lowers its internal entropy at the cost of raising the environment's entropy by more. Schrödinger had already half-conceded the point in his note.

Critique 3: The "new laws of physics" never came. The very success of molecular biology showed that heredity and replication can be understood with ordinary chemistry and quantum mechanics — no new laws required. By that measure, the book's most thrilling conjecture was wrong, even though it drew many people into the field.

Critique 4: The "code-script" conflates information with execution. He called it both law-code and executive power. Later molecular biology showed that DNA by itself does nothing; reading and executing it depend on a whole apparatus of protein machines (polymerases, ribosomes and so on). John von Neumann's slightly later theory of self-reproducing automata explicitly separated the "description" from the "constructor." And more recent developmental biology and epigenetics (the study of mechanisms that affect gene expression without changing the DNA sequence) suggest that "genome = complete blueprint" is itself a simplification.

A fair verdict: as science, it is partly wrong, partly borrowed, partly overstated. As a way of posing a question, it is close to irreplaceable. Its value lies not in getting the answers right but in handing the right question, in the right form, to the right generation.

Schrödinger Beyond This Book

Read only the main text and you'd take Schrödinger for a thoroughgoing physical reductionist. But the book's epilogue, "On Determinism and Free Will," already turns another way. If my body runs entirely on physical law, and yet I directly experience myself as steering it, the only consistent conclusion, he says, is roughly this: "I," in the widest sense of the word, am the one who controls the "motion of the atoms" according to the laws of nature. He then reaches for the central claim of the Upanishads, the ancient Indian scriptures: the individual self (Atman) is identical with the all-pervading whole (Brahman). The Dublin publisher originally lined up reportedly withdrew over this epilogue, and the book came out from Cambridge University Press instead.

His late lecture collection Mind and Matter (1958) develops this into two arguments. First, the "principle of objectivation": to build an objective picture of the world, science first removes the observer from the picture. So when we fail to find consciousness, color or sweetness in that picture, it isn't because they don't exist but because they were excluded from the start. Second, the "arithmetical paradox": there is one world, yet billions of separate consciousnesses experiencing it. His only way out was to hold that consciousness is fundamentally one and its plurality only apparent — the position he took from Vedanta, the school of Indian philosophy grounded in the Upanishads.

Put that together with this book and you get the whole Schrödinger: he believed physics could explain every mechanism of life, yet insisted that it could not, in principle, explain the subject who experiences that life — because science's method was built on leaving the subject out.

Crossovers

  • Information theory · Landauer's principle: Landauer showed that erasing one bit dissipates at least kT ln 2 of heat, putting information and entropy in the same ledger. Alike: both treat order as information. Unlike: information theory asks about the limits of computation, not how life stays alive.
  • Complexity science · Dissipative structures: Prigogine showed that a liquid heated from below self-organizes into hexagonal convection cells, sustained by energy flowing through far from equilibrium. Alike: both keep order by throughput. Unlike: convection cells carry no code and can't replicate or evolve.
  • Distributed systems · Replication and error correction: databases keep data intact on failing hardware through replicas and checksums. Schrödinger credited fidelity entirely to energy barriers, which is only half right: cells also lower errors with polymerase proofreading, DNA repair and Hopfield's "kinetic proofreading" (spending extra energy on a second check) — half structure, half paid-for correction.
  • Neuroscience · The free energy principle: Karl Friston argues that living systems minimize "surprise" so their states don't scatter into unsurvivable regions. Alike: both define being alive as resisting disorder. Unlike: his free energy is an information-theoretic variational quantity, not a thermodynamic one.
  • Buddhism · Non-self and continuity: Buddhism treats the self as a moment-to-moment stream (santāna), not a substance. Alike: the negentropy whirlpool is also form without fixed material. Unlike: Schrödinger's epilogue arrives at the great Self of Atman-Brahman, exactly what Buddhism denies.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① Physics' exact laws are almost all statistical: order is what countless randomly moving atoms average out to.

② Precision is bought with numbers — n particles give an error of about 1/√n — so anything that thinks must be far larger than an atom.

③ Yet a gene has very few atoms and stays stable at body temperature for centuries: that is life's central puzzle.

④ The answer is quantum mechanics: the gene is a molecule held by an energy barrier; its stability comes not from numbers but from the hill between energy levels.

⑤ A mutation is a quantum jump from one stable configuration to another, which is why it comes in leaps — and why it must be rare.

⑥ Wallpaper has order without information; a Raphael tapestry has both. Hereditary material must be an aperiodic crystal.

⑦ The arrangement of atoms is the code-script: information capacity needs combinations, not bulk.

⑧ What an organism feeds upon is negative entropy: it takes in order, gives off disorder, and pays its entropy debt with the difference.

⑨ Life's order is "order from order," like a clock that resists thermal noise through rigid structure.

⑩ He guessed neither the molecule nor any new law of physics, yet he handed the right question to the right generation.
