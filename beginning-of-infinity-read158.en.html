The Beginning of Infinity

Explanations That Transform the World · David Deutsch · 2011 (with The Fabric of Reality · 1997 folded in)

中文 →

In One Sentence

For hundreds of thousands of years human civilization barely moved, then in the last few centuries it took off. Deutsch's claim is that the difference was not more intelligence, more observation or better luck, but that we learned to seek good explanations and to criticize them systematically. A good explanation is one whose details all pull their weight, so it cannot be tweaked at will; and because every explanation can be wrong and every error can be corrected, the growth of knowledge has no ceiling: we are always at the beginning of infinity.

Where It Sits

Deutsch (born 1953 in Haifa, Israel, based at Oxford for most of his career) is one of the founders of quantum computing: in 1985 he gave the first precise description of a universal quantum computer. His philosophy comes from Karl Popper, his physics from Hugh Everett's many-worlds. He makes a perfect sparring partner for Kuhn's Structure of Scientific Revolutions, which this site has also covered: Kuhn says science switches between paradigms without necessarily closing in on anything; Deutsch says science is closing in on the truth, and that this is the most important process in the universe.

Core Claims

  • Knowledge is made of explanations, not predictions and not piles of observations. Theories begin as bold conjectures and are winnowed by criticism and experiment; experience serves to kill conjectures, not to give birth to them. What separates good explanations from bad is whether they are hard to vary.
  • Problems are inevitable; problems are soluble. Anything not forbidden by the laws of nature is achievable, given the right knowledge (paraphrased). So every evil is, at root, a shortage of knowledge, and both pessimism and sustainability-as-standing-still misread the situation.
  • People matter cosmically. A person is a universal explainer: whatever can be understood, a person can in principle understand. Once knowledge begins to grow in the form of explanations, its effects can reach without limit, even to astronomical scales.

The Core Concepts, One by One

1. A good explanation is hard to vary

Deutsch opens with the plainest of questions: why are there seasons? The ancient Greek answer: Hades, god of the underworld, abducted Persephone, and her mother Demeter, goddess of the harvest, was so stricken with grief that the earth went barren; that was winter. Later Persephone was allowed to return to her mother for part of each year, the goddess rejoiced, and spring came.

The story is testable in its way (it predicts that the seasons will cycle), but that is not where it fails. Every detail could be swapped out with no loss of explanatory power. Replace Persephone with another goddess, grief with anger, the underworld with the sea floor, and the story "explains" the seasons just as well. Worse, it cannot explain why the hemispheres have opposite seasons: one goddess cannot be grieving and rejoicing at once. Yet add one more twist to the plot and the counterexample is absorbed. An explanation that can absorb any outcome explains nothing.

Now the modern account. Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5 degrees to the plane of its orbit, so for half the year the northern hemisphere leans toward the Sun: sunlight strikes it more directly and the days run longer, which is summer, while the southern hemisphere leans away and has winter. Every part bears load. Change the angle and the seasons change in strength; remove the tilt and there are no seasons; and the account predicts, for free, opposite seasons in the two hemispheres and midnight sun at the poles. You cannot alter any detail and keep it working. It also disposes of the common intuition that summer happens because we are closer to the Sun, since then both hemispheres would have summer together.

That is the whole of Deutsch's standard: a good explanation's content is locked in place by what it explains. Testability is necessary (the myth is testable too) but the real bar is being hard to vary. It changes how you meet a theory: before asking "what evidence supports this?", ask "if I changed this part, would it still work?" Claims that can be adjusted to fit any outcome, horoscopes or the conspiracy theory that "explains" everything, are usually bad explanations.

2. Fallibilism: knowledge comes from conjecture and criticism, not the senses

The whole book takes apart an idea of knowledge that has been popular for centuries: empiricism, the view that knowledge is derived or induced from sense experience. Popper, whom Deutsch admires, had already argued that no theory is read off observations; conjecture comes first, and observation only tests it. Observation is always theory-laden: to call a dot of light in a telescope "a moon of Jupiter" already presupposes a whole body of optics and celestial mechanics.

He is especially hard on inductivism, the idea that what has happened again and again will go on happening. His example, paraphrased: someone living in the twentieth century saw every year begin with "19" all their life, and by induction should have been sure the next would too; then 2000 arrived and the "law" broke. We are not fooled not because we induce more carefully, but because we have an explanation of how calendars work. Predictions come from explanations, not from repetition.

He rejects justificationism as well: the view that knowledge must be certified as true by some authority (the senses, scripture, experts), which is the textbook definition of knowledge as "justified true belief." Nothing can certify truth, Deutsch says; all knowledge is conjectural and fallible (this is fallibilism). But fallible does not mean unknowable. Errors are unavoidable, yet they can be found and corrected, and the result of correcting them is progress.

He also takes aim at instrumentalism, the view that theories are merely tools for prediction and need not describe a real world. The "shut up and calculate" attitude in quantum mechanics is, for him, exactly this disease: give up explanation and you give up the core of knowledge. What changes is your stance: "I'm not sure yet" stops being a reason to stop and becomes the starting point for criticism.

3. The Enlightenment: the start of a tradition of criticism

If good explanations are so useful, why did sustained progress only begin in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries? Because good explanations were occasionally thought of, then quickly suppressed. What was truly new in the Enlightenment was not any particular discovery but an institutionalized tolerance, even encouragement, of criticism: a tradition of criticism. It rejected seeking knowledge from authority. The question was no longer "who said so, what do the classics say" but "does this survive criticism?"

Classical Athens and Renaissance Florence each had brief "mini-enlightenments" that then went out. This leads to the book's most politically charged pair of ideas: static societies and dynamic societies. A static society stays stable by suppressing change, so that a person sees much the same world in a lifetime as their grandparents did; Sparta is the type, a set of institutions built to stop anything from changing. A dynamic society makes the correction of error its normal condition.

The same idea shapes his view of democracy. He adopts Popper's criterion: the point is not "who should rule" but "can bad rulers and bad policies be removed without bloodshed?" That is, democracy builds error correction into political institutions.

4. Memes: rational and anti-rational

Deutsch borrows Dawkins' "meme": an idea that replicates from person to person as genes replicate from generation to generation. The key question is what makes an idea get passed on, and he distinguishes two strategies.

Rational memes replicate by being useful to the people who hold them and surviving their criticism: you test a good method and teach it to others. Anti-rational memes replicate by disabling the recipient's capacity for criticism: taboos, "don't ask," punishment for doubters, "this is how our ancestors did it." Whether they survive has nothing to do with whether they are true.

This gives a mechanism for static societies: a static society is one dominated by anti-rational memes. And there is a counterintuitive corollary. To replicate memes in a static society, a person must accurately guess the rules behind their elders' behavior, which takes creativity. Deutsch conjectures that human creativity may first have evolved to copy memes more faithfully (paraphrased): a capacity born to conserve, which, once turned loose on criticism, became the engine of unlimited progress. It invites a second look at the subjects around you that "can't be discussed": the more an idea demands that you not examine it, the more you should.

5. The jump to universality

This is the book's most beautiful observation: many systems, improved bit by bit, suddenly jump at some point to universality, from doing a few things to being able, in principle, to do an entire class of things.

SystemBefore the jump (limited cases)After the jump (universal)
WritingPictograms: every new word needs a new symbolAlphabet: a few dozen symbols can write any word that can be spoken
NumbersRoman numerals: harder to write as they grow, clumsy to calculate withPlace-value (Hindu-Arabic) numerals: ten symbols express any number

Heredity(Conjectured) early replicators that copied only particular moleculesGenetic code: one code can build any protein and any organism
ExplainersAnimals: limited skills, inborn or learned by trial and errorPeople: can create any explanation that can be created

Table: kinds of jump to universality discussed in the book (writing, numbers, computing and the genetic code are the book's examples; the table is this page's arrangement).

These jumps often share a secret: digital representation and error correction. Letters are discrete, so a slightly botched letter can still be recognized; DNA bases are discrete, so they can be copied exactly. In an analog signal errors accumulate along the way, but a discrete signal can be straightened out at every step. Error correction is what makes long chains of copying and computation possible.

It changes how you see people: human intelligence is not a bigger toolbox than a chimpanzee's; it has crossed a threshold and become universal. So whatever can be understood, people can in principle understand; aliens or AGIs, if they are people, are the same kind of thing as us, differing only in speed and memory.

6. Knowledge is information that causes itself to remain

Deutsch offers a physicist's definition of knowledge, roughly: knowledge is information which, once physically embodied in a suitable environment, tends to cause itself to remain so. Knowledge in genes (how to build an eye, how to digest food) keeps its carrier alive and breeding, so it gets copied; a good idea makes the people and civilizations that hold it succeed, so it spreads.

Biological evolution and human thought thus share one framework, variation plus selection, with one crucial difference. Genetic variation is blind random mutation and selection works through death, over millions of years. Human ideas can "die first" in the mind through conjecture and criticism, letting our theories die in our stead, as Popper put it. So human knowledge grows vastly faster than evolution, and with far greater reach: evolution could never build a creature able to leave the atmosphere, since every step must pay off immediately, but people can design a rocket for a goal that does not yet exist.

This is the ground for his rejection of a widely quoted line of Hawking's: that the human race is just a chemical scum on a moderate-sized planet (paraphrased). Chemically, Deutsch agrees; but knowledge makes people causally special. His example, roughly: to explain why there is a chilled bottle of champagne in an astrophysicist's fridge, the laws of physics and cosmology are not enough; you have to mention people, their expectations and their knowledge. In a universe where knowledge keeps growing, what most shapes the future is not stars but explanations.

7. The principle of optimism: all evils are due to insufficient knowledge

Put together, these give Deutsch's principle of optimism, roughly: all evils are caused by insufficient knowledge. This is not a mood that things will get better but an epistemological claim: for anything the laws of physics do not forbid, the knowledge to achieve it exists; we just have not found it yet.

He uses it to take apart two popular ideas. First, "spaceship Earth": the planet as a life-support system tailor-made for us, which we need only avoid breaking. Deutsch replies that most of Earth's surface, oceans, deserts, polar ice, high mountains, would kill a person without knowledge and technology within days; Earth is "habitable" because we keep reshaping it with knowledge. Second, Malthusian resource pessimism: resources are finite, so population and consumption must hit a wall. But resources are themselves created by knowledge; before the combustion engine, oil was a nuisance of black sludge underground.

Hence his sharp critique of "sustainability": if it means keeping things as they are and taking no risks, it is a recipe for a static society. There is only one real kind of stability: solving problems continually, where each solution brings new and better problems. He also rejects the reading of Easter Island as the classic case of "ecological suicide" (Diamond's Collapse is its best-known version), arguing that the root of the tragedy lay in a static culture unable to correct its errors. What this changes is your stance toward risk: not "do less to avoid mistakes" but "keep the capacity to correct errors, and learn fast."

8. Many worlds: the good explanation of quantum mechanics

Apply the same yardstick to quantum mechanics. In the single-photon double-slit experiment, photons are sent one at a time, yet an interference pattern slowly builds up on the screen, as if each photon were interfering with something. The Copenhagen interpretation says: don't ask what the photon does in between; the wave function "collapses" when you measure. For Deutsch, this is instrumentalism abandoning explanation.

He accepts Everett's interpretation: the wave function never collapses, the equations of quantum mechanics mean what they say, and reality is a multiverse, in which many versions of the photon, the apparatus and the experimenter coexist and interfere with one another, until their differences are amplified to the macroscopic scale and they stop interfering observably. Only then do they separate into what we call "worlds."

The book introduces a key term for this picture: fungibility, meaning multiple instances that are identical in every respect and cannot be told apart. His analogy is money in a bank account: if you have a hundred dollars, asking "which dollar" makes no sense; they are fungible. Universes in the multiverse are like this before they differentiate: two identical universes are not two separate identical things but a fungible whole; measurement makes them distinguishable, and so they "split." This is far subtler than the popular version in which every measurement copies the universe: differentiation is gradual and a matter of information, not the conjuring of new universes. It is a load-bearing wall of his whole system; reject many-worlds and his account of quantum computation (below) wobbles too.

9. Beauty is objective, and "people" are not only humans

Two bolder, more controversial extensions. First, beauty can be objective. Flowers evolved to attract insects, yet humans find them beautiful too. His conjecture: flowers and pollinators needed a signal that works across species and is hard to fake, so it had to be hard to vary, and in meeting that standard it touched some objective beauty (paraphrased).

Second, an artificial general intelligence that can create explanations is a person. Deutsch argues that real AGI cannot be had by piling up rules or feeding in data, because creativity is the generation of new explanations, and since we do not yet understand it ourselves, we cannot write the program (paraphrased). He is also skeptical of Turing-test thinking: judging only behavior, not explanation, is instrumentalism again. The ethical corollary is weightier: if an AGI is a person, enslaving it or controlling it as a tool is the oppression of a person. (The book predates large language models; Deutsch has said in later interviews that he does not consider today's LLMs to be AGI.)

The Skeleton

The book's argument compresses into a single chain:

(1) Knowledge is conjectured, then pruned of error by criticism, so all knowledge is fallible, and all of it can be improved. (2) The test of improvement is whether an explanation is hard to vary; only good explanations have reach, governing things they were never designed for, as Newton's law of gravity governs distant galaxies. (3) Good explanations accumulate only within a tradition that tolerates criticism: that is the Enlightenment, and the dividing line between dynamic and static societies. (4) Once explanations accumulate, people cross the threshold of universality and become entities able to understand anything understandable. (5) Hence "problems are inevitable, problems are soluble," and every evil is a shortage of knowledge; pessimism and sustainability-as-standing-still both underrate knowledge. (6) Apply the same standard to physics and you get many worlds, the reading of quantum mechanics that most refuses to give up on explanation. Conclusion: progress has no upper bound because error correction has no end; we are always at the beginning of infinity.

Common Misreadings & Critiques

Misreadings

  • "Optimism" is not "everything will be fine." Deutsch says plainly that civilization could be destroyed. His optimism is that problems have solutions, not that they solve themselves; if anything, it demands that you work hard to find them.
  • Fallibilism is not relativism. "All knowledge may be wrong" does not mean "all claims are equally good." There is an objective difference between good and bad explanations, and progress is real.
  • "People matter" does not single out Homo sapiens. His "people" are any entities that create explanations; what counts is knowledge, not carbon.

The strongest critiques

  • Many-worlds is far from consensus. Deutsch presents it as practically the only serious option, but the field is much more plural. Besides Copenhagen there is Bohmian mechanics (particles always have definite positions, guided by a "pilot wave," with no need for many worlds); objective-collapse theories such as the GRW model (the wave function collapses spontaneously with tiny probability, faster for bigger systems); QBism (the wave function represents an observer's beliefs, not the world itself); and Rovelli's relational interpretation (properties are defined only in interactions; see this site's Helgoland). Their experimental predictions largely coincide with many-worlds (objective collapse is the exception, testable in principle), and no experiment has yet picked a winner. Many-worlds also has a recognized hard problem: if every outcome happens, what does "a 30 percent chance of this result" mean? Deutsch (1999) and David Wallace derived the Born rule (the rule that turns a wave function into measurement probabilities) from decision theory, but whether the derivation succeeds is still debated.
  • "Hard to vary" is not operational enough. Critics point out that how hard a theory is to vary depends heavily on how you carve it into "details," and any theory can be reformulated to look tighter or looser. It is an excellent intuition but hard to use as a criterion that settles a contest between two theories.
  • His blanket dismissal of induction and Bayesianism draws pushback. Deutsch holds that assigning probabilities to theories ("I'm 80 percent sure this is right") is meaningless. Advocates of Bayesian epistemology (treating beliefs as probabilities updated on evidence) reply that scientists, engineers and machine learning systems do this every day, and well; and that conjecture-and-criticism itself requires some judgment of how much evidence weighs. Many philosophers think his treatment of the problem of induction declares victory rather than solving it.
  • Techno-optimism versus existential risk. Researchers of existential risk argue that some mistakes are irreversible: a nuclear war, an engineered pandemic, a runaway AI. Make one and there is no chance to correct it. Deutsch answers that slowing down and precaution are risks too, because they weaken our ability to create knowledge; critics reply that he underrates risks where once is enough. Environmental scholars add that knowledge's power to create resources does not mean it can patch an ecological collapse in time.
  • Anthropocentrism and the "universal explainer" claim. "People can understand anything understandable" comes close to circular. The philosopher Colin McGinn's "cognitive closure" argument holds that, just as other animals have concepts forever beyond their reach, the human brain may be structurally incapable of some problems (consciousness, for instance). Deutsch appeals to universal computation, but whether that bridges a conceptual gap is unsettled.

Deutsch Beyond This Book

The Beginning of Infinity centers on knowledge and progress; for the full Deutsch you need The Fabric of Reality, written fourteen years earlier. Its ambition is to weave a unified worldview from four strands, each incomplete alone, each supporting the others.

  • Everett's quantum mechanics: reality is a multiverse.
  • Popper's epistemology: knowledge grows by conjecture and criticism.
  • Turing's theory of computation: computation is a physical process, and a universal computer can simulate any physically possible process.
  • Darwin's and Dawkins' theory of evolution: replicators accumulate knowledge through variation and selection.

Here the case for many worlds is at its most direct. From single-photon interference he argues for "shadow photons": the photon we see is blocked or deflected by invisible "shadow" photons, and whatever produces measurable effects is real; they are photons in other universes. He also poses a challenge that has been quoted ever since, roughly: when Shor's algorithm (the 1994 quantum algorithm for factoring large numbers) factors a huge number, the computation required far exceeds what the visible universe could hold, so where was it done? His answer: in parallel, across many branches of the multiverse. (Most quantum computing researchers reject this framing, attributing the speedup to a clever choreography of interference rather than massive parallelism in other universes.)

His other strand is the "Turing principle." In his 1985 paper he recast the Church–Turing thesis from a mathematical claim into a physical one: every finite physical system can be simulated to arbitrary precision by a universal computing machine (this strengthened form is often called the Church–Turing–Deutsch principle). The book goes further: it is possible to build a virtual-reality generator whose repertoire includes every physically possible environment (paraphrased). This is the physicist's version of "people are universal": the universe can be fully understood and simulated by a part of itself.

Over the last decade or so, he and Chiara Marletto have developed constructor theory: instead of describing the world through initial conditions plus laws of motion, it writes physical laws as statements about which transformations are possible, which are impossible, and why. A "constructor" is something that brings about a transformation while remaining unchanged itself (like a catalyst, or a machine in a factory). It is an attempt to formalize "anything not forbidden is achievable," and it is still at an early stage.

Crossovers

  • Neuroscience · predictive processing: Andy Clark, Karl Friston and others hold that the brain constantly generates guesses about the world and corrects them with sensory error, so perception itself is conjecture and criticism. Where it differs: predictive processing's mathematics is Bayesian probability updating, exactly what Deutsch rejects.
  • Immunology · clonal selection (Burnet, 1957): the body does not "learn" how to fight a new virus; it blindly generates a vast range of antibodies in advance, then amplifies the few that actually bind the antigen. Variation first, selection after, the same shape as Popperian knowledge growth. Where it differs: the immune system's guesses are random; human guesses are guided by explanation.
  • Distributed systems · von Neumann's reliable machines from unreliable parts (1956): von Neumann showed that redundancy and majority voting, straightening out errors at every step, let you build arbitrarily reliable computation from faulty components. This is the secret Deutsch sees behind the jump to universality: discreteness plus error correction. Byzantine fault tolerance, error-correcting codes and quantum error correction share the structure. Where it differs: engineering corrects against a known noise model, while Deutsch's tradition of criticism must catch errors nobody anticipated.
  • Consciousness · Penrose on non-computability: in The Emperor's New Mind, Roger Penrose used Gödel's theorem to argue that human mathematical understanding has a non-computable element, so consciousness is not any algorithm, squarely opposed to Deutsch's view that people are universal computers and AGI is possible in principle. Where they agree: both think current AI approaches miss the point.
  • Buddhism · the Kālāma Sutta: the Buddha told the Kālāmas not to accept a teaching because of tradition, scripture, a teacher's authority or attractive reasoning, but to examine it for themselves: an ancient Indian refusal to seek knowledge from authority. Where it differs: the Buddha's test is suffering and its ending in practice, not whether an explanation is hard to vary.

Ten Sentences

1. Knowledge comes not from observation but from conjecture; observation's job is to kill the wrong conjectures.

2. A good explanation is hard to vary: every detail bears load, and changing one brings it down. Persephone can be swapped for any goddess; Earth's tilt cannot be changed by a single degree.

3. "It has always been so" is never a reason: predictions come from explanations, not repetition.

4. All knowledge may be wrong, but fallible is not unknowable: errors can be found and corrected, and that is progress.

5. The Enlightenment's real invention was not a discovery but a tradition that tolerates and encourages criticism; democracy's value lies in removing mistakes without bloodshed.

6. Rational ideas spread by surviving criticism, anti-rational ones by stopping you from criticizing: whatever you are forbidden to question, question.

7. Alphabets, place-value numerals, universal computers and the genetic code each jumped to universality, and so did we: people are universal explainers, able to understand anything understandable.

8. Problems are inevitable; problems are soluble (paraphrased): anything not forbidden by the laws of nature can be done, given the right knowledge.

9. Earth is not a spaceship built for us; knowledge made it habitable, and resources are not found but made: knowledge turned black sludge into oil.

10. Every solved problem brings better new ones, so however far we go, we are always at the beginning of infinity.
