The Enneads

Plotinus (c. 204/5–270) · edited by his student Porphyry, c. 300

中文 →

In One Sentence

Everything flows out of a single, utterly simple, unsayable source, the One, the way light pours from the sun, descending through Intellect, Soul, bodies and finally matter; and the human soul never really left that source. The task is not to acquire anything but to turn around, strip away, and go back inward, until it is "the flight of the alone to the alone."

Where It Sits

Plotinus was the last great original philosopher of antiquity. He saw himself as nothing more than an interpreter of Plato; later scholars, from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries on, called the system he built "Neoplatonism." Through Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, Meister Eckhart and Marsilio Ficino (whose complete Latin translation appeared in 1492), he became the headwater of Western mysticism and of "negative theology."

The Core Argument

  • Reality comes in levels, and the higher you go, the more unified and the more real it is. At the top is the One; below it, Intellect (Nous); below that, Soul (Psyche); at the bottom, matter, which is next to nothing. A lower level isn't a different kind of thing from the higher one. It's a weaker version, an image, of the higher one's power.
  • Everything "flows out" from the source, and the source loses nothing. This isn't creation, or a choice, or an event in time. It's the necessary overflow of something full, the way fire can't help giving off heat.
  • The human self can move up and down. Part of our soul always stays in the intelligible realm and never descended. Salvation means pulling attention back from the body and its errands, turning toward the higher self that was there all along, and finally going past thought itself into union with the One.

Put together: the universe is an outflow, a human life is a return flow, and the way back runs through the inside of you.

A word on the book itself. Plotinus came to Rome at forty to teach and only started writing around fifty. He produced 54 treatises and never revised them, according to his student Porphyry's Life of Plotinus. Porphyry later sorted them by topic into six sets of nine. The Greek for nine is ennea, hence the title. The arrangement runs from ethics (Book I) through the cosmos, soul and intellect up to the One (Book VI), so the layout itself traces a path of ascent. References below such as "I.6" mean Book I, treatise 6.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. The One: a "one" that sits above being

What it is. Plotinus starts from something surprisingly plain (VI.9.1, paraphrased): everything that exists exists by being one thing. An army, a choir or a flock doesn't exist unless it's one whole. Take a house or a ship apart into a pile of timber and the house or the ship is gone. Let a body lose its unity and it becomes a corpse, then dust. Unity isn't a property a thing happens to have. It's the condition of the thing being there at all.

Follow that upward. Anything made of parts has a borrowed unity, borrowed from something more unified than itself. Keep tracing it back and you need a source that borrows unity from nothing, because it simply is unity, with no parts at all. That is the One (Greek to hen). It can't have parts or attributes. It can't even think, because thinking splits into a thinker and a thing thought, and the moment there's a split it isn't absolutely one. So Plotinus says the One is "beyond being." He's riffing on Plato's line in the Republic that the Good lies epekeina tēs ousias, beyond being. The One gives being without appearing on the list of beings, the way the sun makes things visible without being one of the lit-up objects.

How you talk about it. Any predicate would turn it into "a something," so you can only say what it isn't: not this, not that. This is what came to be called apophatic or negative theology, the approach to God by way of denial. Plotinus admits as much (V.3.14, paraphrased): we don't say it, we say things about it, like people who feel something inside them and can't name it. Even the name "One" is a stopgap. Its job is to deny multiplicity, not to give a positive description.

Why it matters. This move turns "the highest thing" from the biggest being (a god with a will, the largest object) into the condition of there being any beings at all. It changes how you look at things: when you ask what something fundamentally is, Plotinus tells you to look at what makes it one thing. A personality, a team, a piece of music is held together by a unity you can't point to, yet if you pulled it out, the whole would fall apart.

2. Emanation: an overflowing spring, not a craftsman

What it is. If the One is perfectly simple and lacks nothing, why is there anything else? Plotinus answers with a set of images he returns to again and again (V.1.6, V.4.1): the sun gives off light, fire gives off heat, snow gives off cold, perfume gives off scent. Whatever is complete throws off an image of itself. It doesn't do this because it wants to or needs something. It does it because it is full. In his words (V.2.1, paraphrased): being perfect, it overflowed, and the overflow became something else.

Two details matter. First, the source loses nothing. The sun doesn't dim from shining, and a spring doesn't run dry from feeding a river. Second, scholars call this the doctrine of "double activity." Everything has an activity of its essence (the heat that makes fire fire) and an activity that comes from its essence (the warmed air around it). The second depends on the first and never acts back on it. Emanation is the second kind.

Why it isn't "creation." In the Christian account, God wills the world into being out of nothing, at a beginning. Plotinus's emanation has no beginning (the cosmos is eternal), no craftsman's plan, and no "out of nothing." It's an eternal, necessary dependence, like a shadow on its light source rather than a pot on its potter. Christian theologians borrowed his language heavily and then had to separate "necessary outflow" from "free creation" very carefully, which shows how different the two ideas are.

And then the turn back. What flows out starts out formless. It takes shape by turning back to gaze at its source. That's the basic rhythm of the whole system: rest in the source, flow out, turn back. The late Neoplatonist Proclus later made it official as the triad remaining–procession–reversion (monē–proodos–epistrophē), but the pattern is already in Plotinus. It changes how you look at things: a thing's identity is set less by where it came from than by what it turns toward and gazes at. The same goes for people: you're shaped by what you look at.

Schematic. The circles aren't places. They mark degrees of unity and reality, rising toward the center. Flowing out is a step down in being; flowing back is the soul's ascent.

3. The three hypostases: One, Intellect, Soul

One treatise is titled On the Three Primary Hypostases (V.1). A "hypostasis" here means one of the three basic levels of reality. We've met the One. The other two take up most of the book.

Intellect (Nous). The first thing to overflow from the One. It takes shape by looking back at the One and becomes thinking itself. What it thinks is the whole set of Plato's Forms: Justice itself, Beauty itself, the Circle itself. And thinker and thought are one and the same. Intellect doesn't have the Forms; it is the Forms, thinking themselves. This thinking isn't step-by-step reasoning (first A, then infer B). Everything is present at once, eternally, the way a mathematician doesn't read through a proof line by line but at some moment simply sees the whole thing. Plotinus describes the intelligible world (V.8.4, paraphrased) as a place where everything is transparent, nothing is dark, and each thing holds all the others in itself and sees all of them in each: each is all.

Soul (Psyche). Below Intellect comes Soul, which includes the World Soul that drives the cosmos as well as our individual souls. What marks Soul out is that it can't see everything at once. It has to think one thing after another, which is "discursive reasoning" (Greek dianoia). And that one-thing-after-another is where time comes from. In On Eternity and Time (III.7) Plotinus gives a lovely definition: time is the life of soul, in the movement by which it passes from one way of living to another. Intellect lives in eternity, and eternity isn't an endlessly long time. It's everything present at once. Soul, restless in that total stillness, wanted to unfold its contents one by one and make them its own, and so it "temporalized itself." The time of the cosmos is the breathing rhythm of the World Soul.

Matter. Further down, Soul's power gives out, and you reach the place where the light runs out: matter, sheer shapelessness, with no qualities of its own. It's barely anything, just a vacancy that receives form, like a mirror that holds no image of its own but lets images appear. In I.8 Plotinus boldly calls it "evil itself." Evil isn't a positive force. It's the total absence of good, the way darkness isn't a thing but only the place the light doesn't reach.

The three levels side by side: the lower you go, the weaker the unity, the more step-by-step the knowing, the closer to time. (The image comes from V.6.4: the One as light, Intellect as the sun, Soul as the moon borrowing its light. Elsewhere he often simply likens the One to the sun.)

Why it matters. The three hypostases are a map of the cosmos and also a map of the mind. You can pass through all three in one afternoon: crunching numbers, you live at Soul's level of reasoning; suddenly seeing the point of something, you touch for a moment Intellect's all-at-once seeing; and very rarely, even the sense that "I'm seeing it" drops away. Plotinus's cosmology is also a layered theory of consciousness.

4. The undescended soul: your best part never left

What it is. On the Descent of the Soul into Bodies (IV.8) opens with one of the very few first-person passages in the whole work (IV.8.1, paraphrased): Often I have woken up out of the body into myself, stepped outside everything else and into myself, and seen a beauty of wonderful greatness… and then, after resting in the divine, I come down from intellect to reasoning, and I'm left puzzled: how did I come down again?

His answer to the puzzle is the boldest and most disputed claim in the book (IV.8.8, paraphrased): not all of our soul came down; part of it always stays in the intelligible world. We just don't notice. Noticing has to pass through the soul's level of perception, and that level is crammed with the body's needs, pains, desires and noise. He compares it to activity somewhere in the body that we don't feel unless it reaches the part that perceives.

The "we" can move. That leads to a striking view of the self. "We" (hēmeis) isn't pinned to one level. Whatever level your attention is on, that is "you" right now. A person can live like an animal that eats and sleeps, or can live at the level of Intellect. It's the same soul either way. What differs is where it points its gaze. Picture someone standing in water: the lower body is submerged, but the head is always above the surface. The only question is whether attention goes to the cold water or to the sky.

Why it matters. This flips the logic of spiritual practice. You're not trying to get something you lack. You're trying to notice something you've always had and haven't been looking at. Salvation is a question of attention. It changes how you look at things: when you feel trapped, Plotinus would say only the part of you that came down is trapped, and you can choose which part you identify with. This is also the root of his disagreement with Christianity, and even with his own school (see the criticisms below).

5. Contemplation comes before action: even plants are gazing

What it is. On Nature, Contemplation and the One (III.8) opens with what sounds almost like a joke (paraphrased): what if we said that everything, not only rational animals but plants and the earth that bears them, longs for contemplation (theōria) and aims at it? Wouldn't that sound absurd? Then he argues, in earnest, that it's true.

He has Nature speak (III.8.4, paraphrased): I was born from contemplation and I love to contemplate; I gaze, and what I gaze at takes shape, the way figures flow from a geometer's thinking as he draws. Nature doesn't make things with its hands like a craftsman. It makes them like someone contemplating, the form spilling over from the gazing into something visible.

That leads to a counterintuitive conclusion: action is a weakened form of contemplation, its shadow. In his words (paraphrased), when people's power of contemplation grows weak, they turn to action as a shadow of contemplation, like children who are bad at abstract thinking and so go and make things with their hands. Nobody really wants action for its own sake. You act in order to see something: you want a thing done so you can see it finished and see that it's good.

Why it matters. It turns the modern ranking of values upside down. We tend to treat doing as real and thinking as idling. Plotinus says the reverse: action is what you fall back on when you can't see clearly. It changes how you see yourself: someone too busy to stop may not be overflowing with energy at all. They may just be weak at gazing, so they keep doing in order to make up for not seeing.

6. Beauty: not proportion but the presence of form

What it is. On Beauty (I.6) is one of Plotinus's earliest treatises and his most influential. He starts by knocking down the reigning Stoic view that beauty is proportion and symmetry among parts. The rebuttal is quick. If beauty were proportion between parts, simple things couldn't be beautiful. Yet a single color, sunlight, gold, lightning, the stars at night have no parts and are beautiful all the same. Elsewhere (VI.7.22) he adds a sharper example: a face is beautiful while alive; after death its proportions are exactly the same, but the beauty is gone. So beauty has to be something deeper than proportion: form, life, the light from above showing through matter.

Why does beauty move the soul? Because the soul recognizes its own kind. A beautiful thing carries form akin to the soul, and seeing it is like running into someone from home in a foreign country: a jolt of delight and longing. Ugliness is form failing to master matter, like a lump of clay nobody has shaped.

The ladder, and the statue. The ascent runs from beautiful bodies to beautiful actions and virtues, to beautiful knowledge, to the beauty of Intellect itself, and finally to the source of beauty. The most famous lines go like this (I.6.9, paraphrased): If you don't yet see beauty in yourself, do what a sculptor does with a statue that has to be made beautiful: cut away what's excess, straighten what's crooked, polish what's dull until it shines, and never stop working on your own statue until the godlike splendor of virtue shines out of it.

The beauty of the image is that sculpting subtracts. The statue is already in the stone; you just remove what doesn't belong to it. Right after comes another famous line (I.6.9, paraphrased): no eye ever saw the sun without first becoming sunlike, and no soul sees beauty without first becoming beautiful. Goethe later put the same thought into verse. Knowing isn't spectating, it's becoming like: you can only see what you're already turning into.

7. Evil is absence, and the world is still beautiful

What it is. As we saw, Plotinus defines evil as absence: matter is the far edge of the good, where the light runs out. Augustine took this over, and it became Christianity's standard answer, evil as the privation of good (privatio boni): evil has no substance of its own, so God didn't create it.

But that doesn't make Plotinus despise the world. He wrote a ferocious treatise, Against the Gnostics (II.9). The Gnostics were religious groups of his day who held that the material world was the botched work of an ignorant or even malicious lower creator, with human beings as divine sparks locked in a prison. Plotinus's reply: this cosmos is the finest possible image of the intelligible world, and to despise the cosmos is to despise its source. How can you claim to love the father while spitting on his child? The stars, the order of things, the turning seasons all speak of the beauty above.

What about actual suffering? In On Providence (III.2–3) he uses a theater image. The world is like a play: someone plays the hero, someone the villain, someone the character who gets killed. The playwright fits every part into the whole, and local misfortune gets its meaning from the structure of the whole, as you wouldn't call a painting a failure because some areas are dark. An actor "killed" on stage has only changed costume.

Why it matters. It puts Plotinus at a subtle midpoint. He's more affirming of the world than the Gnostics and more eager to leave it than the Stoics. The world deserves praise, but it's an image, not home. It changes how you hold suffering: you don't have to deny it or sanctify it. It's the dimness you'd expect far from the light, and your task isn't to curse the dark but to turn toward the light.

8. Return and union: take away everything

What it is. The whole system ends in the soul's union with the One (henōsis). But there's a structural problem. The One is beyond thought, so you can't think your way to it. Any state of "I am knowing it" still contains two things, me and it, and so isn't yet one. The last step isn't the completion of knowing but the letting go of it.

Plotinus's instruction is three words long (end of V.3.17, paraphrased): take away everything (aphele panta). Take away bodily sensation, take away images, take away concepts, and finally take away the thought "I am taking away." He offers images. Singers stand in a circle around their leader; facing outward, each sings his own line and goes off pitch, but turned toward the leader the whole choir is in tune. We're always circling the One; we're just often facing away (VI.9.8). Or two centers coinciding (VI.9.10): when the center of one circle sits on the center of another they're one, and only when they separate are they two. In that moment there's no seer and nothing seen. It's less "seeing," he says, than a standing-outside-oneself, ekstasis, the root of the English word ecstasy.

The last sentence of the Enneads (VI.9.11) runs, roughly: this is the life of gods and of godlike, blessed people, a release from everything here, a life that takes no pleasure in the things of this world, the flight of the alone to the alone (phygē monou pros monon). "Alone" doesn't mean lonely. It means simple, with everything added stripped off. Only a simple soul can meet absolute simplicity.

Porphyry reports (Life 23) that in the six years he was with Plotinus, his teacher reached this union four times, and that he himself reached it once in his life, at sixty-eight. This isn't a state you drop into at will. It's a moment occasionally given after a lifetime of practice. Plotinus's last words, as relayed by his student, the physician Eustochius, were roughly: try to bring the god in you back to the divine in the All. The text has variant readings, but the sense fits the whole book.

Why it matters. This is where the distillation of the whole book lands. Plotinus isn't just laying out a structure of the cosmos. He's drawing a map of an inner experience, and you're meant to walk that diagram of concentric circles from the outer ring to the center. It changes how you look at things: the deepest thing isn't far away. It's the point that's left once everything else has been taken away.

The Spine of the Argument

Here's what 54 treatises keep circling, said once:

(1) To be is to be one. Things exist by being one thing, so there must be an absolutely simple source of unity, the One, which lies beyond being and thought and can only be pointed at by denial. (2) Fullness overflows. The One gives off Intellect the way the sun gives off light; Intellect gives off Soul; Soul's last glimmer reaches matter. Each level takes shape by turning back to the one above, and the source loses nothing. (3) Levels of reality are levels of unity. The further down, the more scattered: Intellect sees everything at once and lives in eternity; Soul reasons step by step and lives out time; matter is where the light runs out, and evil is the absence of good. (4) Human beings span every level. Part of the soul never came down, and "we" are wherever our attention is. (5) So salvation is turning, not acquiring. Through virtue, beauty, philosophical dialectic and contemplation you gather yourself inward level by level, like a sculptor cutting away surplus stone, until you "take away everything" and, standing outside yourself, are one with the One.

What does he prove it with? Partly with rigorous philosophical argument: from "unity is the condition of existence" to the One; from "thinking requires distinction" to a One above thinking; from "the composite depends on the simple" to the hierarchy. Partly with a systematic reading of Plato. And partly, and this is his real trump card, with first-person reports of experience. The system is a map that starts from experience and is then braced with argument: the argument tells you there must be a center, and the experience tells you the center can be reached.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "emanation" means God parcels himself out into everything, so everything is God (pantheism). No. Plotinus says plainly that the One is in all things but nothing is the One; the source isn't diminished or divided by what flows from it. Sunlight covers the earth without the earth becoming the sun. A better formula would be "all things are in God, while God exceeds all things." The line is genuinely thin, though. Eckhart later drew it so close that he was charged with heresy.

Misreading 2: Plotinus hated the body and the world. Half right. Porphyry's opening line, that Plotinus "seemed ashamed of being in the body," is a real report. He refused to sit for a portrait (wasn't it enough to carry around the image nature had wrapped him in, without leaving an image of an image?), and he wouldn't talk about his parents or his birthplace. Yet he also wrote Against the Gnostics, the angriest treatise in the book, in defense of the cosmos's beauty. More precisely: what he downgraded wasn't the world but our fixation on it.

Misreading 3: it's an Eastern religion transplanted. Porphyry does report that Plotinus joined Emperor Gordian III's eastern campaign hoping to study Persian and Indian philosophy. But the emperor was killed and Plotinus barely escaped, and there's no evidence he ever encountered Indian texts. The resemblances to the Upanishads are parallels worth studying, not proof of influence.

Criticism 1: an ineffability that undoes itself. A man who says the One is unsayable wrote hundreds of pages about it. Plotinus saw this coming ("we speak about it, not it"), but take the negative road all the way and even "it is the source" or "it is the Good" should be withdrawn, and then the system loses its anchor. Every negative theology faces this problem.

Criticism 2: where does matter come from? If everything flows from the One, then matter, "evil itself," is also its product. Does the source of good give rise to evil itself? Scholars still argue over whether matter is generated by Soul and whether "evil is absence" fits with "matter is evil itself." The crack is real.

Criticism 3: objections from inside his own school. The later Neoplatonist Iamblichus rejected the undescended soul outright. If part of every soul never fell, everyone already has a direct line, so what are religious rites for? Iamblichus held that the whole soul descended and can't climb back by philosophical contemplation alone; it needs divine ritual, "theurgy." Proclus later sided with him. At bottom the argument is about whether salvation comes by one's own power or from outside. Christianity is on the "outside" side too: without grace, no way home.

Criticism 4: can experience prove metaphysics? Even granting that the experience of union is real and profound, there's a gap between "I experienced an undifferentiated oneness" and "the universe has an undifferentiated source." You can take Plotinus as a map for spiritual practice and stay skeptical of his cosmology. Note too that Porphyry's Life reads partly like a saint's life; "four unions" is a disciple's testimony, not a checkable record.

Criticism 5: the arrangement is already an interpretation. Porphyry's thematic ordering scrambles the order of composition and even scatters one long work across different books (III.8, V.8, V.5 and II.9 were originally four parts of a single treatise). Read in book order, the Enneads can look like a system designed in advance. In fact they're essays written over a decade and a half, alongside his teaching, and the concepts shift and strain from one to another.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · Huayan's Net of Indra: every jewel in the net reflects all the others, the Huayan school's image for "one is all." Plotinus pictures the intelligible world the same way: each is all. The difference: Huayan's mirroring is flat, with no higher source; Plotinus's is a hierarchy pointing up to the One.
  • Eastern philosophy · Shankara's Advaita: the eighth-century Shankara read Brahman as quality-less, reachable only by "not this, not this" (neti neti), close to Plotinus's negative talk of the One. Unlike: Advaita treats the world as illusion (maya); Plotinus insists it's a real, beautiful image.
  • Neuroscience · the default mode network: Judson Brewer and colleagues (2011) found experienced meditators show lower activity in the default mode network, the brain system busy when we think about ourselves — rather like "take away everything" removing the chattering self first. Unlike: brain scans find correlates of experience; they can't prove the One.
  • Psychology · William James: The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902) lists four marks of mystical states: ineffable, noetic (it feels like knowing), transient, passive. Plotinus's union hits all four. Unlike: James stays agnostic about what the experience is of.
  • Complexity science · emergence: emergence says higher order, in ant colonies or markets, grows bottom-up from simple local interactions. Plotinus runs the other way: the lower is a weakened image of the higher, and unity can only be borrowed from something more unified.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① Everything that exists exists by being one thing; unity isn't a property but the condition of being.

② The source of all unity, the One, is absolutely simple and beyond being and thought; you can only say what it isn't.

③ The One overflows into everything as the sun gives off light: no will, no moment in time, no loss to itself.

④ Each level takes shape by turning back toward its source: you become what you gaze at.

⑤ Intellect sees everything at once and lives in eternity; Soul reasons step by step, and time is the life of soul.

⑥ Evil isn't a force but the absence of good, the place the light doesn't reach.

⑦ Not all of the soul came down; whatever level your attention is on, that is "you" right now.

⑧ Action is the shadow of contemplation: when people can't see clearly, they keep doing.

⑨ Never stop working on your own statue (paraphrased); no eye sees the sun without first becoming sunlike.

⑩ Take away everything: the flight of the alone to the alone.
