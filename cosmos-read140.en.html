Cosmos

Cosmos · Carl Sagan · 1980

中文 →

In One Sentence

The first line sets the tone for everything after it: "The Cosmos is all that is or ever was or ever will be." What Sagan wants is for you to put yourself back at your true scale: life on a rock orbiting an ordinary star, built from atoms forged in stars that died long ago, and lucky enough to have evolved a brain that can work all this out, which makes us a way for the cosmos to know itself. That knowledge is humbling, and it is also a responsibility: don't destroy yourself before you've understood it.

Where It Sits

Sagan was a planetary scientist at Cornell who worked on NASA's Mariner, Viking and Voyager missions. Cosmos is the companion to his thirteen-part television series, released alongside it in 1980, and it remains one of the most influential works of popular science ever written. The chapters of the book map one-to-one onto the episodes.

The Core Argument

  • Cosmic scale isn't just a set of cold numbers. It's a way of seeing the human situation. Once you really feel how vast space is and how deep time goes, most human vanity, hatred and borders look very small. But that smallness doesn't lead to nihilism. It leads to kinship, because we share an origin with the stars.
  • Science is a way of thinking first and a body of knowledge second. At its heart, wonder and skepticism live together: imagine boldly, let the evidence decide, and be ready to admit you were wrong. Half the book isn't about stars at all. It's about how painfully humanity learned to think this way, climbing out of myth and superstition.
  • Understanding the universe and surviving as a species are the same question. The lifetime of a civilization may be the key variable in how many civilizations the galaxy holds. We are holding a telescope in one hand and nuclear weapons in the other, and which way we go is this generation's choice.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. The shore of the cosmic ocean: a sense of scale

What it is: Sagan's opening image is that the surface of the Earth is the shore of the cosmic ocean. Almost all of human history has been spent on that shore. Only recently have we waded in a little way, with a few Moon landings and a few probes flung outward, and the water is cold and deep and the far side is barely visible (paraphrased). The book keeps doing one thing: it takes something you think you know, sets it against its real scale, and makes you feel again how large or small it is.

In space, he zooms you out step by step: the Earth, the solar system, the Milky Way (one star among more than a hundred billion), groups of galaxies, and hundreds of billions of galaxies in the observable universe. Light circles the Earth more than seven times in a second, yet it takes a hundred thousand years to cross our galaxy. The starlight you see is an old letter. When it left its star, there may have been no humans yet.

In time, his most famous device is the Cosmic Calendar. It first appeared in his 1977 book The Dragons of Eden and became an iconic scene in the first episode of the TV series. You squeeze the whole history of the universe into one year, with the Big Bang at midnight on January 1. Using the roughly 15-billion-year age Sagan worked with (today's figure is about 13.8 billion), it looks roughly like this:

Date on the Cosmic Calendar (schematic)What happened
January 1The Big Bang
Around MayThe Milky Way forms
Early to mid SeptemberThe solar system and the Earth form
Late SeptemberThe earliest life appears on Earth
Mid to late DecemberThe Cambrian explosion, then fish, land plants and dinosaurs
December 31, around 10:30 p.m.The first humans
The last 10 seconds or so of December 31All of recorded human history

Schematic: dates follow Sagan's figures from the time and shift slightly with different estimates of the universe's age. The point is a single one. All of recorded human history fits into the last ten seconds of the year.

The sense of scale does one more thing that's easy to miss. It shows how humans can measure a huge world with very little. The first chapter tells the story of Eratosthenes, head of the Library of Alexandria in the third century BCE. He heard that at noon on the summer solstice, in the southern city of Syene, the sun lit the bottom of a well and upright columns cast no shadow, while at the same moment in Alexandria they did. If the Earth were flat, the shadows would match. Since they didn't, the surface had to be curved. The angle came to about seven degrees, roughly a fiftieth of a circle. The two cities were about 800 kilometres apart, so the Earth's circumference was about 40,000 kilometres, strikingly close to the modern figure. All he used were columns, shadows and reasoning.

Why it matters: many human prejudices are, at bottom, mistakes of scale, such as treating your tribe as the world or your lifetime as history. Sagan isn't telling you that you're small and therefore nothing matters. He wants you to trade "I am at the center of the world" for "I am at a very late point in a very large story."

How it changes the way you see: the next time you're locked in a fight over borders, sects or face, try pulling the camera back one notch, then another. By the third notch, many quarrels reveal their actual size.

2. Star-stuff: we were forged in stars

What it is: the book's most quoted line is "we are made of star-stuff." This isn't a poetic flourish. It names a specific physical process, stellar nucleosynthesis. When the universe was young, it held almost nothing but the lightest elements, hydrogen and helium. A star is a fusion furnace. The Sun converts about four million tonnes of mass into energy every second by fusing hydrogen into helium. When the hydrogen in the core runs out, a larger star goes on to fuse helium into carbon and climbs the ladder from there, making oxygen, nitrogen, silicon and so on, all the way up to iron. Iron is the end of the line. Fusing iron absorbs energy instead of releasing it, so the core of a massive star loses its support, collapses violently and blows up as a supernova, scattering everything the star made over its life into interstellar space. Most elements heavier than iron are also produced in violent events like these.

That scattered gas and dust is pulled together by gravity into new stars and planets. Our solar system is one of these later-generation products. Sagan's point, roughly in his words, is that the nitrogen in our DNA, the calcium in our teeth, the iron in our blood and the carbon in our apple pies were made in the interiors of collapsing stars. The ninth episode adds an equally famous line (paraphrased): if you wish to make an apple pie from scratch, you must first invent the universe.

An analogy: an iron atom is like an old coin that has changed hands many times. It was minted inside a star that died long ago, survived an explosion, drifted for billions of years, and finally ended up in your food and then in your hemoglobin, carrying oxygen to your brain.

Why it matters: it turns "the relationship between humans and the universe" from a metaphor into a material fact. Ancient religions said people and the heavens were connected somehow. Sagan says yes, and you can measure the connection with a spectrometer, because the spectra of stars contain the same elemental lines as your body. This is the model for the whole book: reverence without superstition. The most moving conclusions come from the coldest measurements.

How it changes the way you see: the material boundary of "me" becomes blurry and open. The atoms that make you up are billions of years older than you and will keep circulating after you die. That isn't mysticism. It's an accounting of the elements.

3. One voice in the cosmic fugue: life and selection

What it is: the second chapter is titled "One Voice in the Cosmic Fugue." A fugue is a piece of polyphonic music in which several voices each carry a melody and weave around each other. Sagan's point is that life on Earth is just one voice in the music of the cosmos. It's one possibility that grew out of a shared chemistry, and elsewhere there may be entirely different melodies.

He introduces natural selection through a Japanese legend. At the naval battle of Dan-no-ura in 1185, the Heike samurai were wiped out, and legend says their spirits became crabs. In those waters lives a "Heike crab" whose shell carries a pattern that looks like a scowling warrior's face. Sagan explains this as unintended artificial selection. Fishermen who hauled up crabs threw back the ones that looked like samurai faces out of reverence and ate the rest. Generation after generation, the more face-like crabs left more offspring. Nobody set out to breed them, yet people reshaped them. That's the power of selection. From artificial selection to natural selection is a single step: take away the fishermen, let the environment do the choosing, and give it billions of years.

Then he turns to the origin of life. In the 1950s, the Miller–Urey experiment sealed methane, ammonia, hydrogen and water in a flask and sent sparks through them to imitate lightning in the early atmosphere. Within days, amino acids had formed. Those are the building blocks of proteins. That isn't the same as making life, but it shows that the raw materials of life arise on their own under cosmic conditions and aren't rare. And every living thing on Earth, from oak trees to people, uses the same genetic code, which tells us we all share one ancestor.

Why it matters: the chapter ties two ideas together. Life on Earth is one family, and that family's very existence may be just one of many experiments in the universe. It sets up the question the second half of the book keeps asking: are there other voices out there?

How it changes the way you see: you start to see "human" as one branch of an enormously long and highly contingent family tree, not as the purpose of creation. Sagan doesn't find that bleak. It's precisely because we didn't have to happen that our existence is worth treasuring.

4. Kepler's eight minutes: from mysticism to method

What it is: one of the best chapters in the book tells how Johannes Kepler was talked round by data. The young Kepler was an ardent mystic. He believed God had built the world on geometry, and in his first book, Mysterium Cosmographicum, he proposed that the five regular solids (the cube, the tetrahedron and the rest) fit exactly into the gaps between the orbits of the six known planets. To him this was evidence of divine design, and it was intoxicatingly beautiful.

Later he got hold of decades of data from Tycho Brahe, the most precise naked-eye astronomer of the age. Fitting Mars to a traditional circular orbit, his best result still missed Tycho's observations by eight minutes of arc, about a quarter of the width of the full Moon. Almost anyone would have blamed the observations. Kepler instead said Tycho's data couldn't be that wrong. He gave up the circle, eventually found that planets move in ellipses, and arrived at what we now call Kepler's three laws, which became the foundation of Newton's theory of gravity. Kepler himself wrote that those eight minutes led to a complete reformation of astronomy (paraphrased).

What Sagan draws from this is the beating heart of scientific method: when your most cherished idea collides with reliable data, you choose the data. Kepler gave up the most beautiful idea of his life and got something true in return.

In the seventh chapter, "The Backbone of Night," Sagan traces this attitude back to Ionia, the Greek city-states on what is now Turkey's western coast, around the sixth century BCE. Thales, Anaximander, Democritus and others first tried to explain the world through natural causes. It wasn't angry gods but matter and law. Sagan calls this the starting point of the scientific spirit, and he casts the later Pythagoreans and Plato as the other road, which favored pure speculation, looked down on hands-on experiment, and kept knowledge for a select few.

Why it matters: this section answers "why should we trust science?" Scientists aren't smarter than anyone else. The method has error correction built in, because it requires you to hand your ideas over to evidence and to other people for testing. That's why science is more reliable than any individual.

How it changes the way you see: you start noticing your own regular solids, the explanations you can't let go of because they're so elegant or so flattering. You also become a little warier of authorities who never admit a mistake. Whether someone will overturn their own view for eight minutes of arc is a good test of whether they're really after the truth. In the TV series Sagan boiled this attitude down to a line that has been quoted ever since: extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. The more a claim overturns what we know, the stronger the evidence it needs.

5. Genes, brains, libraries: three leaps in memory

What it is: the eleventh chapter, "The Persistence of Memory," is about information. Sagan reads the history of life as a series of upgrades in how information gets stored:

  • Genes. At first, everything an organism could "know" was written in its DNA, from how to grow legs and digest food to what to run from. That knowledge is reliable but updates very slowly, through mutation and selection over many generations.
  • Brains. Then came nervous systems that could learn within a single lifetime. An animal no longer had to wait millions of years. In its own life it could remember where the water was and who was dangerous. The information stored in brains began to exceed what genes could hold.
  • Libraries. Finally, humans invented writing and began storing knowledge outside their bodies, what Sagan calls extrasomatic knowledge. When someone dies, what they figured out can survive them, to be read by strangers thousands of years later. Books let us talk with the dead we never met.

He makes the Library of Alexandria the symbol of that leap. It was the greatest store of knowledge in the ancient world, said to hold hundreds of thousands of scrolls, and home at various times to Eratosthenes, Euclid and Hypatia, a mathematician and astronomer murdered by a mob in 415 CE. In Sagan's telling, the loss of the Library is one of humanity's great tragedies. It wasn't only the books that were lost but a scientific age that might have arrived much sooner.

The same chapter covers the Voyager Golden Record. Sagan led the selection for this gold-plated copper disc, engraved with the sounds of Earth, music, greetings in many languages and images of humanity, and carried out of the solar system by the probes. It's the smallest library ever made, addressed to readers no one can name.

Why it matters: the framework explains why humans could change the Earth so fast. We are the only species that no longer relies on genes alone to pass on knowledge, and culture accumulates far faster than evolution. It also shows where a civilization is most fragile. Once extrasomatic memory is destroyed, it is truly gone.

How it changes the way you see: ordinary things like reading, archiving and teaching start to look like humanity's unique third memory system at work. You also take book burning, broken traditions and the hoarding of knowledge more seriously, since each of them damages that system.

6. The Drake equation and the lifetime of civilizations: the question comes home

What it is: the twelfth chapter, "Encyclopaedia Galactica," asks how many civilizations in our galaxy could communicate with us. In 1961 the astronomer Frank Drake broke that question down into a string of factors that can each be estimated separately and then multiplied. This is the Drake equation:

FactorMeaningHow much we know
R*How many stars form in the galaxy each yearEstimable from observation
fpFraction of stars with planetsUnknown when Sagan wrote; we now know planets are extremely common
nePlanets per system suitable for lifeGradually being measured
flFraction of those where life actually arisesUnknown (a sample of one)
fiFraction where life evolves intelligenceUnknown
fcFraction of intelligent species that develop detectable technology, such as radioUnknown
LHow long such a technological civilization lastsCompletely unknown, and up to the civilization itself

The Drake equation turns one hopeless question into seven that can be argued about one at a time. Uncertainty grows as you move down the list, and the last factor, L, is about us.

Plugging in what he considered reasonable, optimistic values, Sagan concluded that the galaxy might hold a great many civilizations. But he stressed that the whole result is controlled almost entirely by the last term, L. If technological civilizations destroy themselves soon after acquiring nuclear weapons, very few may exist at any one time. If they can survive that adolescence and last for millions of years, the galaxy may be teeming with them (paraphrased). So the search for extraterrestrial intelligence (SETI) is really also a question about ourselves: can a technological civilization survive?

That leads into the last chapter, "Who Speaks for Earth?" When Sagan was writing, there were tens of thousands of nuclear warheads in the world. He imagines an alien historian writing Earth's entry in the Encyclopaedia Galactica: here is a species that had only just learned to explore the cosmos when it built weapons capable of wiping itself out. His answer is that nation-states speak only for themselves, so someone has to speak for the whole Earth, and that planetary citizen's view is exactly what the cosmic perspective has to offer.

He uses the same comparative method on our neighboring planets. Venus may once have been milder, but today a runaway greenhouse effect from its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere keeps its surface at over 400°C. Mars, meanwhile, is cold and dry. Our two neighbors show two different ways a climate can go wrong, which means the Earth's habitability can no longer be taken for granted.

Why it matters: it connects gazing at the stars with the ground under your feet. The ultimate questions of cosmology end up landing on nuclear disarmament and climate, things humans can actually decide.

How it changes the way you see: once you picture human civilization as a specimen being watched by outsiders, it becomes much clearer that our technical power is growing faster than our wisdom, and that L is a number our generation is writing right now.

The Spine of the Book

The book looks like a ramble, taking in Kepler's mother on trial as a witch, the canyons of Mars and the songs of whales, but it argues only one thing. Humans are a very late form of life in the universe, made of star-stuff and shaped by natural selection. We have only just learned a method, science, that lets us see all this clearly, and that method is both our greatest achievement and the condition of our survival.

The chain of evidence is built in five links. First, physics and astronomy tell us how big and old the universe is and where the elements in our bodies came from (scale and star-stuff). Second, biology tells us that life is chemistry given a great deal of time and that all life on Earth shares one origin (evolution). Third, the history of science shows how humans learned, step by step, to reason from evidence instead of myth, and how often that path nearly broke off (Ionia, Kepler, Alexandria). Fourth, the information view explains why we could change the world so quickly (genes, then brains, then libraries). Finally, the Drake equation and the comparison of planets gather all of it into one practical question: can a species holding this kind of power live long enough? Awe is the starting point, method is the road, and survival is the stake.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

  • Misreading: "it's an outdated astronomy primer." Plenty of the data has aged, including the age of the universe, exoplanets (none confirmed then, thousands now) and accelerating expansion (discovered only in 1998). But the substance of the book is a perspective and a method, and those haven't dated. Read the specific numbers as a snapshot of 1980.
  • Criticism 1: history of science as heroes and villains. Sagan casts the Ionians as pioneers of science and the Pythagoreans and Plato as mystics who suppressed experiment. Historians of science have criticized this as Whig history, judging the past by who turned out to win. In fact Kepler himself was deeply Platonist, and it was exactly his belief in a mathematical order to the cosmos that drove him to fit the data.
  • Criticism 2: the Library and Hypatia are romanticized. Sagan implies that the Library's destruction and Hypatia's death were two scenes from the same Christian anti-intellectual tide. Historians point out that the Library more likely declined over centuries through war, lost funding and political turmoil, and there's no reliable evidence of a single burning in Hypatia's time. Her murder also had a tangled local political backdrop. The historian of science David Lindberg specifically rebuts the claim that the rise of Christianity killed ancient science in Galileo Goes to Jail and Other Myths about Science and Religion (2009).
  • Criticism 3: the Heike crab story probably doesn't hold up. Later researchers, including the crustacean biologist Joel Martin, noted that Heike crabs are too small for anyone to bother eating. The ridges on their shells mark where muscles attach, and similar patterns show up in fossil crabs that no human ever sorted. The story works better as a thought experiment about how artificial selection operates than as evidence of it.
  • Criticism 4: too optimistic about alien civilizations. Most of the Drake equation's factors are still guesses. Swap in a different but equally reasonable set of numbers and the answer can swing from "millions" to "only us." The Fermi paradox (if civilizations are so common, where is everybody?) is still unresolved. Sagan's figures are best read as a stance of openness, not as an estimate.
  • Criticism 5: the charge of scientism. Some argue that Sagan offers science as a substitute worldview for religion, preaches it with missionary zeal, and underrates the questions of value that science can't answer. To be fair, Sagan acknowledged that science can't tell us what we ought to do. But he did believe that value judgments made in ignorance of what the world is really like are built on sand. You can disagree, but you should know that this is where he stands.

Sagan Beyond This Book

Read only Cosmos and you get the lyrical Sagan. Two later books add the sharper and heavier sides of his thinking.

The Demon-Haunted World (1995): a toolkit for doubt. If Cosmos is about the wonder science brings, this book is about the skepticism it demands. Its best-known part is the "baloney detection kit," a set of tools for testing claims. Look for independent confirmation of the facts. Encourage substantive debate among knowledgeable people from all sides. Give arguments from authority little weight, since authorities have been wrong before. Consider several hypotheses and see which can be ruled out. Don't fall for an idea just because it's yours. Quantify where you can. Every link in a chain of argument has to hold. When two hypotheses explain the data equally well, prefer the simpler one (Occam's razor). Ask whether the claim could, in principle, be falsified. The book also has the "dragon in my garage" thought experiment. Someone tells you a fire-breathing dragon lives in their garage, but it's invisible, it floats, its fire gives off no heat, and every possible test has been ruled out in advance. Sagan asks: what's the difference between a dragon that can't be detected in any way and no dragon at all? The book also warns that a society growing ever more dependent on technology while fewer and fewer people understand science will slide toward superstition and pseudoscience. That's the social version of Cosmos's claim that science is a way of thinking.

Pale Blue Dot (1994): ourselves, seen from far away. In 1990, at Sagan's urging, Voyager 1 turned around at about six billion kilometres from home, on its way out of the solar system, and photographed the Earth. In the frame the Earth is a speck, less than a pixel, caught in a band of scattered light. From this Sagan wrote his famous passage (paraphrased): look again at that dot; that's here, that's home, that's us; on it everyone you love and everyone you've ever heard of lived out their lives; all the emperors and generals spilled rivers of blood so that, in glory and triumph, they could become the momentary masters of a fraction of a dot. The photograph compresses the scale of Cosmos into a single image and states its ethical conclusion more bluntly: we have a responsibility to be kinder to each other and to cherish this planet, because there is nowhere else for us to go.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · dependent origination and Indra's net: the Huayan school pictures reality as a net in which every jewel reflects every other, and all things arise in mutual dependence. Star-stuff likewise says you're made of the rest of the universe and will give it back. Both deny a self-contained "I". The difference is that Buddhism is about dependence itself, while Sagan is about where matter comes from.
  • Neuroscience · awe and the "small self": research by the psychologist Dacher Keltner and others finds that awe in the face of vastness makes people feel their self shrink and makes them more helpful. Astronauts' "overview effect" on seeing Earth from orbit is a cousin of this. Sagan's prose is, in effect, a way of inducing it.
  • Quantum philosophy · Wheeler's participatory universe: the physicist John Wheeler drew a letter U in which the universe evolves observers at one end, and their eye looks back at its origin. He called it a self-excited circuit. Its structure matches "the cosmos knowing itself," but Wheeler speculated that observation helps bring reality into being, while Sagan claims only that we happen to understand it.
  • Western philosophy · Spinoza's "God or Nature": Spinoza identified God with the whole of nature and its laws, and Einstein said this was the God he believed in. Sagan's reverence runs on the same line, awe toward the laws themselves rather than a personal God who answers prayers. The difference is that Spinoza reasoned geometrically, while Sagan used telescopes.
  • Distributed systems · single points of failure and replication: the Library of Alexandria was a single store of knowledge, and once it was destroyed the contents were gone for good. The engineering fix is redundant copies, which is the idea behind the libraries' LOCKSS ("Lots of Copies Keep Stuff Safe") project. Genes got there first, with a copy in every cell. The analogy breaks because every copy drifts. Replication is never perfect.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. The Cosmos is all that is or ever was or ever will be, and we have only just started wading in from the shore.

2. Squeeze cosmic history into a year and all of recorded human history fills the last ten seconds.

3. The shadow of a column was enough to measure the Earth. Science's power lies in reasoning, not instruments.

4. The iron in our blood and the calcium in our bones were forged in dying stars. We are star-stuff.

5. Life on Earth is one voice in the cosmic fugue. It all shares one origin, and none of it was inevitable.

6. Kepler gave up his most beautiful theory over eight minutes of arc. When a cherished idea collides with reliable data, choose the data.

7. Life has upgraded how it stores knowledge three times, from genes to brains to libraries, and books let us talk with the dead.

8. Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. Holding wonder and skepticism together is the temperament of science.

9. The Drake equation's answer hinges on its last term. How long a technological civilization lasts is something it writes for itself.

10. We are a way for the cosmos to know itself (paraphrased), so someone has to speak for the whole Earth.
