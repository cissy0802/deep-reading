Moral Mazes

Moral Mazes: The World of Corporate Managers · Robert Jackall · 1988 (updated edition 2010)

中文 →

In one sentence

The managers inside big corporate bureaucracies are not bad people, but the structure they live in quietly swaps "is it right?" for "does the boss want it?", "how will it look?" and "whose desk will this land on?"—there, morality stops being a set of principles and becomes a habit of glancing upward; the smart ones learn not to do the right thing but to stay upstream of the credit and downstream of the blame.

Where it sits

Jackall is a sociologist at Williams College. In the early 1980s he spent several years doing fieldwork inside a handful of large American corporations, talking at length and repeatedly with managers at every level. The result is one of the rare ethnographies of the corporation from the inside—ethnography being the method of immersing yourself in a group for a long stretch and describing its internal rules through observation and interviews. Business schools, ethicists and, later, the tech world have treated it ever since as the coolest-headed account of office politics in print. It doesn't teach you how to win; it lays the rules of the game flat on the table.

Core claims

  • Morality in the corporation is situational: what's right depends on what the person above you wants. A former vice-president quoted in the book has been cited for decades; his point, paraphrased, was that what is right at home or in church is not necessarily right at the company—at the company, right is whatever the guy above you wants from you, and that is what morality means there. Jackall argues this isn't one man's cynicism but a product of bureaucratic structure itself.
  • The link between success and effort or ability is cut. In large bureaucracies, promotion hinges on how bosses and peers judge you socially—whether you're "one of us," a "team player," in the right circle, and lucky—rather than on verifiable performance. Because performance is so hard to attribute, judgment falls back on impressions.
  • The structure of responsibility is inverted: credit flows up, while details and consequences are pushed down and pushed forward in time. Decision-makers get promoted faster than the consequences of their decisions show up, so short-term numbers are rewarded and long-term costs land on whoever happens to be in the seat later. The whole organization is systematically biased toward short-sightedness and self-protection, even when everyone in it considers himself decent.

The key ideas, one by one

1. The bureaucratic ethic: from the Protestant ethic to looking up

What it is. Jackall starts from Max Weber. In The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (READ 11 on this site), Weber argued that early capitalism was powered by a religious work ethic: Calvinists believed God had already predestined who would be saved and that no one could know, so they treated diligent work and accumulated wealth as signs that they might be among the elect. There was a visible moral bond between work and reward—be industrious, frugal and trustworthy, and you deserve to prosper. Jackall says that bond snapped in the twentieth-century corporation. What replaced it he calls the bureaucratic ethic: in an organization of layered reporting lines, your fate sits in your boss's hands, so "what is right" gets redefined as "what satisfies those above and keeps me and my boss safe."

The analogy. Jackall draws a telling parallel himself. The Calvinist God decided arbitrarily and unknowably who would be saved; in the big corporation, career fate looks just as arbitrary and unknowable—except the deity is now your boss and your boss's boss. The old ethic said "work hard and you'll be seen"; the new one says "being seen is what counts as working hard."

The mechanism. Why does this happen? Because in a large organization most outcomes can't be pinned on one person. A product sells well: was it R&D, sales, the market, or the groundwork laid by your predecessor? With no objective attribution, evaluation has to rely on superiors' impressions—and those superiors are judged by their own superiors, so everyone at every level has good reason to keep their eyes on what's above. Jackall noticed that when managers discussed ethical questions they rarely used the language of right and wrong; they used the language of practicality: will this cause trouble, can it be defended, how will it look upstairs? Moral questions are systematically translated into tactical ones.

How it changes your view. When an otherwise upright person says something in a meeting he plainly doesn't believe, you may blame his character. Jackall asks you to ask a different question: who controls this person's incentives, information and exits? In a bureaucracy, conscience doesn't vanish; it gets bracketed. Many of his informants admitted privately that they lived by one set of rules at home and another at work.

2. Fealty and patronage: becoming "one of us"

What it is. The corporation Jackall describes looks more like a feudal system than a merit-sorting machine. He uses the word fealty—originally the sworn loyalty of a medieval vassal to his lord—for the relation between boss and subordinate: the subordinate offers loyalty, service and credit; the boss offers protection, opportunity and promotion. The company is crisscrossed by circles and patronage networks (relationships in which the powerful protect and advance their people in exchange for loyalty) built around particular executives. Your prospects depend heavily on whose name you hang under, and on how that patron himself rises or falls.

The unwritten rules of fealty. From his interviews Jackall distilled a set of expectations bosses hold of subordinates, roughly: don't go around your boss to someone higher up; tell your boss what he wants to hear, even when he says he welcomes dissent; if he wants something dropped, drop it; be sensitive enough to his wishes to anticipate them, so he never has to act like a boss; and if there's something he doesn't want known, your job is to cover it, not report it (all paraphrased). None of these rules is written in any employee handbook, yet nearly every manager he interviewed could recite them.

The social criteria of success. Since performance is hard to measure objectively, promotion runs on soft criteria: are you seen as a team player, do you fit in, are your style and appearance right, do you control your emotions, do you come across as flexible rather than rigid? At the core of all of these, Jackall argues, is predictability and reliability—those above need to be sure you won't embarrass them at the critical moment. Someone too principled, too exacting, too insistent on putting bad news on the table can be tagged "not one of us" however capable he is.

The shake-up. The flip side of fealty networks is fragility. The book describes the chain reaction at a large conglomerate he calls "Covenant Corporation" after a change at the top (details paraphrased): the new chief brings in his own people; members of the old circles are reassigned, sidelined or shown the door; people with bright futures discover overnight that they "backed the wrong horse." You're betting not only on your own performance but on your patron's fate in the next turn of the wheel. So managers are always doing two things: looking up (reading the boss) and looking around (reading which way the wind is blowing).

How it changes your view. Jackall rewrites "is this workplace fair?" as "what information does evaluation here run on?" The vaguer the information, the more weight relationships and impressions carry—not moral decay, but the natural drift of an organization that lacks reliable measurement.

3. Credit flows up, details are pushed down

What it is. Jackall observed a stable distribution of responsibility in big companies. Senior people push the details of execution and specific decisions downward, keeping themselves in the position of "only setting direction." When there's success, credit gathers upward along the reporting line; when there's trouble, the hunt for responsibility stops at the layer closest to the details. Most of the companies he studied were run as "decentralized operations, centralized control": divisions were profit centers, nominally autonomous but obliged to deliver headquarters' numbers every quarter. Pressure is concentrated; responsibility is dispersed.

The analogy. Picture a dam's spillway. The decisions made upstream—how high the targets are set, how tight the budgets are squeezed—create the water pressure, but the valves are installed downstream, and whoever stands next to the valve faces the flood. The people upstream can always say, "I only set the target; how you hit it was up to you."

The mechanism. No villain designed this. It's the sum of many self-interested choices: those at the top naturally want deniability—the ability to say later, "I didn't know the details"; those below, to deliver, tend to carry out the boss's intent in ways that leave no trace—verbally rather than in writing, by hint rather than instruction; and collective decision-making and layered sign-offs dilute responsibility until no one truly owns the outcome. Jackall reports a widely shared piece of managerial wisdom: put as little as possible of anything important on paper, because paper stays put while people move on (paraphrased).

How it changes your view. When a company scandal breaks, the public asks "who gave the order?"—and the answer is often "no one, explicitly." Jackall makes you see that in a bureaucracy the worst harm often needs no order at all—only everyone guessing correctly what those above want, and no one leaving fingerprints.

4. Outrunning your mistakes

What it is. This is the book's sharpest and most quoted idea. Managers usually stay in a post only two or three years before rotating or moving up, while the consequences of many decisions—aging equipment, safety hazards, lost customers, technical debt—take five or ten years to surface. The result is a rational strategy, which informants described in roughly these words: the trick is to outrun your mistakes—to be gone from the job before the consequences catch up with you.

The standard move: milking. The commonest form is milking a business: cutting maintenance, deferring equipment replacement, slashing R&D and training. On this quarter's statement those are costs, so cutting them is instant profit; their price is slow, hidden and delayed. A manager good at milking turns in handsome numbers and gets promoted on them; the hole he leaves is discovered by his successor three years later.

The coke ovens. The book contains a much-retold case (details paraphrased): a battery of coke ovens at one plant—the large furnaces that turn coal into coke and need periodic rebuilding—required a big maintenance outlay. The executive responsible deferred it to protect current profits, posted good numbers, and was promoted. Some years later the ovens deteriorated badly; the cost of fixing them dwarfed the original maintenance bill, and the blame fell on whoever happened to be in the job at that point—someone who hadn't made the decision but was "there."

Schematic: when a decision-maker's tenure is shorter than the time it takes consequences to surface, credit is booked to the decision-maker and the cost to the successor. Numbers and timing are illustrative, not data from the book.

Why it matters. It turns what looks like a question of personal character ("he's so short-sighted") into a structural one: whenever tenure is shorter than the time for consequences to show, and the organization doesn't track a decision's long-run results, short-sightedness is the rational choice. Jackall points out that big companies generally lack any accounting that connects a decision made years ago to its consequences today; the faster people move, the cleaner the break. In such a world, the manager who prudently invests for the long run may be the one weeded out for numbers that aren't pretty enough.

How it changes your view of the world. Next time you hear the story of a new executive who delivers dazzling results within three years, you'll ask: what did his predecessor leave him, and what is he leaving his successor? To judge a decision-maker, look less at the numbers on his watch than at the same job five years after he left it.

5. Dexterity with symbols: language, image and "defensibility"

What it is. Jackall sees skill with language and image as one of the core managerial competencies. Managers say different things in different settings, always phrase things with room to retreat, and are adept at making bad news sound neutral and forced choices sound like bold strategy. The book devotes extended attention to a large public-relations firm and how it crafts public images for clients (details paraphrased): in that line of work, "truth" is often treated as something to be managed—what matters is not the facts themselves but which version the public will accept.

A morality that can be adjusted. Closely tied to this is what Jackall calls an acute alertness to expediency—asking only what is convenient and advantageous right now, not what principle requires. Managers learn to ask first, "what happens to me if I say this now?" and only then decide whether and how to say it. The book discusses how a textile company he calls "Weft Corporation" handled the regulation of cotton dust, which causes lung disease in mill workers (details paraphrased): internal debate centered less on workers' health than on compliance costs, how strict regulators would be, and how to frame the issue publicly.

What happens to whistle-blowers. The book also records a few people who insisted on doing things by the book. One of them, a financial officer he calls Brady, working at "Alchemy Inc.," the chemical subsidiary of Covenant, discovered improper accounting practices higher up and reported them through channels (details paraphrased). The problem wasn't fixed; instead he came to be regarded as unreliable and was eventually pushed out. Jackall's point isn't how dark this is but that other managers, judging Brady, generally concluded he had made a mistake—not a moral one, but a failure to understand the rules, an error of judgment. That is the bureaucratic ethic in its purest form: even onlookers have replaced "was it right?" with "was it smart?" as the measure of a person.

How it changes your view. When an organization's language grows ever vaguer, more upbeat and harder to falsify, it's usually not a sign of better communication but a sign that responsibility is being diluted. Whether a sentence can be held against its speaker later matters far more than how polished it sounds.

The skeleton

The book compresses into one chain: in a large organization individual results are hard to attribute → so evaluation rests on superiors' judgments and impressions → so people direct their attention and loyalty upward, forming networks of fealty and patronage → credit flows up the reporting line while details and responsibility are pushed down, and job rotation decouples decisions from the people who made them → short-term numbers are rewarded and long-term costs are passed on, making "outrunning your mistakes" a rational strategy → language and image become tools of self-protection, and moral questions are translated into questions of prudence → in the end, decent individuals jointly produce an organization that is not decent.

What holds it up? Not statistics but the interviews and cases piled up over years of fieldwork. Jackall presents the managers' own words and their own explanations, so you see that these rules are not an outsider's indictment but common sense that insiders use daily and tacitly share. The book's persuasive force comes from "this is how they describe it themselves."

Common misreadings & criticisms

Misreading 1: the book says managers are bad people. The opposite. Most of Jackall's managers are respectable, family-minded people with private consciences. His argument is structural: bad outcomes don't need bad people—only a structure in which decent people act reasonably and the sum comes out unreasonable.

Misreading 2: it's a guide to office politics. The rules can certainly be read as a survival manual, but Jackall is describing, not recommending. Using the book as a playbook drops you right into the logic it describes: asking only whether something is smart, never whether it is right.

Criticism 1: sample and era. The fieldwork dates from the early 1980s, concentrated in a few companies in traditional manufacturing such as chemicals and textiles, at a time of decline, mergers and layoffs; most informants were white male managers. That setting may have amplified insecurity and politicking. Generalizing it to every organization calls for care.

Criticism 2: representativeness and selectivity. Ethnography's strength is depth; its weakness is that it's hard to verify and generalize. Companies and people are pseudonymous, so readers can't check them, and which stories the researcher chooses to tell already reflects a stance. Critics argue Jackall picked the darkest cases, and that the people most willing to talk candidly are often those who have left or are disaffected, which would make the picture grimmer than reality. Jackall's reply is that the same rules recurred across companies and levels, which shows they are structural; but "recurring" also depends partly on whom he asked and how.

Criticism 3: does it still hold in today's flatter companies? This is the question readers ask most. On one hand, tech companies have fewer layers, more transparent information and much more job-hopping, and some practices—such as postmortems that probe the system rather than the person—explicitly aim to counter blame culture. On the other hand, Jackall's core mechanisms seem to survive in new forms: promotion committees and performance calibration still run on visibility and impressions; project narratives still decide who walks off with the credit; and frequent job-hopping is the ultimate version of outrunning your mistakes—you can change companies before the technical debt comes due. Flattening the hierarchy may not dissolve the maze; it may just make it harder to see.

Criticism 4: no way out. The diagnosis is powerful, but the book offers almost no prescription. It makes the maze visible yet says little about which institutional designs could ease it, or why some organizations are healthier than others. The 2010 updated edition adds an afterword extending the analysis to finance and the crisis that followed (paraphrased), but the overall tone remains diagnosis rather than cure.

Later life. Three decades after publication the book found a second readership in tech and rationalist circles—for instance through a series of essays the writer Zvi Mowshowitz published on the LessWrong forum in 2019–2020, which tied the degree of "mazedness" to the number of layers of middle management, arguing that the more layers, the more an organization is swallowed by maze logic (paraphrased). That is a later extension, not Jackall's own claim.

Neighbors on this site (our comparisons, not Jackall's). Pfeffer's Power (READ 972) urges you to face organizational politics and go get power; Jackall shows what that game wears away morally. Pfeffer's Leadership BS (READ 974) documents the gap between leadership rhetoric and actual behavior—the flip side of Jackall's public talk versus private rules. Argyris's Overcoming Organizational Defenses (READ 962) describes the same split between word and deed as "espoused theory versus theory-in-use," but believes it can be unlearned, which makes him more hopeful than Jackall. And the first of Greene's 48 Laws of Power (READ 971), "never outshine the master," is practically the street version of Jackall's rules of fealty.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · Milgram's obedience experiments: In the 1960s Stanley Milgram had subjects administer ever-stronger "shocks" to a learner (an actor) on an experimenter's orders, and most went all the way. He explained it as the "agentic state": people see themselves as instruments of another's will and hand their sense of responsibility upward. Alike in that bureaucracy also passes moral responsibility up the chain; unlike in that Milgram's subjects had someone giving orders in the room, while Jackall's managers often guess what's wanted before anyone says a word.
  • Philosophy · Arendt's "banality of evil": In Eichmann in Jerusalem, Hannah Arendt argued that vast evil can come from ordinary functionaries who don't think, only process. Alike in that a role can excuse a person from thinking; unlike in scale and kind, and historians have since strongly challenged Arendt's reading of Eichmann himself.
  • Economics · Goodhart's law: The economist Charles Goodhart observed that once a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Alike in that milking is exactly optimizing quarterly profit as a target; unlike in that Goodhart describes a statistical relationship breaking down, while Jackall describes the career calculus of the people inside it.
  • Distributed systems · blameless postmortems: After outages, engineering teams such as Google's SRE groups run "blameless postmortems" that ask where the system failed rather than who to punish, because blame makes people hide information. Alike in recognizing that scapegoating wrecks an organization's information flow; unlike in that engineers build this deliberately as an institution, while Jackall describes a state no one designed.
  • Buddhism · karma and intention: Buddhism teaches that intention (cetana) is itself karma, and its fruits follow the actor wherever he goes—no change of post sheds them. Alike in asking who owns the consequences; unlike in that the corporation has built precisely a mechanism that detaches consequences from actors: karma can't be outrun, but a mistake on the job can.

Ten sentences to keep

1. In the big corporation, what is right is often just what the person above you wants (a former vice-president's words, paraphrased).

2. The bureaucratic ethic has replaced the Protestant ethic: success is no longer the reward for diligence but the product of a superior's judgment, as arbitrary and unknowable as predestination.

3. When performance can't be attributed, evaluation runs on impressions, so being "one of us" matters more than doing well.

4. Boss and subordinate are bound by fealty: loyalty and credit flow up, protection and opportunity flow down—and you're also betting on your patron's fate.

5. The unwritten rules of fealty, roughly: don't go around your boss, tell him what he wants to hear, and cover for what he doesn't want known.

6. Credit flows up the reporting line while details and consequences are pushed down and forward in time; the worst harm often needs no explicit order from anyone.

7. As long as tenure is shorter than the time for consequences to surface, short-sightedness is rational: the trick is to leave before your mistakes catch you (paraphrased).

8. The blame lands on whoever is in the seat when things blow up, not on whoever made the decision.

9. When even onlookers judge a whistle-blower by whether he was smart rather than whether he was right, morality has already been translated into tactics.

10. This is a 1980s field report with a limited sample and a dark cast, yet its mechanisms find new forms in today's flatter world: bad outcomes don't need bad people, only a structure in which good people act reasonably.
