Don Quixote

Don Quijote de la Mancha · Miguel de Cervantes · Part I 1605 / Part II 1615

中文 →

In One Sentence

A fiftyish country gentleman reads so many chivalric romances that he decides to go out and be a knight himself — and the reason the book is still standing after four hundred years is not the two pages about the windmills. It is that this is the first book to take seriously the question of what happens to a person who insists on living inside a story the world has stopped telling, and its answer is far crueller than mockery: nobody corrects him. They start playing along. And the real madness begins there.

Where It Sits

Almost nothing in Cervantes's life (1547–1616) went well. At twenty-four he lost the use of his left hand at the naval battle of Lepanto; sailing home he was seized by corsairs and spent five years as a slave in Algiers, escaping four times and failing four times. Ransomed, he requisitioned grain for the Armada, collected taxes, and did jail time over the accounts. Part I appeared when he was fifty-seven, and what it took aim at was the era's blockbuster genre: the libros de caballerías, the chivalric romances. That is exactly where the book sits. Cervantes did not withdraw to invent some higher literature; he took the pulp everybody was reading, handed it to a reader who believed it, and marched him out into the real, tax-collecting, bad-inn Spain of 1600. The genre shattered on contact — and out of the wreckage came the basic move of the modern novel: put two incompatible languages in one room and refuse to referee.

The Central Claims

  • He is not the only madman; nobody in this book meets the world unmediated. Shepherds conduct their love affairs by pastoral poetry, dukes stage their amusements out of romance, the priest runs his errands out of a moral pamphlet. Quixote's peculiarity is only that his script has gone out of fashion — and that he refuses to run it quietly, indoors, like everyone else.
  • Reality cannot refute a closed system of explanation. Whenever fact collides with belief, he summons an enchanter to absorb the difference. This is not stupidity; it is the characteristic disease of clever people. An explanation flexible enough will translate every disconfirmation into further confirmation.
  • Worse than a delusion is a delusion that gets catered to. In Part I the world simply hits him. In Part II, people who have read Part I build sets, hire actors and write plot for him — he finally succeeds, and that is when he comes apart. Who is madder, the tricked or the trickers? The book pointedly declines to give you a safe seat.

The Core Ideas, One by One

"His brains dried up": the hero is, first of all, a reader

Cervantes will not even fix the man's surname — Quixada, Quesada, Quixana, "it makes little difference." He is an hidalgo: the lowest rung of hereditary Spanish gentry, the kind with a coat of arms, a stew pot and no money. Forty-something going on fifty, with rusted ancestral armour in the attic. The etiology is stated flatly: from reading too much and sleeping too little, his brains dried up and he lost his wits. What he had been reading was Amadís de Gaula and its descendants — sixteenth-century mega-sellers in which knights fight giants, rescue princesses and stay chaste for ladies they have never met.

Why is that premise revolutionary? Because before him, literary madness came from fate, from the gods, from love, from an imbalance of humours. Don Quixote is the first character in literature who becomes what he becomes because of what he has read. His madness is not a clinical condition; it is a format error. He has taken the rules of one genre — romance — and executed them inside another: the world of prices, laws and inn bills he actually lives in.

None of which has aged. Anyone who takes romantic comedy as a dating manual, founder mythology as a career plan, or an ideology as a street map is making the identical mistake — not believing something false, but misjudging where the book applies. Cervantes is not mocking reading. He is mocking the failure to convert units: executing a rule without first asking whether it still holds here. And the book's real ambush is that after three hundred pages of laughing, you notice how many unconverted books you are running on yourself.

"The enchanters did it": an unfalsifiable explanation machine

The windmills take up barely two pages (Part I, ch. 8). He charges thirty or forty of them, calls them giants; Sancho says windmills; he charges anyway, his lance jams in a sail and horse and rider are flung across the field. The dangerous part is the sentence that follows: "that sage Frestón, who stole my books and study, has turned these giants into windmills to rob me of the glory of defeating them."

Look at the structure: prediction fails → belief is not revised → an auxiliary hypothesis is added (an unseen enemy is transforming things) → the belief comes out stronger than before (if enchanters are working against me, imagine how dangerous I must be). Philosophers of science call this unfalsifiability: a claim that no possible fact could overturn. It looks all-powerful and is in fact empty, because it has stopped making any promise about the world at all.

Every "enchanter" in the book is the same machine restarting. The inn is a castle, and the beating he takes there is enchantment working by night. Two flocks of sheep are two armies, and when the shepherds' stones knock out several of his teeth, that is enchanters turning armies into sheep. He always explains in whichever direction is cheapest — and the price is that he can no longer learn anything from reality.

This is the single most usable tool the novel hands a modern reader: to judge whether a belief is healthy, don't ask how much it explains; ask what it would accept as a refutation. A worldview in which every counter-example proves that someone is out to get you — call it conspiracy, call it destiny, call it "they're just jealous" — is Frestón in current dress. Quixote is not slow. Every inference he makes is fluent. The defect is that his system contains no seat from which the world can tell him he is wrong.

The baciyelmo: two people can be right at once

In Part I, ch. 21, he takes a brass basin a barber is wearing on his head against the rain, certain it is the fabled golden helmet of Mambrino. Sancho can see perfectly well that it is a basin, and says nothing. Twenty-odd chapters later the barber catches up with them at an inn and the room divides, half shouting basin, half — for the fun of it — shouting helmet. Sancho settles it by coining a word: baciyelmo, basin-helmet, welded out of bacía and yelmo.

That invented word is the hinge of the whole novel. It is not a compromise; it is a position: the same object is a basin inside the barber's system of uses and a helmet inside Quixote's system of meanings, and the language contains no single word able to hold both — so one has to be built. Note what Cervantes does not do: no narrator steps forward to rule that it is really just a basin. The argument is left standing on the page.

Three centuries later the Spanish philosopher José Ortega y Gasset built his Meditations on Quixote (1914) on that hinge, into the doctrine of perspectivism: everyone sees the world from a particular position, and that position is not an impurity blocking the truth but the condition under which any truth can appear at all. That is what his famous line — "I am I and my circumstance" — actually means. There is no view from nowhere; which is not the same as saying anything goes, because the basin really does hold water and really will not stop a sword.

How it changes what you see: the next time you are deadlocked over a fact, ask first whether the two of you are naming one object out of two different systems of use. In arguments about a policy, a relationship, a layoff, both sides are frequently seeing something true — different uses of the same event. What resolves it is often not a verdict about who is wrong, but a baciyelmo: a coinage roomy enough for both truths.

Dulcinea: love as an act of naming, and the bill for it

A knight requires a lady, so he picks a farm girl from the neighbouring village of El Toboso, Aldonza Lorenzo, and renames her Dulcinea del Toboso — "the sweet one." She never once appears in the book. Quixote concedes he has seen her at a distance a handful of times and never spoken to her; Sancho lets slip that she can pitch an iron bar clean across the barnyard and has a voice that carries over half the village.

This is not comic decoration. It is the book's hardest cut at love: what he loves is not a woman but an object he has unilaterally named, and which therefore can never disappoint him. He says as much, in substance: she is whatever I imagine her to be — the beauty, the chastity, the nobility are all attributes he has granted. Reshaping a person into the form you need is the recipe for every idealised romance, and the reason each one fails: the better you protect the image, the less it can survive one real appearance of the actual person.

Cervantes collects on this brilliantly in Part II. In chapter 10 Sancho is sent to El Toboso to fetch Dulcinea — but he has never met her, because she is not the person he has been describing. So he points at three peasant girls jogging by on donkeys and announces that one of them is she. Quixote looks, and sees three coarse peasant girls, and catches a smell of raw garlic. It is the most startling reversal in the book: this time he sees the truth, and the truth drives him mad. To keep the belief, he must conclude that enchanters have transformed her.

From that moment the controls change hands. He used to translate the world into romance; now Sancho manufactures romance and feeds it to him. The dreamer has been demoted to a man being dreamed at. And the remaining six hundred pages are spent trying to disenchant Dulcinea — under a fraudulent condition, invented by others, that Sancho must give himself 3,300 lashes. The great project of his last years rests on a lie a servant told to get out of an errand.

Sancho Panza: the book's real invention is two people

Sancho is an illiterate farmer who comes along because he has been promised an island to govern. He arrives carrying proverbs, an appetite and common sense: the windmills are windmills, the wineskins are wineskins, the beatings actually hurt. Cervantes's true invention is not Don Quixote; it is putting somebody next to him. In earlier narrative the hero faces enemies or is trailed by a servant. These two are a pair of mutually audible worldviews, and not one fight in the novel is as good as their bickering.

Better still, they infect each other. The Spanish writer Salvador de Madariaga named the process: the "quixotisation of Sancho" and the "sanchification of Quixote." Sancho grows more credulous, more imaginative, starts talking about honour; Quixote grows more practical, starts counting money, starts doubting. By the end it is Sancho begging his master not to die, pulling at him to get up and go be shepherds instead — while the master says quietly, "in last year's nests there are no birds this year." After a thousand pages, idealism and common sense have traded places.

From this the Russian critic Mikhail Bakhtin derived his account of what the novel as a form is: dialogism. Epic has one voice — the narrator's voice is the truth. A novel sets several social languages against each other inside one work — the knight's diction, the peasant's proverbs, the cleric's admonitions, the merchant's arithmetic — and the author hands down no final judgement for any of them. In other words: what makes a novel a novel is not that it tells a story but that it declines to adjudicate. And the prototype is a mad squire and a fat farmer riding side by side, neither convincing the other, neither shutting up.

Cide Hamete Benengeli: a book pretending it was written by someone else

Halfway through Part I (ch. 9), the story simply stops: the narrator says his source has run out. Then he claims to have stumbled on a sheaf of Arabic papers in the market at Toledo, hired a Morisco to translate them, and discovered that they are the History of Don Quixote by the Moorish historian Cide Hamete Benengeli. So the book becomes: Cervantes relaying a translator relaying an Arab chronicler writing about a man who read fiction as history.

The layering is not a stunt. The narrator adds, in passing, that since the historian is an Arab and lying comes naturally to that nation, he may well have shortchanged Quixote's exploits. Watch what that sentence does: it stamps the sole available source "unreliable." There is nowhere in the book you can stand and say this part is true. That is metafiction: a novel that writes into its own content the fact that you are reading a book, and that books lie.

Part II takes it to the limit: the characters in Part II have read Part I. The neighbour Sansón Carrasco informs Quixote that a book about him has sold twelve thousand copies and is being read all over Spain — so master and squire travel as celebrities, recognised at every stop. Quixote begins to fret about his public image; Sancho complains that the book made him look like a fool. A fictional character worrying about his press was, in 1615, an astonishing move.

Then reality supplied a harder one. In 1614 someone using the name "Avellaneda" rushed out a spurious sequel. Cervantes's counterattack was to write it into his own book: in Part II, ch. 59, Quixote overhears men at an inn reading the fake, leafs through it, denounces it, and changes his itinerary on the spot — the false book says he goes to Zaragoza, so he will go to Barcelona instead, in order to prove it a fraud. Later he runs into a character out of the counterfeit and makes him swear before a magistrate that he has never met the real Don Quixote. A fictional character deposing to his own authenticity. The wall between fiction and reality has never since been fully rebuilt.

The Duke, the Duchess and Barataria: when the whole world plays along

The centre of gravity in Part II is a Duke and Duchess who have read Part I. They install the pair in their castle and then deploy dozens of servants, sets and props to build bespoke adventures: counterfeit distressed damsels, counterfeit prophecies of enchantment, and a wooden horse called Clavileño — blindfold the riders, work the bellows for wind, light the firecrackers in its tail, and the two of them believe they have flown through the sky. This is where the comedy turns cold. He has finally found a world that obeys the rules of chivalric romance, and it is a set, built at cost, so that people can watch him fall over in it.

Cervantes puts the point in his chronicler's mouth, in substance: he holds the mockers every bit as mad as the mocked, and this Duke and Duchess came within a hair's breadth of looking like fools themselves for taking such trouble to make fools of two. The joke has been swivelled around. Any reader who has spent three hundred pages feeling saner than the hero is now sitting in the ducal box.

And the most moving passage in the novel happens inside that prank. The Duke palms off a small town on Sancho as his promised "island" — Barataria, a name that plays on barato, cheap — expecting the illiterate farmer to humiliate himself. Instead Sancho governs well. His judgements are fast and shrewd: a woman accuses a man of assault, Sancho orders the man to hand her a purse of money, then orders him to go take it back — she clamps onto it and cannot be budged, and Sancho throws the case out, telling her that if she had defended her body with half the ferocity she just defended that purse, there would have been nothing to try. He bans gambling, sets fair prices, patrols the streets at night — and is systematically starved by a court physician who removes each dish as bad for his health. After ten days he resigns of his own accord: "naked I was born, naked I stand; I neither lose nor gain."

That episode overturns every laugh that came before it. A man everyone had written off as a clod, installed specifically to embarrass himself, delivers a record more decent than anything in the castle — on nothing but common sense and a refusal to take — and then walks away from it. Cervantes's meaning is unmissable: rank and wit are two different things, and the capacity to govern rests not on blood but on plain fairness plus knowing when to step down.

The ending: sanity is death

The third sally ends on the beach at Barcelona, where the "Knight of the White Moon" — Carrasco in disguise — defeats him on the condition that he go home and stay there a year. He falls ill on the road, sleeps six hours, and wakes with the sentence that collapses the book: "I am no longer Don Quixote de la Mancha, but Alonso Quixano, whom the world called the Good." He denounces the chivalric romances as rubbish, makes a will leaving everything to his niece on the condition that she never marry a man who has read one — and dies three days later.

What stings is the staging. While he was mad he was alive; the instant he became sane he died. And the man weeping at the bedside, begging him not to die, urging him to get up so they can go and be shepherds, is Sancho: "the maddest thing a man can do in this life is to let himself die for no reason at all." The last holder of the ideal is the farmer who originally just wanted an island.

Cervantes does not pick a side for you. Read it as disenchantment: the world won, the dream broke, a corpse remains. Read it as mercy: he is finally allowed to die as himself, under his own name, with "the Good" attached to it. But either way, "I am no longer Don Quixote" fixes the book's real subject — not whether idealists are lovable, but how far a person can be carried by what he believes, and how much of him is left standing when the belief is withdrawn.

The Distilled Spine

A thousand pages, one bone: a man applies an obsolete rulebook to the world → the world answers with pain → the world switches to playing along → he breaks → he becomes sane → he dies. The jokes are in the first half; the bill comes due in the second.

  • First sally (opening of Part I): he rides out alone, has an innkeeper "dub" him a knight, rescues a boy named Andrés from a whipping, is beaten senseless and carted home. The priest and the barber burn most of his library and tell him an enchanter made off with the whole room.
  • Second sally (the bulk of Part I): now with Sancho. Windmills, sheep, Mambrino's helmet, the freed galley slaves. Several free-standing love stories are stitched in along the way (the novella The Ill-Advised Curiosity among them); readers complained at the time, and in Part II Cervantes has his own characters concede the criticism. He ends up caged and tricked into being hauled back to the village.
  • Third sally (Part II): Part I is now published and everyone has read it. The El Toboso "enchantment" hoax → the undecidable vision in the Cave of Montesinos → the full ducal apparatus of pranks → Sancho's island governorship → defeat at Barcelona → home, sanity, death.

What does it actually prove, and how? By running the same man through two different worlds. Part I throws him against real walls; Part II drops him into padding built to his measurements. He loses both times, and the second loss is total — because what a delusion cannot survive is not exposure but indulgence. The moment other people start cooperating, you lose your last calibration point.

Common Misreadings & Honest Criticism

  • Misreading 1: that the book is about tilting at windmills. That scene is under a third of one percent of the text and comes very early. Remembering Don Quixote as a man fighting a windmill compresses a long novel about reading, fame, power and growing old into a reaction image.
  • Misreading 2: that he is a pure idealist. The damage he does is real. Andrés, the boy he "rescues," is tied up and flogged worse the moment he rides off, and reappears later to curse him and beg him never to help again — even, he says, if he sees him being murdered. The chain gang he frees does not go thank Dulcinea; they stone him and strip him. Cervantes insists that the consequences of good intentions be charged to the good intentions.
  • Misreading 3: reading the ending as an inspirational fable about dreams losing to reality. The text is colder than that. Sanity is not celebrated — it kills him on arrival. Madness is not celebrated either — it leaves injured people all down the road. The book refuses to give either one the prize.
  • The great quarrel: the Romantic reading. Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century readers took this as a funny book. Quixote as a sublime tragic hero is largely a nineteenth-century German Romantic invention (Schelling, the Schlegels, Heine), later pushed further by Unamuno, whose Life of Don Quixote and Sancho (1905) treats "quixotism" as a faith in which being laughed at counts as martyrdom. Anthony Close's The Romantic Approach to 'Don Quixote' (1978) is the systematic rebuttal: these readings project nineteenth-century preoccupations onto a seventeenth-century comic novel. Most scholars now sit in the middle — it is a book meant to make you laugh, but the target of the laughter shifts in Part II, and Cervantes engineered the shift himself.
  • Nabokov's indictment. Teaching the book at Harvard (his lectures were published later), he tallied its beatings and humiliations, called it crude and cruel, and rejected its reputation as a warm humanist classic — while conceding that the character has outgrown the book and become, in substance, a gaunt giant we no longer laugh at. It is the reaction worth reading: you can love this novel without having to defend its violence.
  • "The first modern novel" is a contestable title. Murasaki's Tale of Genji precedes it by six centuries; Spain's own Lazarillo de Tormes (1554) had already put a poor boy's survival in the first person; Rome had the Satyricon. The defensible claim is narrower: it is the first long fiction to make "how rival discourses undercut each other" its structural principle — that, and not "writing ordinary people," is what the modern novel inherited.
  • Its acknowledged structural weakness. Part I is padded with interpolated tales unrelated to the main line, and the rhythm suffers; readers said so at the time. Part II is tighter and deeper but also slower and heavier on spectators. Plenty of readers — including plenty of scholars — think that if you only read half, read Part II.
  • What translation costs you. In Spanish this book is extremely funny: parodied registers, rustic accents, puns, Sancho mangling proverbs by the fistful. A coinage like baciyelmo survives in translation only at a discount. Any Don Quixote that reads as solemn rather than hilarious has already lost half of itself. A related distortion: the musical Man of La Mancha and its song "The Impossible Dream" are twentieth-century American products almost opposite in tone to Cervantes — and they shape most people's first impression of the book.

Ten Sentences

1. This is not a comic anecdote about fighting a windmill; it is the first book to ask what happens to someone who insists on living by an obsolete script — and the answer is that the world hits him first and humours him second.

2. The hero is above all a reader: his disease is not believing something false but failing to convert units — running one genre's rules inside another genre's world.

3. "An enchanter turned the giants into windmills." To judge whether a belief is healthy, don't ask how much it explains; ask what it would accept as a refutation. Any worldview that translates every counter-example into "someone is out to get me" is Frestón in modern dress.

4. Sancho's coinage baciyelmo is the hinge of the book: the same object has different true names inside different systems of use, and many arguments need not a verdict but a new word roomy enough for two truths.

5. Dulcinea never appears — he loves an object he named himself, which therefore can never disappoint him; reshaping a person into the form you need is precisely why idealised love collapses.

6. Part II's great reversal: he finally sees the truth (three peasant girls on donkeys) and the truth drives him mad — after which he is demoted from a man who dreams to a man being dreamed at.

7. The book's real invention is two people: a mad squire and a fat farmer riding abreast, neither convincing the other, neither shutting up — what makes a novel a novel is not that it tells a story but that it declines to adjudicate.

8. In Part II everyone has read Part I; characters start worrying about their reputations and end up swearing depositions to their own authenticity — the wall between fiction and reality has been down since 1615.

9. The Duke and Duchess hire a cast and build him sets: what a delusion cannot survive is not exposure but indulgence — and the chronicler notes that the mockers are as mad as the mocked, which is the seat the reader has been sitting in.

10. "I am no longer Don Quixote, but Alonso Quixano the Good" — while he was mad he lived; the moment he was sane he died — and the man weeping and begging him to get up and go be shepherds is the farmer who originally just wanted an island.
