Behave

Behave: The Biology of Humans at Our Best and Worst · Robert M. Sapolsky · 2017

中文 →

In One Sentence

No behavior — pulling a trigger, or reaching down to help a stranger up — has a single cause: it is the stacked product of neurons firing a second earlier, hormones from hours before, a childhood years before, a culture centuries in the making and evolution millions of years deep, which means that "nature or nurture?" and "biology or society?" are the wrong questions to begin with.

Where It Sits

Sapolsky is a Stanford neuroendocrinologist and primatologist who spent more than three decades following wild baboons in Kenya, and he is known both for his research on the biology of stress and for being a spectacular lecturer. This 700-plus-page book is his grand reckoning: neuroscience, endocrinology, genetics, developmental psychology, anthropology and evolutionary biology pressed into a single framework.

The Core Argument

  • To explain a behavior, you have to work backwards through time, one scale at a time. The first half of the book is a telescope turned around: one second before the act, seconds to minutes before, hours to days, days to months, adolescence, childhood, the womb, genes, centuries of culture, millions of years of evolution. Every layer is explaining the same act.
  • These layers are not rival explanations; they are different cross-sections of one event. Genes are not destiny, hormones are not switches, and no brain region is a "violence center." Almost every biological effect turns out to be "it depends" — on the environment, on the culture, on what has happened to you before. The most honest sentence biology can usually offer, Sapolsky says, is: "It's complicated."
  • If behavior is the sum of countless prior causes, none of which you chose, then free will, blame and punishment all need rethinking. Already in this book he leans toward the view that the better we understand the biology of an act, the harder it becomes to righteously blame the person — just as we no longer treat an epileptic seizure as demonic possession.

The Key Concepts, One by One

1. The reversed telescope: one act, ten timescales

Sapolsky opens with a scene: someone does something — maybe the worst thing (pulls a trigger), maybe the best (runs into a burning building). Then he asks: why did that happen? He refuses to let any one discipline own the answer. Instead he pulls the camera back, frame by frame:

The architecture of the book's first half: each row pulls the camera back by roughly an order of magnitude.

The point of the table is not to memorize every row but to absorb a shift in outlook: whenever someone says "violence is genetic" or "violence is all society's fault," you immediately see that they are looking at a single row. Sapolsky offers an analogy (paraphrased): asking whether behavior is caused by genes or environment is like asking whether the area of a rectangle is caused by its length or its width. The two multiply; take either away and there is nothing left to ask about.

How it changes the way you see things: the next time a headline announces that scientists have found the brain region, gene or hormone "for" some behavior, you will reflexively ask three questions — in what environment? in whom? interacting with which layers above and below?

2. The frontal cortex: doing the harder thing when it's the right thing

The prefrontal cortex (PFC) sits at the very front of the brain and is proportionally larger in humans than in any other species. Sapolsky's definition of its job recurs throughout the book: the frontal cortex makes you do the harder thing when it's the right thing (paraphrased). Delaying gratification, suppressing an impulse, following a rule, weighing the long run against the moment — that is its work.

The classic case is Phineas Gage, a nineteenth-century railroad foreman whose frontal lobes were pierced by an iron tamping rod. He survived with his intellect seemingly intact, but according to his doctor's records the steady, dependable man became crude, impulsive and unable to carry out plans; his friends said he was "no longer Gage." People with frontal damage often know the rules perfectly well and still cannot act on them.

Two features of the frontal cortex matter most:

  • It is the last part of the brain to mature. Myelination — the insulating sheath that speeds up nerve fibers — is not complete in the frontal cortex until around age 25. Teenagers are not stupid; their limbic systems, which drive reward-seeking and risk, are running at full throttle while the brakes have not yet been installed. From this Sapolsky draws a striking conclusion: because it matures last, the frontal cortex is the part of the brain least constrained by genes and most sculpted by experience. Your childhood, your culture, your history are written mainly here.
  • It is a limited resource. Under cognitive load the frontal cortex fatigues. The book describes the kind of experiment that shows it: subjects asked to hold a seven-digit number in mind, then offered a choice between chocolate cake and fruit salad, picked the cake more often (Shiv and Fedorikhin, 1999). When the mind is full, self-control yields.

It is constantly tugging against another player in the "one second before" layer: dopamine. Sapolsky stresses that dopamine mainly encodes not pleasure but the anticipation of reward. In monkey experiments, sustained dopamine release peaked when the chance of reward was 50 percent — uncertainty itself is the most compelling thing of all (Fiorillo, Tobler and Schultz, 2003). That is why gambling, endless scrolling and "maybe this time" are so hard to stop.

How it changes the way you see things: self-control is not a stock of virtue; it is the function of an organ that tires, that is weakened by hunger and stress, and that has a different capacity at different ages. That buys a little more understanding for teenagers, for exhausted people and for your own 2 a.m. impulses — and a reminder not to make important decisions when your brain has been wrung dry.

3. Hormones are amplifiers, not switches

Popular culture has two hormone myths: testosterone is the "aggression hormone" and oxytocin is the "love hormone." Sapolsky spends many pages dismantling both, and his central conclusion is this: hormones rarely conjure a behavior out of nothing; they amplify whatever behavior the situation already calls for.

Testosterone. It does not directly make people aggressive. It makes them care more about status and more willing to do whatever maintaining status requires. If status in a group is won by fighting, it amplifies fighting; if it is won by generosity, it amplifies generosity. The book cites an elegant experiment (Eisenegger and colleagues, Nature, 2010). Women were given testosterone or a placebo and then played the ultimatum game, in which one player proposes how to split a sum of money and the other can accept or reject; if she rejects, neither gets anything. Those who had actually received testosterone made fairer offers — in this game, having an offer rejected is a loss of face, and fairness is how you keep your standing. Better still: those who merely believed they had received testosterone, whatever they had actually taken, made less fair offers. It was the social myth that testosterone makes you aggressive, not the hormone, that made them selfish.

Oxytocin. It does boost trust, intimacy and cooperation — but toward your own people. In studies from 2010 and 2011, Carsten De Dreu and colleagues found that Dutch men given oxytocin, when faced with trolley-problem-style moral dilemmas, became less willing to sacrifice someone with a Dutch-sounding name and more willing to sacrifice someone with an Arab- or German-sounding name. Oxytocin makes you warmer toward the in-group and potentially colder toward the out-group. It is a tribal hormone, not a hormone of universal love. (A caveat: intranasal oxytocin research has since come under serious methodological fire for small samples and poor replicability; see the criticisms below.)

Glucocorticoids — the stress hormones, of which cortisol is the best known. This is Sapolsky's home turf. Short-term stress sharpens you and mobilizes energy; chronic stress enlarges and sensitizes the amygdala (the brain's threat-and-fear hub) while weakening the frontal cortex and the hippocampus (which handles memory and context). Someone living under prolonged stress becomes quicker to fear, quicker to read ambiguity as threat, and worse at reining in impulses. This is how poverty, discrimination and violent surroundings travel all the way from social fact into neural circuitry.

How it changes the way you see things: whenever you hear "hormone X makes people do Y," ask "in what situation?" The same chemical, under different social rules, can push toward opposite behaviors.

4. Genes: there is no "violence gene," only gene × environment

Sapolsky's critique of genetic determinism is concrete. Most of a gene's "meaning," he reminds us, lies not in the stretch of DNA that codes for a protein but in the promoters and transcription factors that regulate when, where and how strongly it is switched on — and those switches are precisely what the environment flips. Epigenetics — chemical marks that experience leaves on DNA, changing how genes are expressed without changing the sequence — lets early life turn a gene's volume up or down for years.

The best-known example is the low-activity variant of the MAOA gene (which codes for an enzyme that breaks down neurotransmitters), dubbed the "warrior gene" by the press. In a long-running New Zealand cohort study, Avshalom Caspi and colleagues (2002) found that carrying the variant on its own did not predict antisocial behavior; risk rose sharply only when it was combined with childhood maltreatment. In well-treated children, the same gene made almost no difference. Later replications and meta-analyses have been mixed, but it became the textbook case of gene-by-environment thinking.

Sapolsky's summary, roughly: genes are not about inevitability; they are about potentials and vulnerabilities. They do not decide what you will become; they decide how sensitive you will be to different environments.

How it changes the way you see things: high heritability does not mean "unchangeable." Heritability measures how much of the variation between individuals, within a particular range of environments, tracks genetic differences. Change the environment and the number changes.

5. Culture and ecology: centuries of selection written into this moment's reaction

Pulling back to the scale of centuries, Sapolsky asks how the way a group has made its living for generations produces measurable psychological, even physiological, differences.

  • Collectivism and individualism. People raised in East Asian cultures tend to attend to context and relationships; Westerners tend to attend to individuals and their attributes. The difference shows up even in attention tasks and brain imaging. Thomas Talhelm and colleagues (2014) proposed the "rice theory": within China, people from rice-growing regions — where irrigation and labor-swapping demand large-scale cooperation — think more holistically and collectively than people from wheat-growing regions.
  • The culture of honor. In herding societies, wealth walks around on four legs and can be stolen, while the law is far away, so a man must protect himself with a reputation for retaliating against any slight. The psychologists Richard Nisbett and Dov Cohen traced the American South's tradition of violence to early settlers from the herding regions of the Scottish-Irish borderlands. In a 1996 experiment, a confederate bumped into subjects in a hallway and muttered an insult. Men from the South showed sharp rises in cortisol and testosterone and were primed for aggression; Northerners mostly shrugged it off. A livelihood from centuries ago had become hormones in the bloodstream.

How it changes the way you see things: much of what you take to be "personality" is an ecological adaptation bequeathed by history — sensible in its original setting, potentially a burden in a new one.

6. Us versus Them: the brain sorts automatically, but the sorting rules can be rewritten

The second half of the book turns to specific topics, and the most important chapter is "Us versus Them." Sapolsky's two conclusions look contradictory but are really one.

First, sorting into groups is nearly automatic and extremely fast. Imaging studies find that the amygdala can respond to faces of another race within the blink of an eye. The social psychologist Henri Tajfel's "minimal group paradigm" shows how arbitrary the sorting can be: divide strangers into two groups by whether they supposedly prefer the paintings of Klee or Kandinsky (actually assigned at random), and they immediately favor their own group, even when it gains them nothing.

Second, what we sort by can be rewritten. This is what Sapolsky most wants us to grasp. In one experiment (Wheeler and Fiske, 2005), when subjects were asked to judge whether an unfamiliar face looked like someone who would enjoy a particular vegetable — that is, to imagine the person as an individual — the amygdala response to other-race faces disappeared. A 2001 study by Robert Kurzban and colleagues was even more direct: when the people subjects watched wore the jerseys of two different basketball teams, the brain's encoding of race dropped markedly, and team colors replaced skin color as the category that mattered.

Humans seem built to need an "Us" and a "Them," but who counts as "Them" is not hard-coded. That is why the same person can treat his neighbors as enemies in wartime and hug strangers in a football stadium.

How it changes the way you see things: the way to reduce hostility is not to pretend that everyone is the same but to give people a larger "Us" to share and to let them see each other as individuals — contact, cooperation, common goals.

7. Free will and blame: how far are we from treating epilepsy as witchcraft?

In the final chapters Sapolsky reaches the book's most contested ground. His reasoning runs like this: if an act is the product of every preceding layer — neurons, hormones, childhood, genes, culture, evolution — and you chose none of them, then where is the "you" that stands apart from biology and chooses freely? He calls this imagined self the homunculus: a little driver sitting in the brain, exempt from cause and effect.

His most powerful argument is historical. A few centuries ago Europeans tried, and sometimes burned, people having epileptic seizures as witches or as the possessed. Today we know a seizure is abnormal neuronal firing, and nobody blames the patient. He also cites Charles Whitman, who shot people from the University of Texas tower in 1966: an autopsy found a tumor pressing near his amygdala, and Whitman himself had asked in his note that his brain be examined, because he too felt something was wrong with him. Sapolsky's point is that much of what we now call "sin" may one day be understood, as epilepsy was, as illness or misfortune.

He also cites a much-discussed study of Israeli parole judges: across each session between meal breaks, the share of favorable rulings fell from about 65 percent to nearly zero, then jumped back after the judges had eaten (Danziger and colleagues, 2011). If whether someone goes free can track a judge's blood sugar, how much is left of the rational, fully autonomous decision?

He does not argue for doing away with restraint. Dangerous people still have to be kept from harming others, just as a car with failed brakes has to be towed off the road — not because the car is guilty, but because it is dangerous. What he wants to abolish is retribution: the moral satisfaction of believing the wrongdoer deserves to suffer.

How it changes the way you see things: when you want to say "he's just a bad person," the book makes you ask one more question — what second, what year, what childhood, what culture pushed him to that moment? That is not an excuse for atrocity; it is trading anger for understanding and prevention.

The Spine of the Book

The whole argument compresses into a single line:

(1) Behavior is not caused by any one factor but by factors spanning ten timescales — and the first half spends ten chapters showing that every layer genuinely matters. (2) Every layer's effects are conditional: hormones amplify situations, genes set sensitivities, brain regions are shaped by experience — so "nature versus nurture" is a false question. (3) Our best and worst behaviors run on the same machinery: the circuits that bind us to our own people also make us shun "them"; the brain region that produces moral disgust will also cast outsiders as contaminants. (4) Yet all of this machinery is highly malleable: sorting rules can be rewritten, chronic stress can be eased, and a baboon troop can turn gentle within a generation. (5) So our stance toward behavior should shift from judgment and retribution to understanding and changing conditions.

The evidence comes mainly from three sources: laboratory neuroscience and endocrinology (human and animal), social psychology experiments, and cross-cultural and historical comparison — plus the author's decades of watching baboons in the field.

The book's deepest value lies in no single finding but in forcing you to hold every layer in mind at once — after which any claim that reduces behavior to one cause will look too simple.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

  • Misreading one: "biological explanation means fatalism." Quite the opposite. Sapolsky insists that the heart of biology is plasticity: brains change, gene expression changes, cultures change. The most hopeful parts of the book come straight out of the biology.
  • Misreading two: "they found the brain region / gene / hormone for violence." The book's main thesis is that no such single cause exists. Reading it as "the amygdala makes people violent" or "testosterone makes people aggressive" mistakes the very thing the author is arguing against for his conclusion.
  • Criticism one: a fair number of the studies it cites have not survived the replication crisis. When the book came out in 2017, the replication crisis in social psychology was in full swing, and some of the studies it cites have since proved hard to reproduce or much weaker than claimed. The "Lady Macbeth effect" (Zhong and Liljenquist, 2006, reporting that feeling morally unclean makes people want to wash their hands) has failed repeated replication attempts; intranasal oxytocin studies are widely criticized for small samples and shaky methods; the Israeli judges study has been challenged on confounds — for example, cases of prisoners without lawyers tended to be scheduled just before breaks, so scheduling rather than hunger may explain the pattern. Sapolsky flags reservations about some of this work, but overall any single social-psychology experiment in the book should be treated as an intriguing lead, not a settled fact.
  • Criticism two: "behavior has causes" does not entail "no one is responsible." This is philosophers' most common rejoinder. Compatibilists — those who hold that determinism and free will can coexist, such as Daniel Dennett — argue that responsibility does not require an uncaused homunculus. As long as an action flows from a person's own reasons and values and is responsive to reasons, holding them responsible makes sense; and holding people responsible is itself one of the "environments" that shape future behavior. Sapolsky goes further in his later book Determined, denying free will outright, but the debate is far from over.
  • Criticism three: breadth comes at the cost of depth. Seven hundred pages across a dozen-plus disciplines means some chapters read like a brisk tour of the literature, and the synthesis sometimes rests on the author's storytelling gifts rather than on a single testable model. Some readers also feel that his positions on political questions, such as criminal justice, occasionally run ahead of the evidence.

Sapolsky Beyond This Book

Stress and status: Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers (1994). A zebra chased by a lion throws its stress response wide open and, once it escapes, recovers within minutes. Our trouble is that we can trigger the same response over a mortgage, a boss's frown or a pension thirty years away — and keep it switched on for months. Acute stress saves your life; chronic stress makes you sick, contributing to hypertension, weakened immunity and impaired memory. He has also studied status and health: in baboon troops, low-ranking animals have higher stress hormones and worse health, which echoes the British Whitehall studies of civil servants, where the lower the grade, the higher the rate of heart disease. Social rank is not just a feeling; it gets under the skin. This line of work is the foundation of the hormone and months-before chapters of Behave.

A baboon culture of peace: A Primate's Memoir (2001) and related research. In the early 1980s one troop he studied, the Forest Troop, fed on contaminated meat from a tourist lodge's garbage dump, and its most aggressive males died in large numbers of tuberculosis. The survivors became unusually gentle, grooming more and showing lower stress hormones. More remarkable still, a decade later — when the original males were long gone and the new ones had transferred in from other troops — the gentle culture was still intact (Sapolsky and Share, 2004). It is his strongest counterexample to the idea that violence is unchangeable nature, and the source of Behave's hopeful ending.

Free will denied outright: Determined (2023). What is a leaning in Behave becomes the whole thesis here: he argues that there is no scientifically identifiable room left for free will, rebutting compatibilism and the idea that quantum randomness could make us free. Read it as the full-length version of Behave's final chapters.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda): "When this exists, that comes to be" — every phenomenon arises from many converging conditions and has no independent essence. Sapolsky's ten-layer regress is practically a web of dependent origination, and "no homunculus at the wheel" is close to the teaching of no-self. The difference: Buddhism points toward liberation through practice; Sapolsky points toward criminal-justice reform.
  • Philosophy · Spinoza's Ethics: In the appendix to Part I, Spinoza says people believe themselves free only because they are conscious of their desires and ignorant of the causes that determine them. That is exactly Sapolsky's doubt about free will, and Spinoza drew from it the conclusion that understanding should replace resentment — Behave's closing stance, three centuries early. The difference: Spinoza offers a consolation (understanding necessity is a kind of freedom); Sapolsky offers none.
  • Distributed systems · the "root cause" fallacy: In "How Complex Systems Fail" (1998), Richard Cook argued that pinning an accident on a single root cause is fundamentally wrong — failures arise when many small faults line up at once. Today's blameless postmortems run on the same logic as Sapolsky's view of blame. The difference: a system can be redesigned; a childhood cannot be rolled back.
  • Chinese philosophy · Xunzi on transforming nature: Xunzi held that human nature comes with selfish desires and that goodness is the product of deliberate effort — learning and ritual. Sapolsky's point that the frontal cortex matures last and is most shaped by experience gives Xunzi's "learning must never stop" a neural footnote. The difference: Xunzi passes moral judgment on nature (he calls it bad); Sapolsky refuses to label biology good or evil.

The Book in Ten Sentences

① No behavior has a single cause: it is the sum of every layer from one second to a million years before.

② Asking whether behavior comes from genes or environment is like asking whether a rectangle's area comes from its length or its width.

③ Biology's most honest answer is usually "it depends" — almost every factor's effect hinges on context.

④ The frontal cortex makes you do the harder thing when it's the right thing; because it matures last, it is the part most shaped by experience.

⑤ Dopamine encodes anticipation, not pleasure — "maybe" is more addictive than "certainly."

⑥ Hormones are amplifiers, not switches: testosterone amplifies whatever keeps your status; oxytocin warms you to your own and cools you to outsiders.

⑦ Genes are not about inevitability but about potentials and vulnerabilities — without an environment, a gene has nothing to say.

⑧ The way your ancestors made a living centuries ago can become the hormones in your blood after an insult today.

⑨ The brain is built to sort Us from Them, but who counts as Them can be rewritten — a team jersey can override skin color.

⑩ We once burned epileptics; the better we understand the biology of an act, the more we should trade retribution for understanding and prevention.
