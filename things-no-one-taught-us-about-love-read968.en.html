Things No One Taught Us About Love

Things No One Taught Us About Love: How to Build Healthy Relationships with Yourself and Others · Vex King · first published 2023 as Closer to Love, revised and retitled 2024 · folds in the same author's You Can't Please Everyone (2026)

中文 →

In One Sentence

We were all taught to go looking for love; almost nobody taught us how to actually be loving — first toward ourselves, then toward anyone else. Vex King's whole case fits in a line: the love between you and other people is a photocopy of the relationship you have with yourself. Leave the original untouched, and however many partners you run it past, the same page comes out.

Where It Sits

Vex King is a British writer and life coach who went through homelessness and poverty as a child and built his audience on Instagram with short, warm posts about self-worth; his debut, Good Vibes, Good Life (2018), went to number one on the Sunday Times bestseller list. This is the book where he takes that "be good to yourself" philosophy and points it squarely at relationships — first published in 2023 as Closer to Love, revised and retitled in 2024. His 2026 follow-up, You Can't Please Everyone, moves the same theory of self-worth onto people-pleasing and the hunger for approval, so it is covered here too.

The Core Claims

  • Your relationship with yourself is the template for every other relationship. How you treat your own needs, mistakes and limits gets copied straight into whom you choose, how you behave with them, and what you are willing to put up with. In his words: "the love you experience with others will be a direct reflection of the love you share with yourself."
  • Love is not a feeling that descends from outside to rescue you; it is an ongoing practice that starts from within. Expecting romance to fix your problems and certify your worth means handing the foundations of your life to someone else for safekeeping — and sooner or later they give way.
  • A healthy relationship isn't one where you sand yourself down to fit; it's one where you show up whole. Changing your habits, hiding your personality and saying yes when you mean no buys short-term peace and long-term erosion — of your self-worth and of your self-knowledge at once.

The Key Concepts, Unpacked

1. The photocopy principle: your relationship with yourself is the original

Start with a familiar pattern. Someone breaks up, falls for someone new, breaks up again — and every time, around month three, they hit the same wall: the same insecurity, the same fights, the same "why can't they care about me a bit more?" King's diagnosis: the partner changed; the original didn't. If somewhere deep down you believe you aren't enough, that love has to be earned by performing, you will be drawn to people who keep you performing. You'll read their coolness as your failure and treat a scrap of warmth from them as an enormous gift.

The mechanism isn't mystical. Each of us carries an internal price tag — a sense of what we deserve — and it governs three things. Whom you pick: a person who treats you well can feel "flat" or "too good to be true" if they don't match your price. What you tolerate: the lower the price, the more crossed lines start to look normal. How you read the other person: a low price makes every ambiguous signal look like rejection. Between them, those three things largely decide where a relationship goes — and all three sit on your side of it.

So when King says "love yourself first," he doesn't mean you must reach total self-acceptance before you're allowed to date — that's a common misreading, and we'll come back to it. He means that if you want to fix a relationship, the highest-leverage place to work is usually not the other person but the price you've put on yourself. Change the original and every copy changes with it.

What shifts is the first question you ask about your love life: from "why do I keep meeting the wrong people?" to "what kind of person do I keep choosing, and what do I keep putting up with?" The first leaves you waiting on luck. The second gives you something to do.

2. Love is a verb: action, vibration and a way of living

King breaks love into three layers: love as an action, love as a "vibration," and love as a way of life. All three push against the same popular idea — that love is a feeling that simply arrives and simply leaves.

Action is the easy one. Feelings fluctuate; the chemical rush of early romance ebbs within months or a year or two. What remains is whether someone is willing, day after day, to listen, apologize, keep their word, and take something off your plate when you're exhausted. On King's view, what a person keeps doing tells you far more about whether they love you than what they say or how they make you feel in the moment. The same goes for loving yourself: it isn't a warm mood, it's going to bed on time, refusing to run yourself into the ground, and treating promises to yourself as real promises.

Vibration is King's signature "good vibes" vocabulary, and it needs translating to stop sounding cosmic. Roughly, he means your inner state radiates outward and draws in people and relationships that match it. Anxious, depleted people tend to get tangled up with equally depleted people — or with people who are good at exploiting depletion. Steadier people are more visible to other steady people, and quicker to spot instability for what it is. Strip off the language of the universe and what's left is a plainer mechanism — a filter: whatever state you're in determines which people register as "the right feeling." (The metaphysical part comes in for criticism below.)

A way of life brings the first two down to earth: love isn't a skill you switch on inside a romance; it's one attitude that runs through how you treat yourself, friends, family and strangers. Someone who is curt with waiters and merciless with themselves rarely turns gentle and steady the moment a partner walks in.

The change here is in the unit you measure love by: from intensity (how hard I've fallen) to consistency (what we actually do for each other every day). The first is weather; the second is climate.

3. Self-betrayal: every yes you didn't mean marks you down

This is the book's sharpest idea, and the seed of the whole of You Can't Please Everyone. Self-betrayal is when you know perfectly well what you want and don't want, and choose the opposite anyway — to keep others comfortable, to avoid friction, to be liked. King's examples: saying "yes" when you want to say "no"; putting other people's needs before your own; compromising your values to keep others comfortable.

Any one instance is just a small sting: the party you didn't want to go to, the plan you nodded through, the joke at your expense you laughed along with. The trouble is the compounding. Think of it as credit. Every "I don't actually want this" that you personally overrule makes your inner voice behave like a friend you keep standing up — eventually it stops calling. In the end the problem isn't that you can't say no; it's that you genuinely no longer know what you want. That is exactly what King means when he says that reshaping your habits and burying your personality to get along wears away both self-worth and self-knowledge.

There is a quieter layer too. People-pleasing looks like giving, but it is often a trade. You hand over compliance and secretly expect approval and safety in return; when the other person doesn't pay up, the hurt curdles into resentment. People-pleasers usually don't love others too much — they fear losing them too much. It's fear wearing love's clothes.

What it changes is your idea of kindness: real kindness includes keeping faith with yourself. Someone who breaks their word to themselves will struggle to keep it to anyone else.

4. Boundaries: a door, not a wall

Say "boundaries" and many people hear coldness, blocking, going no-contact. That's the picture King wants to correct: a boundary isn't a wall to keep people out; it's a door, and you decide when it opens and how wide. You Can't Please Everyone puts it even more bluntly: learn to set boundaries without cutting people off.

A concrete case: your parents want you home for dinner every week, and your working week is already overdrawn. The people-pleasing move is to go every time and sit there irritable. The cut-off move is to stop answering the phone. The boundaried move: "For the next couple of months I can only make it every other week — but when I come, I'll stay the whole evening." At heart, a boundary is an honest report of what you can carry, so the relationship rests on the real you rather than on a version of you that's gritting its teeth.

King pays particular attention to two emotions that reliably show up after you set a boundary: guilt and anger. His approach comes down to this. Guilt is often just the discomfort of breaking an old habit, not evidence you did something wrong — after years of yes, the first no is bound to feel shaky. Anger is often a signal that a line which should have been drawn long ago has been crossed for a long time. Both are worth listening to; neither should be allowed to make the decision.

One detail is easy to miss: a boundary is enforced by you, not obeyed by them. "You can't talk to me like that anymore" is a demand the other person can ignore. "If you talk to me like that again, I'll end this conversation" is your own action — and you can always carry it out.

What changes is your picture of closeness. Being close doesn't mean having no edges. It's precisely clear edges that let people draw near — because both sides know that getting close won't mean being swallowed.

5. Old wounds and triggers: the fight is rarely about this

King spends a good deal of the book on how unprocessed emotion and trauma seep into relationships. His central term is triggers: a throwaway remark from a partner, an unanswered message, and you erupt or collapse, with a reaction far out of proportion to the event. That mismatch usually means the thing being pressed isn't the small thing in front of you but an older wound.

Think of an old scar: it doesn't hurt day to day, but brush it lightly and you flinch. The touch was gentle; the wound is what hurts. Your panic when a partner hasn't replied for three hours may not be about those three hours at all, but about some much earlier experience of being ignored or left. Miss that, and you'll keep demanding "stop touching that," without ever tending to the wound itself.

He ties this to attachment styles. Attachment theory began with the British psychiatrist John Bowlby and was developed experimentally by the psychologist Mary Ainsworth: the pattern of interaction between an infant and its main caregiver settles into a set of expectations — will the people closest to me respond when I need them? — and we carry those expectations into adult love. In broad strokes there are a few recognizable patterns: secure (closeness feels safe; you can lean on others and also be alone), anxious (fear of abandonment, a need for repeated reassurance), avoidant (the closer someone gets, the more you want to pull back, using independence as armor), and a mixed pattern that craves closeness and fears it at once. Recognizing your own attachment pattern isn't about labeling yourself; it's about understanding why you keep losing control in the same spot — so you have a chance to pause just before you do.

What changes is how you read a fight. Next time your emotions run away with you, ask first: "Is this reaction proportionate to what just happened?" The part that isn't proportionate is your homework, not evidence against them.

6. Self-disclosure, and why no relationship is wasted

If the earlier sections are about defense, this one is about offense: how does a relationship actually deepen? King's answer is unambiguous — through self-disclosure. "Having a high level of self-disclosure," he writes, "is one of the most important building blocks in an intimate relationship, as well as in social ones, too." Self-disclosure means letting someone see the real you — your fears, your history, what you actually want — instead of an edited version.

This closes the loop with self-betrayal. The habitual pleaser finds disclosure hardest of all, because they're always presenting the self they think the other person wants to see. So the pleaser is caught in a paradox: the harder they work at being liked, the less anyone actually knows them — and love that never reaches the real person can't fill the gap, however much of it there is. To be loved, you first have to be seen.

On endings, King has a widely quoted passage: no relationship is a total waste; however painful, it teaches you what you want and don't want, and reminds you that you deserve better; if it's painful enough, a bad relationship can be the catalyst for a journey inward — "but no relationship ... is worth damaging future opportunities to connect with others or yourself." The weight of that sentence is in its second half: let yourself hurt, but don't let one injury turn into a lifelong guard against all intimacy. He also argues that the grief after a breakup should be lived through as real grief, not numbed with the next relationship.

What changes is how you book a "failed" relationship: not as a bad debt but as expensive, useful intelligence — provided you actually read it.

7. Folded in: You Can't Please Everyone — coming back to your centre

The 2026 book pulls the camera back from romance to the whole of life: work, family, the approval economy of social media. Its key idea is your "centre": the stable point made of self-trust, authenticity and inner peace. Every move to win approval tugs you away from that centre, and the force doing the tugging is a fear buried deep: that who you truly are might not be worthy of love, acceptance and belonging.

That completes the argument of the earlier book. Why do people betray themselves? Fear. Fear of what? That the real self won't make the cut. So people-pleasing isn't a habit problem; it's a worth problem. Drilling "how to say no" treats the symptom; the cure is rebuilding trust in your own inner voice — acting on your own judgment in small matters, again and again, and discovering that the sky stays where it is. The title is the first dose of the remedy: you were never going to please everyone. Accept that, and you can stop exhausting yourself in a race you were always going to lose. The book also takes up how to chase success and happiness without trading away your peace or your sense of self — ambition, in other words, doesn't have to be paid for in self-betrayal.

Read together, King's system runs along one line: fear of being unlovable → pleasing and self-betrayal → a silenced inner voice → relationships built on a self that isn't real → feeling empty no matter how loved you are. The way out runs the other direction: keep small promises to yourself → set boundaries and let people be unhappy → recognize old wounds → dare to disclose → be seen, and loved, as you are.

The Skeleton of the Book

The book establishes one thing: the quality of your intimate relationships is capped by the quality of your relationship with yourself. The chain runs like this. Everyone carries an internal price on themselves (am I worth loving?). That price decides whom you choose, what you tolerate and how you read the other person. People with a low price try to buy love by pleasing, and pleasing is, at bottom, self-betrayal. Self-betrayal drives the real self further out of sight, so that even when you're loved, what's being loved is a mask — which is why the emptiness never lifts. The only way to break the loop is from your own side: hold your boundaries, tend your old wounds, dare to be seen.

What does he prove it with? Not experiments, mostly, but three things: personal stories (his own path, readers' letters, cases from coaching); accessible retellings of established psychological ideas (attachment, self-disclosure, boundaries, grief); and a steady supply of small exercises you can do today (reflective prompts, tiny habit-building steps). Its weight lies not in discovering anything new but in taking ideas that come up over and over in therapists' offices and putting them, in warm and almost barrier-free language, in front of people who have never been in one.

The key to reading it is its direction of travel: most relationship books teach you how to get someone to love you; this one keeps turning your gaze back — first look at the quality of the love you're carrying in.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "If you don't love yourself first, you don't deserve love." This is the cruelest reading of "love yourself first" — it turns a direction into an entry requirement and hands people already riddled with self-doubt one more reason to think they don't qualify. The better reading: self-love and being loved grow together and feed each other. You don't have to finish some inner course before you're allowed into a relationship; you keep holding onto yourself inside it.

Misreading 2: "Boundaries mean cutting off anyone who makes me uncomfortable." Online slogans about "protecting your peace" make it easy to shrink boundaries down to going no-contact. King argues the opposite: boundaries exist so relationships can continue, not to supply an excuse for leaving. Someone who meets every bit of friction with the block button doesn't have clear boundaries; they have avoidance.

Misreading 3: Treating attachment styles like star signs. "I'm anxious, so…", "He's avoidant, so he's hopeless" — the most common abuse in pop psychology. Attachment research itself stresses that adult attachment is better described as positions on two continuous dimensions (how anxious, how avoidant) than as four boxes, and that it shifts with relationship experience. Labels are fine for understanding yourself; they are not a verdict on someone else.

Criticism 1: The metaphysics of "vibration." King writes out of the "good vibes" strand of spiritual self-help, and claims like "your frequency attracts your relationships" have no scientific basis. Strip them away and the filtering mechanism that's left (your state shapes whom you choose and how you read them) is reasonable; taken literally, though, it slides easily into victim-blaming — "you got hurt because your vibration was off."

Criticism 2: Self-esteem is not a master key. Psychology has long since second-guessed the idea that raising self-esteem improves everything. A major 2003 review led by Roy Baumeister found that self-esteem's links to relationships and achievement are mostly weak, and that good outcomes often produce high self-esteem rather than the reverse. Another line of research treats self-esteem as largely a readout of how accepted we are by others. Either way, the one-way causation of "love yourself, then love others" is too tidy: in real life, having been loved well is often where learning to love yourself begins.

Criticism 3: Limited depth, plenty of repetition. Readers broadly find it warm, easy to read and a good entry point, but anyone who has read about attachment or spent time in therapy will find little that's new, and a lot of the page count is aphorism and restatement. It works as a first book on the subject, not the last.

Criticism 4: Trauma isn't a self-help project. Encouraging readers to reflect on old wounds is useful in itself. But real trauma, abusive relationships or severe attachment difficulties need more than self-guided exercises. Reading "heal your wounds" as "just think it through on your own" can keep some people white-knuckling it when they should be getting help.

Crossovers

  • Western philosophy · Aristotle on self-love (philautia): Book IX of the Nicomachean Ethics argues that our attitudes toward a friend are extended from our attitudes toward ourselves — a friend is "another self." He also splits self-love in two: the base kind that grabs wealth and honors, and the good kind that pursues one's own virtue. That's the photocopy principle in classical dress. The difference: Aristotle's good self-love means becoming good, not feeling good.
  • Buddhism · Loving-kindness starts with yourself: In the Visuddhimagga, Buddhaghosa teaches mettā practice beginning with oneself ("may I be happy, free from enmity"), then widening to loved ones, neutral people and enemies. Same order as King's "love yourself first" — but the Buddhist endpoint is loosening attachment to a self, while King wants to build a sturdier one.
  • Biology · Membranes and autopoiesis: Maturana and Varela defined life as a system that produces and maintains its own boundary. A cell membrane isn't a wall but a selectively permeable door: seal it completely and the cell starves; open it completely and the cell dissolves. "A boundary is a door, not a wall" is literal here — without a boundary, there is no self to connect with anyone. The difference: a membrane's rules are written in molecules, while a person has to say theirs out loud, again and again.
  • Distributed systems · Backpressure: A service that accepts every request gets overwhelmed and spreads the failure up its call chain; healthy systems let downstream components tell upstream ones to slow down, or reject some load. Saying no isn't a fault — it's how the whole system stays alive. The difference: machines don't feel guilty about refusing.

The Book in Ten Sentences

1. We were taught to go looking for love; nobody taught us how to be someone who loves — first ourselves, then others.

2. The love between you and other people is a photocopy of your relationship with yourself; leave the original alone and every partner prints the same page.

3. The price you put on yourself decides whom you choose, what you tolerate, and how you read every signal the other person sends.

4. Love is a verb: to know whether someone loves you, watch what they keep doing, not what they made you feel for a moment.

5. Every time you want to say no and say yes, you betray yourself a little; do it enough and your inner voice behaves like a friend you keep standing up — it stops calling.

6. People-pleasers usually don't love others too much; they fear losing them too much — fear wearing love's clothes.

7. A boundary is a door, not a wall, and it's enforced by you, not obeyed by them; the guilt that follows is mostly just an old habit breaking.

8. When the reaction dwarfs the event, what's being pressed is usually an old wound, not the small thing in front of you.

9. To be loved, you first have to be seen: the harder you work at being liked, the less anyone actually knows you.

10. You were never going to please everyone; accept it and you can return to your centre and love people as your whole self — no relationship is wasted, however painful, but don't let one wound close every door to closeness.
