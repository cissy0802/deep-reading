The Great Transformation

The Great Transformation · Karl Polanyi · 1944

中文 →

In One Sentence

For nearly all of human history the economy was part of society, embedded in kinship, religion and politics. Nineteenth-century Britain was the first to try the reverse: make all of society answer to a self-regulating market, with people (labor), nature (land) and purchasing power (money) bought and sold like any other good. That experiment was a utopia, society was bound to fight back, and the tug-of-war that followed spiraled into the Depression, the collapse of the gold standard and fascism.

Where It Sits

Polanyi was born in Vienna, grew up in Budapest, worked as an economic journalist in "Red Vienna" in the 1920s, and fled to England in 1933, where he taught economic history to adult workers. He wrote the book in the United States, and it came out in 1944, the same year as Hayek's The Road to Serfdom (Read 65). The two books are the twentieth century's thesis and antithesis on markets and society, and Polanyi is still the starting point whenever people talk about "market society" or the backlash against globalization.

Core Arguments

  • The self-regulating market isn't a natural order. Someone had to build it. Adam Smith wrote of a human "propensity to truck, barter, and exchange." Polanyi uses anthropology and history to show that this propensity was never the main way economic life got organized. A national market in which everything could be bought and sold had to be pushed into existence by the English state, one statute at a time.
  • Labor, land and money are "fictitious commodities." None of them is produced for sale. Hand them over to the price mechanism and people starve, nature gets stripped, and businesses get wiped out by deflation or inflation. So wherever market society spreads, self-protection follows.
  • The "double movement": market expansion and social protection advance together and pull against each other. Nineteenth-century liberal civilization was never overthrown by its enemies. That tension pulled it apart. The gold standard locked countries into deflation, protection kept the market from adjusting, and fascism was one of the ways out of the deadlock.

Put together: the market is a good servant and a bad master. Make it society's master and society strikes back, and the striking back isn't always pretty.

The Key Concepts, Unpacked

1. Embeddedness: the economy used to have no room of its own

What it is. "Embeddedness" is Polanyi's signature idea. In most human societies economic activity isn't a separate sphere, and there's no distinct "economic motive." It sits inside other social relationships. People work, share and trade to keep their standing, honor kinship duties and earn prestige, not mainly to maximize individual gain. His showcase example is the kula ring, recorded by Bronisław Malinowski, the anthropologist who lived among the Trobriand Islanders of Melanesia. Islanders paddled canoes across open sea to pass shell necklaces and armbands around a vast circuit. The objects were useless and inedible. Exchanging them built honor and partnership.

From cases like this Polanyi drew three principles of economic organization, later adding a fourth, exchange:

  • Reciprocity rests on symmetry. Two clans or families give to each other over many years, my fish for your yams. Nobody settles the account, and trying to settle it would be an insult.
  • Redistribution rests on centricity. Goods flow into a chief, a temple or a palace and are handed back out. Pharaonic granaries and imperial China's grain-transport and price-stabilizing granary system run on this logic.
  • Householding means producing for your own household or estate, self-sufficiently. It's Aristotle's oikonomia, the management of a home.

Markets always existed, but under these three principles they played a supporting role, hemmed in by custom, guilds and royal law. The nineteenth century turned it upside down: instead of the economy being embedded in social relations, social relations were embedded in the economic system.

An analogy. Think of a family kitchen. Cooking involves "resource allocation": how much rice, how many vegetables, who eats first. But nobody makes the kids bid for the last piece of meat or sets Grandma's portion by her "output." Allocation in the kitchen is embedded in love, duty and house rules. Polanyi's market society is like someone announcing that from now on everything at home goes to the highest bidder, and if Grandma can't pay she goes hungry, because that's efficient.

Why it matters. It knocks out a default assumption. The textbook's "rational economic man" isn't the permanent bedrock of human nature. He's a pattern of behavior that a specific set of institutions produced at scale. People aren't born only able to count prices. A society that speaks only in prices trains them to.

How it changes your view. Next time someone says "that's just the market, nothing to be done," ask: under what rules, laws and customs did this become a market? Where are the market's boundaries, and who drew them? Healthcare, schooling, organs, votes, housing: whether and how far these go to market has always been a political and moral choice, not a law of nature.

2. Fictitious commodities: labor, land, money

What it is. Polanyi defines a commodity plainly: a thing produced for sale on a market. By that test, the three most important inputs of a market economy aren't commodities at all.

  • Labor is another name for human activity, which can't be separated from life itself. Nobody is born in order to be sold.
  • Land is another name for nature, which nobody made.
  • Money is a token of purchasing power. It isn't usually produced. Banking and state finance bring it into being.

Yet a self-regulating market system can only work if all three trade freely at market prices: wages, rent, interest. So society has to pretend they're commodities. That pretense is what "fictitious" means. His central verdict (paraphrased): leave the market mechanism sole director of people's fate, their natural surroundings, even the amount and use of purchasing power, and it will demolish society.

An analogy. Treating labor as a commodity is like treating a river as a pipe. You can price it and schedule it, but it floods and it dries up, and the harder you treat it as a pipe, the harder the un-pipe-like parts bite back.

Why it matters. This is the hinge of the whole argument. Because these three are fictitious commodities, marketizing them provokes resistance by its very structure, not because agitators stir it up. House prices, rents, health insurance, climate, currency crises: today's fiercest political fights cluster on exactly these three lines, and that's no accident.

How it changes your view. It gives you a diagnostic map. When a social conflict breaks out, check whether it's pressing on one of these three nerves. Fights over gig workers' protections, over whether a home is for living in or for investing, over Greece being forced into austerity inside the eurozone all share one structure: something that shouldn't be fully commodified got pushed too far.

3. The satanic mill and Speenhamland: how a labor market was made

What it is. "Satanic mill" comes from William Blake's line about "dark Satanic Mills." Polanyi uses it for the social machine that ground people down into "labor." He tells three key episodes of English history.

Enclosure. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries landlords fenced off common land for sheep, and peasants lost their footing. Polanyi's surprising move is to defend the Tudor and Stuart crown. Its repeated anti-enclosure decrees seem to have failed to stop enclosure, but they slowed the pace and gave the people being hit time to adjust. The direction of change often can't be helped, he argues, but the rate of change decides whether a society is transformed or destroyed.

Speenhamland (1795). Berkshire magistrates meeting at Speenhamland decided to top up low earnings on a scale indexed to the price of bread and the size of the family. Whatever wages failed to cover, the parish poor rate made up. It was meant to protect the poor. Polanyi argues it was a disaster: employers could cut wages because the parish would pay the difference, workers earned about the same however hard they worked, so effort and self-respect collapsed together, and the poor rates crushed small ratepayers. He calls it a "right to live," something that held back the formation of a labor market, but in a degrading way.

The New Poor Law of 1834. Speenhamland was abolished. Relief was now given only inside workhouses deliberately made worse than the worst job outside. From then on hunger was the whip that drove people to work, and only then did England have a truly competitive labor market. Polanyi's conclusion: the free labor market didn't sprout from the soil. The state forced it into being with statutes and workhouses.

Why it matters. This sets up the book's most quoted line: "Laissez-faire was planned; planning was not." Building a free market took enormous amounts of legislation, administration and coercion. The protective measures that came later (factory acts, public health laws, legal trade unions) arose piecemeal and spontaneously, in different countries and at the hands of people with very different ideologies. They weren't a collectivist conspiracy.

How it changes your view. When someone says that lifting regulation will let things "naturally" return to the market, Polanyi reminds you that the "natural" market needs land registries, contract enforcement, laws against strikes, a monetary system, courts and police. The market isn't what's left when government steps back. It's an institution that government builds and maintains. That swaps "state versus market" for a better question: what kind of market is the state building?

4. The double movement: the market's pull and society's counter-pull

What it is. Two forces drove nineteenth-century history at once. One was economic liberalism, which aimed to extend the market into everything through laissez-faire, free trade and the gold standard. The other was social protection, which aimed to shield people, nature and productive organization through protective laws, restrictive associations and other forms of intervention. Different classes carried each one: the trading classes mainly carried the first, while the working and landed classes both pushed the second. But protective measures often came from across classes and parties.

Polanyi makes an almost statistical case that the countermovement was spontaneous. Factory laws, public health, workers' compensation and municipal utilities appeared at roughly the same time in Victorian England, Bismarck's Germany, Third Republic France and Habsburg Austria, countries of wildly different political stripes. A "collectivist conspiracy" couldn't have produced the same prescriptions under such different ideologies. The likelier explanation is that they all ran into the same kind of harm.

Schematic: the double movement. Both forces grow, each stalls the other, and the system seizes up in the 1920s and 1930s. The curves are illustrative, not real data.

An analogy. Picture a rubber band stretched ever tighter. The market pulls one end, social protection the other. Protection isn't the villain, because it keeps people alive. But if the market system itself doesn't change, more protection makes the market seize up (prices stuck, costs that won't fall), a seized market makes people suffer more, and suffering brings demands for more protection. Eventually the band snaps.

Why it matters. The frame explains how the liberal civilization of the nineteenth century could keep a century of relative peace and then fall apart within decades. Socialists or protectionists didn't wreck a market that was otherwise perfect. The market was designed to demand something no society could deliver.

How it changes your view. It lets you read "populism" and "anti-globalization" as a predictable structural response, not just voters being foolish or misled. Many economists today, Dani Rodrik and his "globalization paradox" among them, use Polanyi's lens on the political earthquakes after 2016: when market integration runs too far ahead of social protection, politics drags it back. But the same frame warns that a countermovement guarantees no happy ending.

5. The gold standard and the four pillars of nineteenth-century civilization

What it is. The book opens with the scaffolding. Nineteenth-century civilization rested on four institutions: the balance-of-power system, the international gold standard, the self-regulating market and the liberal state. The gold standard was the linchpin, and the self-regulating market was the source from which the rest flowed.

Here's how gold worked. Each currency was pegged to gold, so exchange rates were fixed. A country running a trade deficit lost gold, its money supply shrank, prices and wages fell, and eventually its exports became competitive again. Textbooks call that "automatic adjustment." For society it meant deflation, unemployment and downward pressure on wages. In the nineteenth century, when workers had neither votes nor unions, that pain could be suppressed. After the First World War workers could vote and organize, so governments couldn't sit by while unemployment soared, and yet they wouldn't give up gold.

In Polanyi's telling, countries paid a huge price in the 1920s to get back onto gold (Britain returned at the prewar parity in 1925) and got chronic deflation and political fracture in return. Britain left gold in 1931, others followed, and the international order disintegrated. Out of the wreckage came three routes: Soviet socialism, the American New Deal, and German and Italian fascism. Polanyi doesn't treat fascism as a random outburst of barbarism. He treats it as one "solution" to the deadlock of market society, using the state to patch up a market economy by force at the price of destroying democracy.

Why it matters. It ties a seemingly technical monetary arrangement to war and totalitarianism. An institution that stops governments from responding to voters' pain will eventually send voters looking for someone willing to smash everything.

How it changes your view. It makes you wary of any design that puts an economic rule beyond democratic reach. Much of the debate about the eurozone (a fixed-rate zone a lot like the gold standard) and about the politics of austerity carries Polanyi's shadow. The rule may be sensible in itself, but if it asks society to absorb more adjustment than it can bear, politics will find another exit.

6. The myth of economic man, and freedom in a complex society

What it is. Polanyi thought the nineteenth century's deepest legacy wasn't the factory. It was an idea: man as "economic man," society as a market, gain-seeking as human nature. This "economistic fallacy" takes the institutions of one peculiar era for the universal human condition.

The final chapter, "Freedom in a Complex Society," answers the Hayekian worry that limiting the market means losing freedom. Polanyi's reply is that half of nineteenth-century freedom was the freedom to exploit, the freedom to make outsized gains without contributing to the community (paraphrased). The other half was freedom of conscience, speech, association and choice of job, and that half is precious. Ending market society doesn't mean ending markets, still less ending freedom. The point is to protect those precious freedoms deliberately through institutions instead of hoping the market will throw them in for free. He adds that people must accept the reality of society: power and coercion can't be abolished in a complex society, and pretending otherwise is the real threat to freedom.

Why it matters. It offers a view of freedom that is the opposite of Hayek's and just as serious. Hayek feared that planning would lead to serfdom. Polanyi feared that an unbridled market would drive people into fascism, which is serfdom too. Both were explaining the same catastrophe. They just located the disease at opposite ends.

The Spine of the Argument

Here is the whole book as one line:

  1. First, break the myth. Anthropology and economic history show that most societies organized their economies through reciprocity, redistribution and householding, with markets in a supporting role. The idea that humans are natural traders isn't a fact.
  2. Then, show how it was built. Through enclosure, the abolition of Speenhamland and the New Poor Law of 1834, England turned labor, land and money into tradable things and created the first market society. Laissez-faire was planned.
  3. Next, why the backlash was inevitable. The three are fictitious commodities. Leave them wholly to the market and people and nature are destroyed, so social protection rises up spontaneously, and the double movement begins.
  4. Then, how it broke. After 1918 the painful adjustments the gold standard demanded collided head-on with a democratized society's demands. Market and protection jammed each other, and the international system fell apart in 1929–31.
  5. Finally, the endings and the way out. Fascism, socialism and the New Deal were different answers to the same deadlock. The way out is to put the economy back under society while deliberately protecting individual freedom.

What it proves, in one line: a self-regulating market society can't sustain itself as a way of organizing human life, in logic or in history, and its failure is the root of the twentieth century's catastrophes. The evidence comes from three places: anthropological comparison (market society is the exception), English social history (it was manufactured), and synchronized legislation across countries (the countermovement was spontaneous).

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: Polanyi was against markets. No. He was against market society, a market that becomes the master principle for organizing everything. He says plainly that markets as an institution won't disappear. What matters is whether they sit under society's rules. Reading him as a champion of central planning gets him wrong.

Misreading 2: the countermovement is always good. Also no. The double movement describes a tension, and Polanyi himself counts fascism as one response to the crisis of market society. Protection can mean factory acts. It can also mean xenophobia, tariff wars and dictatorship. Using him to cheer every populism reads him backwards.

Criticism 1: the Speenhamland history. This is where the book takes the most fire. Polanyi's account of what Speenhamland did leans heavily on the 1834 Royal Commission report on the Poor Laws, a document written to justify abolishing the old system and soaked in ideology. In 1963 the economic historian Mark Blaug argued that the allowance system didn't cause the collapse of wages and the population boom the report claimed, and that rural poverty owed more to things like the postwar farm depression. Later, Fred Block and Margaret Somers pointed out that on this point Polanyi had inherited the narrative of the very side he was attacking. His overall thesis may survive, but this key piece of evidence is shakier than he made it look.

Criticism 2: is "market society is the exception" overstated? Classicists and economic historians, such as Peter Temin in his work on the Roman market economy, argue that the ancient Mediterranean, Song China and elsewhere had well-developed markets, including markets in labor and land, so "pre-modern economies barely ran on markets" goes too far. In anthropology the dispute became the substantivism vs. formalism debate. Substantivists, Polanyi's camp, held that economics' rational-choice models don't fit non-market societies. Formalists held that everyone chooses under scarcity, so the models apply everywhere. Most scholars now sit in between: markets are ancient, but their place in society really did change in kind in the nineteenth century.

Criticism 3: "embedded" and "disembedded" are fuzzy. If markets need state legislation, courts and police to function (which Polanyi himself insists), then the market was never truly disembedded. It was embedded in a different set of institutions. Fred Block draws the conclusion: an "always embedded market economy." On this view disembedding was a political program, a utopia, never a state that was actually reached. That arguably makes Polanyi more coherent, but it also shows his usage isn't fully consistent. In 1985 the sociologist Mark Granovetter recast "embeddedness" as economic action embedded in networks of personal relations. It became a core concept of economic sociology, but it's a different idea.

Criticism 4: did the prophecy fail? In 1944 Polanyi believed the era of market society was over. The postwar Bretton Woods system did re-embed markets within national welfare states and capital controls, an arrangement the political scientist John Ruggie called "embedded liberalism." But neoliberalism's revival from the late 1970s brought a fresh wave of marketization, which suggests his obituary was premature. On the other hand, the political backlash after the 2008 financial crisis is widely read as the double movement playing out again. As a prophecy it missed. As an explanatory frame it gets used more every year.

Criticism 5: the whiff of functionalism. "Society protects itself" makes society sound as if it had a will. Critics note that Polanyi says little about who organizes the countermovement or through what political mechanisms. It reads as if "society" as a whole simply responded. Real protections come out of struggles among specific classes, parties and interest groups, and they often include incumbents rent-seeking under the banner of "protecting society."

Polanyi Beyond This Book

After The Great Transformation, Polanyi went to Columbia University to study ancient economies. He co-edited Trade and Market in the Early Empires (1957) and left behind the posthumous Dahomey and the Slave Trade (1966). There he turned the book's scattered anthropology into a sharper distinction. "Economic" has two meanings. The formal meaning is the optimal choice of means to ends under scarcity, which is the textbook definition. The substantive meaning is how people get their living from nature and from one another, which is an "instituted process." The first broadly fits only market societies. The second is universal. He also coined the "port of trade": in the ancient world, long-distance trade often gathered at neutral harbors controlled by political authorities, where prices were set by treaty and custom rather than set by supply and demand. Whydah, the port of the West African kingdom of Dahomey, was one. This supplies the methodological foundation of The Great Transformation: look at every society through the lens of market society and you'll "see" markets everywhere.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · Fiske's four relational models: In the early 1990s the anthropologist and psychologist Alan Fiske proposed that all human relationships are built from four mental templates: communal sharing, authority ranking, equality matching and market pricing. They line up almost one-to-one with Polanyi's householding, redistribution, reciprocity and exchange. What's alike is that both treat the market as one mode among four, and Fiske found that applying the wrong template, like paying your hosts for dinner, feels deeply offensive. What differs is that Polanyi tells a history of institutions, while Fiske describes cognitive structures everyone carries at once.
  • Evolutionary biology · immunity and autoimmunity: The countermovement looks like an immune response, a body defending itself against injury. But an overactive immune system attacks its host: lupus and cytokine storms are protection turned lethal. Fascism was society's autoimmune disease. Unlike the immune system, though, society's reactions weren't tuned by evolution, and nobody tunes them.
  • Distributed systems · backpressure: When upstream produces faster than downstream can absorb, a system has to push back and throttle the source, or queues blow up and the whole service falls over. Polanyi's defense of the Tudors for slowing enclosure is a case for social backpressure. Unlike software, though, society's capacity can't be computed. You only see it when it fails.
  • Buddhism · dependent origination and emptiness: Buddhism teaches that phenomena lack intrinsic nature and arise only from conditions, with no self-standing essence. Polanyi says the economy has no existence apart from social relations, so the "self-regulating market" mistakes a dependent thing for a self-sufficient entity. Unlike the Buddhist claim, his is historical: he thinks disembedding really was attempted and did real damage.

The Book in Ten Lines

① For all of history the economy was embedded in social relations. The nineteenth century first embedded society in the economy.

② The "natural propensity to truck and barter" isn't a fact. Reciprocity, redistribution and householding did most of the organizing, and markets played a supporting role.

③ Labor, land and money weren't produced for sale. They are fictitious commodities, and leaving people, nature and purchasing power wholly to prices will destroy society.

④ A self-adjusting market is a stark utopia. It couldn't last long without annihilating the human and natural substance of society (paraphrased).

⑤ Laissez-faire was planned; planning was not. The state built the market with statutes and workhouses, while protections sprang up on their own across countries.

⑥ The direction of change often can't be stopped, but its rate decides whether a society is transformed or destroyed.

⑦ The double movement: market expansion and social protection grow together and hobble each other, and that tension tore nineteenth-century civilization apart.

⑧ The gold standard demanded pain that democratic societies would no longer bear, so politics went looking for other exits, fascism among them.

⑨ A countermovement guarantees no happy ending: protection can mean factory acts, or tariff wars and dictatorship.

⑩ Ending market society doesn't mean ending markets or freedom. Precious freedoms have to be guarded deliberately by institutions, not left for the market to supply.
