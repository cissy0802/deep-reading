Amoeba Management

Ameeba keiei · Kazuo Inamori · 2006 (with his 1998 book on management accounting, Jitsugaku)

中文 →

In One Sentence

Cut the company into several hundred units of five to fifty people; let each set its own monthly plan, buy and sell from the units next to it, and produce its own profit statement, so that everyone can see today whether their group made money or lost it — the point is not finer control, it is mass-producing a scarce species: turning one businessman into several hundred, until the shift supervisor on the line is doing the arithmetic too, and the arithmetic is his own.

Coordinates

Kazuo Inamori (1932–2022) founded Kyocera, later founded DDI (today's KDDI), and in 2010, aged seventy-eight and drawing no salary, took the chairmanship of Japan Airlines while it was in bankruptcy reorganisation. This book describes the divisional accounting system he worked out at Kyocera. It is the second half of the same man's project: his better-known A Compass to Fulfillment is the doctrine of character; this is the machinery that puts the doctrine on a report every single day. Management accounting scholars treat it as the canonical case of the micro-profit center — profit responsibility pushed down far below the level of a division.

The Central Claims

  • The whole principle of business is one line: maximise revenue, minimise expenses. The difficulty is never understanding it, it is getting ten thousand people to act on it — and people only work for numbers they can see and can recognise their own hand in.
  • So cut the organisation small — small enough that the causal link between a unit's numbers and its own actions is still visible. In a big department's report, one person's effort is diluted into noise; in a small unit's report, the extra push made yesterday shows up today.
  • But cutting small immediately releases a monster: parochial self-interest. Which is why the other half of amoeba management is not accounting but philosophy — without a shared standard of "what is right as a human being," this system will precisely convert one company into several hundred small ones that fleece each other.

The Core Concepts, One by One

1. The amoeba: why that unpleasant name

An amoeba is a single-celled organism with no fixed shape: it deforms to fit its surroundings and, when it grows large enough, splits in two. Inamori chose the name for exactly those three properties — small, deformable, able to divide and recombine.

An amoeba is typically five to fifty people: a production line, a single process step, a sales team. It has its own leader, writes its own monthly plan, and keeps its own books. It is not an administrative department but a small business. When volume grows, one amoeba splits into two; when a line shrinks, two merge back into one. The org chart is not a poster revised once a year but a living body that divides and fuses as the market moves.

The origin is unglamorous: Kyocera grew faster than Inamori could personally supervise, and what he wanted was not more managers but more people who thought like an owner. The conventional answer is hiring and promotion — but the mindset of an owner is not taught, it is extracted by circumstance. Nobody genuinely cares about the electricity bill and the delivery date until they are responsible for a statement with their own name on it. So he did not set out to train businessmen; he set out to manufacture, in bulk, positions that require one, and then put ordinary employees into them.

The shift it forces: when you complain that your staff lack a sense of ownership, the truth is usually that your organisation contains no positions in which anyone could be an owner. Mindset is a product of position, not of training.

2. Hourly efficiency: one report the whole company can read

The heart of the system is a report called the hourly efficiency statement (jikan atari saisan). Its arithmetic is simple enough for a line supervisor who has never taken an accounting class:

The structure of hourly efficiency. It flattens profit into one comparable number: how much added value one hour of this unit's work creates.
LineWhat it contains
Total productionExternal sales plus sales made to other amoebas inside the company
ExpensesMaterials, purchased parts, utilities, depreciation, floor space, purchases from other amoebas… but never the unit's own labour cost
Added valueTotal production minus expenses
Hourly efficiencyAdded value divided by the unit's total hours worked

Two design decisions deserve a hard look.

First, wages are deliberately excluded from expenses. This is the line accountants most often attack, and the most carefully considered thing in the book. Inamori's reason is psychological: put payroll in a unit's cost line and every member, doing the daily numbers, is staring at his own salary as a deduction — being reminded each morning that he is a burden. Worse, amoebas would begin comparing whose people are cheaper, and start bidding labour costs down against each other. So he moved people out of the subtraction in the numerator and into the divisor: labour is no longer a cost but the time in which value is created. That single move changes the report's grammar from "how much did you spend" to "what is your hour worth."

Second, the divisor is hours, not headcount. Overtime therefore stops being free heroism: work two extra hours without a matching rise in added value and the number falls that same day. The metric closes off the easiest shortcut — throwing time at the problem — and pushes people toward yield, process and lead time instead.

The statement is updated daily, compared monthly against plan, and visible to everyone. Its real function is not appraisal but translation: it turns a vague collective goal into a question answerable before you go home — is our hour worth more today than it was yesterday? The shift it forces: most corporate metrics fail to move anyone because the feedback loop is too long and the attribution too muddy. Shortening the loop is itself an act of management.

3. Internal trade: turning cost centres into businesses

The report alone is not enough. In a conventional manufacturer only sales has revenue; every upstream step is a cost centre — and the only contribution a cost centre can make is to spend less. It has defence available to it and no offence.

The decisive move is that amoebas buy and sell from one another. Forming sells semi-finished pieces to firing, firing sells to machining, machining sells to the sales amoeba, which sells to the customer and takes an agreed commission on the sale as its own revenue. Every internal handover is booked as an internal sale, so each process step suddenly has a top line, therefore its own profit and loss, therefore a business to answer for.

The power of this is that it reverses the direction of motivation. A cost-centre supervisor thinks "don't blow the budget." An amoeba leader with a top line starts thinking "can we lift yield and ship more," "can this batch go out two days early," "should we bring this step in-house." He has stopped being a man who saves money and started being a man who makes it.

The cost arrives in the same move: who sets the internal price? Set it high and the upstream unit is milking the downstream one; set it low and the upstream unit works for nothing. Inamori's answer is that internal prices must be derived backwards from the market — once the final selling price is fixed, it is apportioned back across the steps according to the value each adds, with disputes settled by the leader one level up. This is the most honest difficulty in the book: there is no objectively correct transfer price. It is permanently a negotiation requiring arbitration, and the arbiter's fairness is the load-bearing wall of the entire system.

4. Maximise revenue, minimise expenses — and "pricing is management"

Inamori repeats that the principle of business is almost banal: push sales as high as they will go, push costs as low as they will go, and what remains is profit. Manufacturers habitually assume that costs rise in proportion to volume; he refuses that. What he demands is revenue up with expenses flat — breaking the fixed linkage between the two. Amoeba management is the tool for breaking it, because the linkage only comes apart when every small unit is watching both numbers itself.

Its companion is the line he valued most: "pricing is management." A price is not a number quoted by the sales department; it is a decision the person running the business must make personally, because it simultaneously fixes three things — whether the customer buys, how much volume you get, and how much is left per unit. Price too high and the volume vanishes; price too low and a year's work yields nothing. His criterion is to find the single point at which the customer is glad to pay and your own profit is greatest — not the industry norm minus two points.

Why this matters outside manufacturing: it moves profit out of the finance department's year-end conclusion and into a decision made on the front line during every quotation. The shift it forces: most people assume profit is earned through work. Inamori points out that a very large share of it is settled in the thirty seconds when you name a price.

5. Three purposes: what the system is actually for

Inamori states three purposes, and the order matters:

  • Build divisional accounting tied directly to the market — so that a change in market price reaches the smallest unit without being smoothed away level by level. In conventional structures the market signal is filtered on the way down, and by the time the shop floor hears it, it is stale.
  • Develop people with an owner's mindset — by manufacturing positions, as above, not by running courses.
  • Achieve management by everyone — every member can read the unit's statement, so every member can have an opinion about how it is run; where effort and numbers are visibly linked, people actually engage.

The one most often left out of the copy is the second. Plenty of companies import only the first — push accounting downward, make appraisal more granular — and end up with an exquisitely detailed performance surveillance system instead of several hundred businesspeople. The distinguishing question is simple: is the statement a battle map the unit leader uses, or a report card his boss uses? The first makes people think. The second makes people manage the books.

6. The accounting underneath: one-to-one correspondence and the double check

His 1998 book on accounting is the other half of the machine, for the obvious reason that if the books are not trustworthy, several hundred unit statements are just several hundred elegant lies. He gives seven principles; four are worth memorising.

  • One-to-one correspondence — every physical movement of goods must be matched, strictly and immediately, by the corresponding movement of paper and money. No "ship now, paperwork later." This is not only anti-fraud: once slippage is permitted, the books and the warehouse are permanently divorced, and every number downstream loses meaning.
  • The double check — anything involving money or vouchers passes through two people. His stated reason is unusual: not because employees are distrusted, but so as never to leave a person alone with a temptation. The mercy of a good system is that it does not manufacture criminals.
  • Glass-walled management — the numbers are open to everyone. If you are going to demand that everybody participate in running the business, you cannot ask them to do it blindfolded; and openness is itself the strongest form of discipline.
  • Cash-based management — follow the actual movement of money rather than accounting profit. His standing question is: we made all that money, so where is it? Accrual profit can be lying inside inventory and receivables where nobody can touch it.

The remaining three are muscular management (no fat, no stockpiles, buy what you need when you need it), perfectionism (numbers may not be "roughly right"), and improving profitability (carrying the accounting consciousness into every transaction). The shift it forces: an incentive system's ceiling equals the credibility of the data under it. Where the books are soft, finer measurement is more dangerous, not less.

7. Philosophy: why the system turns on you without it

Divide a company into several hundred self-accounting units and parochialism follows almost by construction: upstream wants the internal price higher, downstream wants it lower, everyone wants to hand the unprofitable job to somebody else, and nobody wants to absorb a temporary reallocation for the good of the whole. Amoeba management is a very sharp knife: it amplifies the motive to answer for your own statement several hundredfold — and that motive is morally neutral.

Inamori's countermeasure is not another layer of rules but a shared criterion, drilled relentlessly: ask first what is right as a human being, rather than which choice flatters our unit's numbers. Kyocera wrote it down, recited it, and invoked it in meetings. He is explicit: amoeba management does not work without the philosophy.

This is also the part that transplants worst. A system can be copied; a criterion cannot. It has to be paid for daily in the conduct of the people at the top, and the moment senior management discounts it once in front of real money, the philosophy is demoted to a slogan in everyone's eyes — while the knife stays sharp. The shift it forces: any organisational design that pushes responsibility down and cuts units small must simultaneously answer who prevents local optimisation from eating the whole. The answer is either stronger central coordination or stronger shared values. It cannot be neither.

The Argument in Brief

The book is one straight chain of reasoning: the principle of business is maximum revenue and minimum expense → but ten thousand people will not exert themselves for a ledger they cannot see → so cut the company into hundreds of units that keep their own books → use internal trade to give every process step a top line and make it a business → use one number, added value per hour, to make all units comparable and visible the same day → so that each unit leader is forced by circumstance into being an owner → and because that same knife amplifies parochialism, backstop it with a shared philosophy and books you can trust (one-to-one correspondence, the double check, glass walls, cash).

Its relation to A Compass to Fulfillment fits in a sentence: that book answers why you should raise your character; this one answers how, once raised, it shows up on a report every day.

Misreadings, Criticisms, Live Disputes

  • The commonest misreading is to treat it as a KPI cascade. The statement is a battle map for the unit itself; the moment it becomes the instrument by which superiors grade subordinates, people begin managing the number instead of the business — haggling over transfer prices, dumping loss-making steps on neighbours, dressing up month-end. Identical machinery, opposite effect.
  • Hourly efficiency is not profit. It excludes wages and carries no allocation of corporate overhead or R&D, so it measures value created per hour, not money earned. Two amoebas are comparable precisely because neither includes labour — but anyone who mistakes the figure for real profit, and uses it for investment decisions or cross-company comparison, will reach absurd conclusions.
  • Transfer pricing is the system's permanent wound. There is no objectively right internal price, only negotiation and adjudication. Which means: it consumes a great deal of talking and meeting time; it depends finally on the fairness and judgement of senior management; and inside a company whose leadership is not fair, the system will accelerate the rot rather than correct it.
  • The administrative overhead is heavy. Daily numbers, monthly plans and variance analysis, frequent meetings and morning assemblies are themselves a substantial expense. Kyocera could carry it because its work is highly divisible and its output measurable. Many firms that import the system simply add a layer of bureaucracy.
  • Its domain of validity is badly underestimated. It suits work with clean process boundaries and countable output — manufacturing and sales. In research, design, platform work and tightly coupled knowledge work, "who created this added value" cannot be attributed cleanly, and forcing the split rewards grabbing measurable tasks while avoiding the unmeasurable ones that matter more.
  • The philosophy is genuinely contested. Daily recitation of the corporate creed and "what is right as a human being" as the supreme criterion read to supporters as the system's foundation and to critics as moral instruction shading into a personality cult. Its efficacy also rests heavily on the founder's own moral capital, which is difficult for professional managers to renew.
  • The Japan Airlines turnaround cannot all be booked to amoeba management. Inamori did introduce route-level divisional accounting after taking over in 2010, making each route's daily profit and loss visible, and the airline relisted roughly two and a half years later. All true. But the same period also brought court-supervised debt forgiveness on a very large scale, a capital injection from a state-backed turnaround fund, deep route and headcount cuts, and favourable tax treatment of carried-forward losses. Attributing the recovery to a management accounting system alone is survivor's narrative; the honest version is that it was one conspicuously effective ingredient in a restructuring package, not the package.

Ten Sentences

1. The principle of business is one line — maximise revenue, minimise expenses. The hard part was never understanding it; it is getting ten thousand people to act on it at once.

2. So the company is cut into units of five to fifty people, each planning, accounting and reporting for itself: the org chart is not a poster but a living body that splits and merges with the market.

3. He did not want more managers, he wanted more people who think like owners — and that mindset is not taught, it is extracted by having to sign your own statement. Mindset is a product of position.

4. The one metric is added value per hour: (total production − expenses) ÷ hours worked — arithmetic a line supervisor can do in his head.

5. Wages are deliberately kept out of expenses and put into the divisor instead: people stop being a cost to be cut and become the time in which value is made; the report stops asking what you spent and starts asking what your hour is worth.

6. The divisor is hours, not heads, so overtime is no longer free heroism — put in two extra hours without more added value and the number drops the same day.

7. Let the units buy and sell from each other and every process step acquires a top line: a cost centre can only save money; a unit with revenue starts trying to make it. The price of that is a transfer price nobody can compute and someone must arbitrate.

8. "Pricing is management" — a price simultaneously decides whether they buy, how much you sell, and what is left per unit; a large share of your profit is settled in the thirty seconds when you name it.

9. Underneath sits the accounting: goods matched one-to-one with paper, every cash operation double-checked, numbers open to all, cash rather than accrual as the measure. The double check is not distrust; it is refusing to leave anyone alone with a temptation.

10. And the knife is neutral: amplifying "answer for your own statement" several hundredfold amplifies parochialism by the same factor. Hence Inamori's insistence that without a shared philosophy the system does not stand — every design that cuts responsibility small must say who stops local optimisation from eating the whole.
