King Lear

The Tragedy of King Lear · William Shakespeare · c. 1605–1606

中文 →

In One Sentence

An old king tries to hand over power while keeping the treatment that came with it, so he asks his three daughters to say out loud how much they love him — and that one demand dismantles his family, his kingdom, his mind, and finally whatever he was. What makes the play unbearable is the last turn of the screw: once he has been stripped down to a bare old man and can at last see the world as it is, the world gives him nothing back for having seen it.

Coordinates

Shakespeare (1564–1616) wrote King Lear around 1605–06, mid-way through the great tragedies; the earliest recorded performance was at court on 26 December 1606. The story was off the shelf: old King Leir appears in Holinshed's chronicles and had already been staged as an anonymous play, King Leir, while the Gloucester subplot comes from Sidney's Arcadia. What matters is what Shakespeare changed: in every earlier version Cordelia's army wins, the father is restored, virtue is rewarded. Shakespeare has them both die. The one thing audiences have never forgiven the play for is the thing he added himself.

The Central Claims

  • You cannot give away the substance of authority and keep the title. Lear hands over the land and the rule while reserving "the name, and all th' addition to a king" (paraphrased). Everything that follows is interest on that sentence. He thought his standing lived in other people's respect; it had always lived in his soldiers and his acres.
  • People see only once they've been stripped — and seeing buys them nothing. Lear thinks of the poor for the first time in a storm; Gloucester recognises his son only after his eyes are gone. The play grants that suffering teaches. It coldly refuses the sequel: that being taught will save you.
  • It pushes the question "will heaven set this right?" all the way down, and returns no answer. The characters call on the gods constantly; the gods never once reply, and the only innocent person on stage is killed last. No work in Western literature withholds consolation more completely.

The Core Ideas, One by One

"Nothing": the moment love is put on a scale

Three minutes in, Lear announces to the whole court that the kingdom will be split three ways — "which of you shall we say doth love us most, that we our largest bounty may extend where nature doth with merit challenge?" Look at the structure of that sentence: he isn't asking about love, he is running an auction. The two older daughters read the market instantly. Goneril loves him more than words can wield the matter, dearer than eyesight and liberty; Regan says her sister has described her own heart exactly, only she comes up short — Regan will double it. Cordelia, aside, asks herself what she can possibly say: "Love, and be silent." When her turn comes she says one word: "Nothing."

Lear's reply is the engine of the play: "Nothing will come of nothing. Speak again." So she says it in full: "I love your majesty according to my bond, no more nor less." And she adds the thing that is almost inhuman as courtship and unanswerable as logic: if my sisters give all their love to you, why do they have husbands? When I marry, half my love and care and duty goes with him.

What makes this hold up a four-hour play is that it catches an extremely ordinary mistake. Lear doesn't want love; he wants love in measurable form — now, in public, by percentage, convertible into land. And love demanded in that form is precisely the thing that can't be delivered in it. Goneril and Regan can pay, because what they're paying with is talk. Cordelia can't, because what she has is the real thing. The old man's verdict comes out exactly inverted: he exiles the only love in the room and keeps the two invoices.

"Nothing" then recurs through the play like an infection. Edmund pockets his forged letter and tells his father it is "nothing, my lord." The Fool tells Lear he is now a nought with no figure in front of it — "I am a fool, thou art nothing." By Act 3 Lear really is nothing. The play is a subtraction machine: it shows you a man holding everything, removes it one item at a time, and asks what is left at zero.

"Reason not the need": the surplus is the person

Lear keeps one piece of substance for himself: a hundred knights, quartered by turns on each daughter — partly display, partly the only armed men still taking his orders. So the daughters start haggling. Goneril says fifty will do. He goes to Regan, who suggests twenty-five. He turns back to Goneril: then I'll come to you, fifty is better than twenty-five. And the two of them work him down together:

Pushed to "what need one," Lear delivers the most important defence in the play: "O, reason not the need! Our basest beggars are in the poorest thing superfluous. Allow not nature more than nature needs, man's life is cheap as beast's."

In one stroke that defines what a human being is, and the definition is counter-intuitive: what separates us from animals is not the necessities but the useless margin above them — the decent coat you didn't strictly require, the bit of ceremony, the deference. The daughters' logic is airtight: you don't govern anymore, so what do you need them for? That is exactly what makes efficiency logic so brutal — it is always right, and it is always shaving people down toward the animal. You can hear Regan's "What need one?" in every modern argument about what can be cut.

How it changes the way you see things: next time you're about to dismiss someone with "you don't actually need that," remember Lear — what's being refused is usually not the object but the last proof that they still count. And note that Lear loses this argument. He convinces no one and walks straight out into the storm. Being right is no protection against being put out of the house.

Unaccommodated man: the storm as an epistemology lesson

Shut out by both daughters, Lear runs onto the heath and shouts at the lightning — "Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks!" — first demanding that the sky smash this ungrateful world flat, then offering the famous self-defence: "I am a man more sinned against than sinning." Up to here he is just an old man who feels wronged.

The turn comes as he is about to duck into the hovel. He stops and says something that has nothing to do with himself: "Poor naked wretches, wheresoe'er you are, that bide the pelting of this pitiless storm — how shall your houseless heads and unfed sides, your looped and windowed raggedness, defend you from seasons such as these? O, I have ta'en too little care of this!" Then the line addressed to everyone who has ever held power: "Take physic, pomp; expose thyself to feel what wretches feel."

Notice the mechanism. Lear was king for decades and must have heard a thousand reports of famine and vagrancy. He never once knew it. What made him know it was standing out in the rain himself. The play's definition of compassion is correspondingly unsentimental: compassion isn't information, it's shared exposure — you remember that some people have no roof at the point where yours comes off.

One step further is the play's most astonishing scene. Inside the hovel is a beggar feigning madness, "Poor Tom" — in fact the framed and fugitive Edgar, smeared with filth and wearing a blanket. Lear stares at him and arrives at a philosophical question: "Is man no more than this? … Thou art the thing itself: unaccommodated man is no more but such a poor, bare, forked animal as thou art." Then he starts tearing at his clothes: "Off, off, you lendings!"

The word repays a pause. Accommodation means lodging, provision, kit; unaccommodated is a man issued with nothing — no clothes, no roof, no title, no standing. Lear's point is that king, father, lord are all lendings, and the only part a person genuinely owns is the shivering animal in front of him. It is close to an anthropological manifesto, and the man shouting it is the one who used to stand at the very top — he has walked all the way down to the floor of common humanity, and only there does he stand level with anybody.

Two kinds of Nature: Lear's cosmic order, Edmund's jungle

The fiercest conflict in the play is between two uses of a single word. Nature is used more than forty times, and the two sides mean opposite things by it.

For Lear (and Gloucester), nature is an order: fathers are honoured, ranks hold, and the heavens answer to human conduct. So when his eldest daughter defies him, his instinct is to file a complaint with a goddess — "Hear, Nature, hear! dear goddess, hear!" — asking her to make Goneril barren, or else give her a child from whom she can learn "how sharper than a serpent's tooth it is to have a thankless child." Gloucester does the same, blaming the recent eclipses for sons turning on fathers and subjects on kings. In this scheme, family duty is part of the structure of the universe: break it and the sky reacts.

Edmund's nature is a jungle. Gloucester's illegitimate son opens with a soliloquy that is unnervingly modern: "Thou, Nature, art my goddess; to thy law my services are bound. Wherefore should I stand in the plague of custom…" His accounting is clean: I am a year or so younger than my legitimate brother and was conceived outside a marriage bed rather than in one — why does he get everything and I nothing? If the rules are mere custom, then redistribute by capacity and nerve. "Now, gods, stand up for bastards!"

He also demolishes his father's astrology in the sharpest prose in the play: "This is the excellent foppery of the world, that when we are sick in fortune — often the surfeit of our own behaviour — we make guilty of our disasters the sun, the moon, and the stars … I should have been that I am had the maidenliest star in the firmament twinkled on my bastardizing."

Why this matters: by 1605 Edmund has already finished the modern sentence — the universe has no morals, only power and alibis. He is the only character who is unsuperstitious, prays to nobody, acts fastest, and is by some distance the most likeable villain; Shakespeare deliberately makes him clever, candid and funny, so that he's hard to argue down. And here is the terrible part: the play never arranges for the universe to refute him. Lear shouts at heaven all night and heaven rains. No thunderbolt interrupts the blinding. Edmund does die in the end — but he is killed by his brother's sword. By a man, not by a god.

How it changes the way you see the world: the collision of these two "natures" is the dramatic version of asking whether reality is on the side of justice. Lear's camp believes it is; Edmund bets it isn't. Shakespeare's answer is neither yes nor a simple no, but something colder: if justice exists, only people can carry it out — and whoever waits for heaven to sort things out will spend the night standing in the rain.

Blindness: the parallel line, and the eyes that come out

This is Shakespeare's only tragedy with a subplot as fully weighted as the main plot, and the two lines are the same story shot twice.

The double plot isn't repetition, it's corroboration: run the same law through two households and it stops being one old man's bad luck and becomes how the world works.

The subplot supplies the cruellest scene in Shakespeare: Gloucester, caught helping Lear, is pinned to a chair and has his eyes put out in front of Regan — "Out, vile jelly!" The point isn't the cruelty. Gloucester was blind while he had eyes, and sees once they are gone. He says it best himself: "I stumbled when I saw", and the flatly desolate "I have no way and therefore want no eyes."

It rhymes with Act 1, where Kent defends Cordelia, is roared at, and fires back "See better, Lear." Sight in this play was never about vision: Lear is blinded by flattery, Gloucester by a letter.

Then comes the strange scene at Dover. Blind Gloucester wants to kill himself and asks the "mad beggar" — whom he does not know is his son — to take him to the cliff. Edgar leads him onto flat ground and builds the precipice entirely out of language (the samphire-gatherer halfway down no bigger than a head, the fishermen like mice), then lets him jump. He pitches forward onto level turf; Edgar changes his voice, runs up as a passing stranger, and tells him no one falls that far and lives: "Thy life's a miracle." Gloucester believes it and stops seeking death.

What saves Gloucester is not a miracle but a lie his son invented. It is the tenderest and most disquieting passage in the play: sometimes a person needs a story in order to keep living, and the only possible author of that story is another person.

The Fool and madness: only those outside can describe the system

The Fool is a salaried idiot of the court. He is also the one person who can abuse Lear to his face and keep his head — because his position formally declares that nothing he says counts. It's an elegant institutional trick: a power structure that wants a channel for truth begins by appointing someone who doesn't matter to speak it.

Every line of his cuts. You split the crown and gave both halves away — that's eating the egg and wearing the two shells as a hat. You made your daughters your mothers, and put down your own breeches to be whipped. Lear can't answer any of it, because the Fool only ever tells him things he already knows and won't admit.

After Act 3 the Fool simply vanishes, his last line the riddling "And I'll go to bed at noon." Structurally he leaves at exactly the right moment: once Lear is mad himself, truth-telling no longer needs to be outsourced.

And Lear's madness is the play's strangest device: what he says after losing his mind is clearer than anything he said with it. He arraigns imaginary daughters on the heath (two joint-stools in the dock), then swerves into total social criticism — "a dog's obeyed in office"; "Through tattered clothes small vices do appear; robes and furred gowns hide all. Plate sin with gold, and the strong lance of justice hurtless breaks; arm it in rags, a pygmy's straw does pierce it."

Three kinds of madness stand on that heath: Lear genuinely mad, Edgar feigning it, the Fool professionally mad — one has lost his mind, one his identity, one never had a position to lose. That isn't decoration. Only people who have already fallen out of the order can describe the order accurately. Everyone still inside it is performing, and by the end can't say who they were.

Bond: the one thing in this play that holds

Cordelia's keyword in scene one is bond — contract, tie, obligation you were born into. It sounds chilly at the time. By the end you notice that every reliable person in this play runs on that "no more nor less," not on warmth of feeling.

  • Kent: banished on the spot, he shaves, changes his accent, comes back under a false name and hires on as a servant to the man who threw him out, escorting him to the end without a word of complaint or any prospect of reward.
  • Cordelia: returns with an army for her father. At the reunion Lear kneels and says he is "a very foolish fond old man" and begs her to forget and forgive. She answers in four words: "No cause, no cause." You did me no wrong; there is nothing to pardon. It is the most moving line in the play because it cancels the ledger outright.
  • A servant with no name: as Cornwall goes for the second eye, his own servant draws on him — "I have served you ever since I was a child, but better service have I never done you than now to bid you hold" (paraphrased) — and wounds the duke, who dies of it. A servant turning a sword on a duke was close to unstageable in 1606, and Shakespeare gives the play's only successful act of justice to a man without a name.

Put them side by side and the play's values are plain: it doesn't believe in declarations, it believes in service; not in "how much I love you," but in "I'm here, doing what I owe." Lear demanded the first in Act 1; the whole cost of the play is learning to recognise the second — and he recognises it too late.

Five nevers: the consolation it refuses to give

The French army loses and Lear and Cordelia are captured — which produces the happiest moment of his life: come, let's away to prison, we two alone will sing like birds in a cage, I'll kneel and ask you for your blessing, and we'll hear who's in and out of favour at court as if we were God's spies. It is the first time he has wanted a person rather than power.

Then Edmund's standing order is carried out. Edgar kills him; dying, Edmund has a spasm of conscience — "Some good I mean to do, despite of mine own nature" — and countermands the hanging, minutes too late. Lear comes on carrying his daughter's body, and his first utterance isn't a word at all: "Howl, howl, howl, howl!" Then:

"Why should a dog, a horse, a rat have life, and thou no breath at all? Thou'lt come no more, never, never, never, never, never."

Those five nevers are a perfect line of iambic pentameter — English verse's basic measure, five light-heavy pairs to a line — except that every beat is loaded with the same negation. He fills the most regular metre in the language with a content of exactly nothing: language still moving after it has run out.

The last lines are harder still. Lear stares at her face — "Look on her, look, her lips, look there, look there!" — and dies. What did he see? If he thought she was breathing, he died inside a mistake that spared him; if he saw nothing, it is pure void. Shakespeare will not even grant you the certainty of knowing whether the man died comforted. What is left is Edgar's exhausted couplet: the weight of this sad time we must obey, "speak what we feel, not what we ought to say" — a play that opened on ceremonial speech closing on an instruction to stop making it.

The Spine

The main line is as simple as a physics experiment: give a man everything, take it away one item at a time, see what remains — then, once he can finally see, take that last thing too.

  • Act 1 | The deal: Lear trades land for declarations; Cordelia refuses to price herself and is exiled. Meanwhile Edmund's forged letter sends Gloucester hunting his elder son. In a single day, two old men drive out the only child who loves them.
  • Act 2 | The audit: the retinue is bargained from a hundred down to none. He cries "reason not the need" and walks into the storm — once the power is gone, the title redeems for nothing.
  • Act 3 | Stripped: heath, lightning, hovel. Lear thinks of the poor for the first time, meets naked Poor Tom, tears at his clothes and loses his mind; the same night Gloucester's eyes are put out and he is thrust out of doors. Both lines bottom out in the same rain.
  • Act 4 | Sight returns: blind Gloucester is led toward Dover by the son he doesn't recognise; mad Lear delivers the play's most lucid social criticism; father and daughter are reconciled — "no cause, no cause." Everyone arrives at understanding, and it is late.
  • Act 5 | Repossession: the sisters poison each other over Edmund; Edgar kills him in single combat; Gloucester's heart bursts at the moment he knows his son; the reprieve arrives late, Cordelia is hanged, and Lear dies holding her. Everyone gets what they deserve, and it saves nobody.

So what has it proved? Suffering does make people see — but making people see was never what the suffering was for. Lear learns pity, learns to apologise, learns to want a daughter instead of a kingdom, and is then relieved of the daughter. Five acts establish that moral education is possible; the last eighty lines establish that it doesn't pay out. The play declines to enter "at least he understood" on the balance sheet as compensation.

Misreadings & Honest Objections

  • Misreading 1: Cordelia is the model of perfect filial virtue. She is right, but "nothing" is also a choice hard to the point of pride: she knows her father wants one presentable sentence, knows what humiliating him in open court will cost, and gives not an inch. The play writes her not as a gentle saint but as a woman exactly as stubborn as her father — the resemblance is part of the tragedy.
  • Misreading 2: Lear is just senile. He is frightened early on — "O, let me not be mad, not mad, sweet heaven!" — and he is no innocent about people; he simply wants the performance. His real error isn't age, it's trying to run a title with no assets behind it: transfer the land, the soldiers and the revenues, then require everyone to keep treating you as king. That mistake has no age limit — every founder who "steps back" and still expects to be consulted is making Lear's move.
  • Misreading 3: the lesson is "honour thy parents." The elder daughters are genuinely vicious — but who raised them, and how? From the first scene Lear uses affection as currency and humiliation as discipline. Less a play about ungrateful children than about what a power relationship conducted in the language of love looks like once the power is withdrawn.
  • It was "fixed" for a century and a half. In 1681 Nahum Tate rewrote the ending: Cordelia survives and marries Edgar, Lear is restored, the Fool is cut entirely. That happy version effectively owned the English stage until 1838. Even Dr Johnson admitted that Cordelia's death so shocked him he could not bear to reread the final scenes for years, until editing forced him to. The reception history is the best gloss on the play: its own culture could not digest its ending.
  • Dispute 1: is Lear redeemed, or reduced to zero? A. C. Bradley's classic reading has Lear dying in a flash of joy (believing Cordelia alive), the whole play a punishing purification — call it "the redemption of King Lear." Jan Kott, in Shakespeare Our Contemporary, reads it inside out: he sets it beside Beckett's Endgame and calls it grotesque — the gods absent, the cliff a fake, the suffering yielding no metaphysical return, people flailing on a bare stage. (Peter Brook's glacial 1962 production came directly out of that reading.) Both readings have textual warrant, and the distance between them is the depth of the play.
  • Dispute 2: Tolstoy said it was bad art; Orwell said Tolstoy was frightened. In 1906 Tolstoy attacked King Lear as absurd in plot (nobody recognises a disguised acquaintance), monotonously bombastic in speech, and inferior to the anonymous old play. Orwell answered in 1947 in "Lear, Tolstoy and the Fool": Tolstoy had himself given away his estate late in life, renounced, and expected spiritual dividends — and got family misery. He was Lear, which is why he couldn't stand it. Orwell also names the play's moral core: Lear's mistake is that he renounces in order to be repaid, and a renunciation made for a return isn't renunciation, it's an investment.
  • Dispute 3: the play's imagination of women is narrow. Lear's tirade — women are centaurs below the waist (in Greek myth, human above, horse below) — pins his daughters' betrayal directly on female flesh, and it is the hardest passage to defend. A related thread is worth noticing: there is no mother anywhere in the play (neither Lear's queen nor Gloucester's wife exists), and in his rage Lear says "O, how this mother swells up toward my heart! Hysterica passio" — hysteria being, in the medicine of the day, a disease of the womb. Critics such as Coppélia Kahn ("The Absent Mother in King Lear") argue that what Lear cannot tolerate is his own need to be cared for, and he punishes that need as though it were a female crime.
  • The King Lear you own was stitched together. The 1608 Quarto and the 1623 Folio differ substantially — the Folio drops the mock trial on the heath, among much else — and the Oxford edition simply prints them as two separate plays. Keep that in mind whenever someone tells you what Shakespeare "really meant."

Ten Sentences

1. It all begins as an auction: what Lear wants isn't love but love in measurable form — public, apportioned, convertible into land — and whatever can be delivered on those terms isn't love.

2. "Nothing will come of nothing" is his line, and the play spends five acts proving the opposite: something only grows in a man after he's been subtracted to zero — too late.

3. His real error isn't senility but keeping the title while giving away the assets: standing never lived in other people's respect; it lived in his land and his soldiers.

4. "Reason not the need!" — what separates a man from a beast is precisely the surplus beyond necessity; every cut made in the name of efficiency takes away someone's last proof that they count.

5. Decades as king taught him nothing about the poor; one night standing in the rain did: compassion isn't information, it's shared exposure.

6. "Unaccommodated man is no more but such a poor, bare, forked animal" — crown, fatherhood and dignity are all lendings; tearing at his clothes is the first moment he stands on the same floor as anyone else.

7. By 1605 Edmund has finished the modern sentence — the stars don't care, the rules are only custom, what's left is strength and alibis — and the play never arranges for the universe to refute him.

8. Gloucester "stumbled when he saw," and what saves his life is not a miracle but a lie his son invented: sometimes a person needs a story to go on living, and only another person can write it.

9. The people who hold never declare: Kent comes back under a false name to serve, a nameless servant draws on a duke, and Cordelia says only "no cause, no cause" — this play trusts "I'm here, doing what I owe," never "look how much I love you."

10. Five nevers fill a flawless line of verse with a single negation: suffering does make you see, but being seen by you was never what the world's suffering was for — and Shakespeare will not write "at least he understood" into the accounts as compensation.
