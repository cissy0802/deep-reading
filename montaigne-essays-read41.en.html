In One Sentence

A forty-year-old French gentleman retires to the tower of his own house intending to read out his days, and instead writes a book nobody had written before: he does not argue for any truth, he simply records — with startling honesty — what one particular man thinks, becomes, and later takes back. The method is the argument. Writing one ordinary person down truthfully turns out to get closer to the human condition than any system does, and being honestly uncertain turns out to be more reliable than being confidently right.

Where He Stands

Michel de Montaigne (1533–1592) came from a merchant family near Bordeaux that had only recently bought its way into the nobility. His father had him raised by a tutor who spoke nothing but Latin to him, so Latin was effectively his first language. He served as a magistrate in the Bordeaux parlement, twice as mayor of Bordeaux, and as an occasional go-between for Henri of Navarre and the King — because his entire adult life ran inside the French Wars of Religion, thirty-odd years of Catholics and Protestants slaughtering each other. The Essays are the record of one man insisting on "I'm not sure" in an era when everyone else held an absolute truth and was killing with it. He invented the essay as a form, and invented the practice of using oneself as the specimen; everyone after him — Pascal, Rousseau, Emerson, Nietzsche, Woolf — has had either to pick a fight with him or to admit the debt.

The Central Claims

  • Human judgment is far frailer than humans assume, so the correct posture is suspension, not assertion. Not "I know there is no truth" — that is itself an assertion — but the thing compressed into a question mark: what do I know? Staying inside that question is the only honest place to stand.
  • The road to the universal runs through the utterly particular, not through abstraction. "Every man bears the whole form of the human condition" — so writing one person all the way down is writing everyone; and the only person you can write that way is yourself, because nobody else's interior is available to you.
  • A person is not a stable entity but an ongoing motion. "I do not portray being; I portray passing." An honest book therefore has to permit self-contradiction and has to keep the old wrong sentences, because the changing of the mind is the man. He did not think it through and then write; he wrote in order to find out what he thought.

The Core Ideas, One at a Time

Essai: turning "let me try" into a literary form

The French essai comes from essayer — to try, to test, to assay, as in testing the purity of a metal. Choosing it as a title was Montaigne telling his reader something quite strange up front: these are not treatises (I am not proving anything) and not sermons (I am not converting you). They are trials my judgment runs on various subjects, with no guarantee that any of them arrives.

How big a difference is that? In architectural terms, a scholastic work like Aquinas's is a cathedral: lay the foundation (first principles), build up in courses, every stone load-bearing, one failure and the whole thing comes down. Montaigne writes sea trials: push a small boat out, see how far it goes in these waters, come back and try another heading when it stalls. There are 107 chapters, from "Of Friendship" and "Of Conscience" to "Of Thumbs" and "Of Smells," some over a hundred pages, some two. He cheerfully admits his titles often have little to do with his contents — he digresses and does not intend to fix it, because digression is what a mind actually moving looks like.

Why does that matter? Because before him the default contract of writing was: the author already knows, the reader does not yet. Montaigne tore that up and offered another: the author is also mid-process, and the reader is invited to watch the process. The whole family of forms we now take for granted — the personal essay, the column, the thinking-out-loud blog post — are descendants of that little boat. What it changes for you: you are allowed to stop pretending you have a conclusion.

"What do I know?" — doubt written as a question, not a statement

In 1576 Montaigne had a medal struck: a pair of balance scales on one face, and on the other his motto, Que sais-je? — What do I know? The scales are the point: two pans, neither of them dipping. So is the grammar. It is not "I know nothing," because that sentence eats itself (how would you know?). It is a genuine question, a way of holding judgment in the air and refusing to let it land.

Behind it sits a body of Greek philosophy he had just encountered: Pyrrhonism, a thoroughgoing skepticism holding that for any claim you can find a counter-claim of equal weight, so the rational move is epoché (suspension of judgment) — and the byproduct of suspension is ataraxia, freedom from the anxiety of having to take a side. Sextus Empiricus came back into European circulation via a Latin translation in 1562, and Montaigne is his most important heir.

In the longest chapter of the book, the "Apology for Raymond Sebond" (II.12), he swings this blade for hundreds of pages: the senses deceive (the same dish is bitter when you are ill); reason runs on the senses and is therefore no safer; different peoples reach flatly opposite moral verdicts on the same act. And then the famous reversal: "When I play with my cat, who knows whether she is not passing time with me rather than I with her?" (paraphrased). That is not a witticism. It flips over the default premise that the human is the measure.

How it changes the way you see things: in his hands skepticism leads not to paralysis but to tolerance — if I accept that my judgment may be wrong, I have no standing to burn you for reaching a different one. In a country bleeding out over doctrine, that was not intellectual fastidiousness; it was a political position. And it has a use today: next time you are in an argument, don't ask who is right — ask first what your own conclusion actually rests on and how much pressure that support would take. Most strong opinions turn out to be sitting on alarmingly little.

"I am myself the matter of my book"

The 1580 note "To the Reader" says it flatly: "Thus, reader, I am myself the matter of my book" — and advises you not to waste your leisure on so frivolous and vain a subject. The modesty is real and also tactical, because what he was about to do had almost no precedent: treating the self as the object of study rather than as the position from which one speaks.

Keep those two apart. Others had written "I" before him. Augustine's Confessions is about a soul being rescued by God — the "I" is testimony, and it terminates in God. Montaigne's "I" leads nowhere else; it is the destination. He records what he likes to eat, what frightens him, how bad his memory is, exactly how a kidney stone hurts, his habits of sleep and of the privy — not to confess and not to boast, but because this is the one human specimen he can observe at close range.

So isn't that narcissism? He has a hard answer, tucked into "Of Repentance" in Book III: "Every man bears the whole form of the human condition." Meaning: humanity does not divide into representative great men and inconsequential ordinary ones. Being a person is written on any person — so dig one specific human all the way down and what you strike is not his private business but everybody's.

It was a methodological bet, and it paid. The use to you is immediate: the things you assume are your own private defects — the awkwardness, the backsliding, the gap between what you know you should do and what you do — are not your personal failings. They are the spec sheet for the species. The strongest experience of reading Montaigne is finding that a Frenchman four hundred years ago wrote down the thing you thought only you knew about yourself.

"I portray passing": a book that deliberately keeps its old drafts

The Essays have a structural fact that is genuinely unique: the book has three geological layers. Books I–II appeared in 1580; in 1588 he added Book III and pushed large new passages into the old text; after his death in 1592 his "adopted daughter" Marie de Gournay produced the 1595 edition from his annotated copy, adding another layer. Scholars still mark these A / B / C. The crucial part: he adds, and almost never deletes. The judgment of the man at forty lies on the same page as the rebuttal by the man at fifty-five.

His justification is the deepest sentence in the book: "I do not portray being; I portray passing." If a person is a flow to begin with, then a book about him that has been tidied into consistency has forged the very thing that mattered.

Our public speech runs almost exactly the other way — changing your mind is a gotcha, consistency is a brand. Treat the reversal as part of the work and it stops being a scandal and becomes evidence: proof that this person was actually thinking, rather than defending.

"We are all patchwork": inconsistency is not a flaw, it is the build

Book II opens with "Of the Inconstancy of Our Actions," an underrated document in the history of psychology. He starts with the evidence: the same man is brave today and cowardly tomorrow, generous to strangers and stingy at home, lucid about one thing and unbelievably stupid about another. His conclusion: "We are all patchwork, and so shapeless and diverse in composition that each bit, each moment, plays its own game" (paraphrased). He goes further — the distance between us and ourselves is often greater than the distance between us and other people.

Why is that counterintuitive? Because classical virtue theory and ordinary language alike presuppose a stable "character." Montaigne says that is narrative, not fact: we see a person perform in a few situations, autocomplete an entire personality from it, and then use that fictional consistency to predict him, to demand things of him — and to demand them of ourselves.

Modern psychology eventually supplied the experiments: behavior turns out to be shaped by situation to a degree that often outweighs supposedly stable traits, while our explanations of other people systematically overweight "that's just how he is" and underweight "look at the spot he was in" — a bias called the fundamental attribution error. Montaigne had no laboratory. He got to the same place through four hundred pages of honest observation of a single subject.

How it changes things. About others: don't fix a verdict on someone from one performance, and don't expect anyone to be the same person in every room — that isn't hypocrisy, it's construction. About yourself: "I am not the same person I was yesterday" is not a symptom, it's the standard condition — and admitting it stops you putting yourself on trial for every inconsistency.

Custom: the gentlest tyrant — and "Of Cannibals"

In "Of Custom" (I.23) Montaigne describes coutume as a tyrant that takes power quietly: it begins with mild suggestion, and once you are used to it, it shows a violent face — because by then you can no longer tell "we have always done it this way" from "this is how things are." The whole power of custom is its invisibility: it does not persuade you, it prevents you from noticing there was a question here at all.

The famous application is "Of Cannibals" (I.31). His subject is the Tupinambá of Brazil, known to him through a servant who had been to the New World and through three Tupi men he met face to face at Rouen in 1562. He writes their horrors down plainly: in tribal war they eat their captured enemies. Then he turns the blade: "I think there is nothing barbarous and savage in that nation, except that every man calls barbarism whatever is not his own practice." And he gives the comparison: torturing a man alive and roasting him by degrees — which is what was happening weekly in the French wars — is a great deal more barbarous than eating one already dead.

Better still are the two observations he records the three Tupi making about France (paraphrased): first, that grown armed men were taking orders from a child (Charles IX was then a boy king); second, that some men were gorged and stuffed with everything while others begged at their gates — and that the starving ones had not taken the full ones by the throat. These are the sharpest lines of social criticism in the book, and Montaigne adds not one word of comment. He just lends us the outsider's eyes, and our self-evident arrangements fall apart on their own.

This is the earliest and most elegant piece of cultural relativism in Europe. But watch the direction of the move: he is not admiring cannibals, he is using them as a mirror held up to France. The transferable method: the next time a practice strikes you as obviously wrong, ask first — if I had just arrived from elsewhere and were seeing our own arrangements for the first time, which of them would leave me speechless?

Death: from "learn to die" to "don't bother, Nature will teach you"

Early Montaigne wrote a chapter with an impeccably Stoic title: "That to Philosophize Is to Learn to Die" (I.20) — think about death constantly, rehearse it, wear off its strangeness, so that you are not caught out when it comes. That was the standard Senecan prescription and he took it whole.

Then reality interrupted. In "Of Practice" (II.6) he records a riding accident: one of his men came up behind at full gallop and knocked him flying; he was carried home unconscious and bringing up blood, and his household expected him to die. The experience he reported after coming round was nothing like what he had prepared for. It was not terror. It was a strange sweetness and slackening, like sliding into sleep — the sense of life gently going out. That is first-hand data, and it demolished the theory he had read.

So by "Of Physiognomy" (III.12), late, he changes his position in public: unlettered peasants, he observes, die better than philosophers precisely because they do not rehearse — while all that rehearsal takes a thing that happens exactly once and makes it happen hundreds of times in advance. He writes, in effect: if you don't know how to die, don't trouble yourself; Nature will instruct you on the spot, fully and sufficiently.

Why the reversal is worth dwelling on: it is the book's whole method in one live specimen. He did not swap in a more fashionable opinion; he was corrected by something that happened to his body — and he did not go back and delete the earlier chapter. Both stand. What you get from it is not the advice itself but the move: when your experience and your convictions come to blows, trust the experience, and keep the record of both.

The back shop, and "the mayor and Montaigne were always two"

"Of Solitude" (I.39) contains an image that has been quoted for four hundred years: "We must reserve a back shop all our own, entirely free, in which to establish our real liberty and our principal retreat and solitude." The image needs unpacking. A sixteenth-century shop had an open front where anyone could walk in, handle the goods and haggle — and a small room behind it belonging to the owner, which customers did not enter. People, he says, need one of those: keep the shopfront trading — obligations, office, courtesies, performance, all of it done properly — but hold a back room nobody may enter, where you keep company with yourself and where the valuations are not set by the day's trade out front.

He tested this in office, as mayor of Bordeaux twice over. In "Of Husbanding Your Will" (III.10) he puts it bluntly: "The mayor and Montaigne have always been two, with a very clear separation." He treated public office as a coat you put on — wear it seriously, wear it well, but do not let it grow into your skin. He was rude about the moral demand of his day that a man burn himself entirely for his office: that isn't nobler, it is a failure to tell the role from the person.

Why this lands so hard now: the dominant story of modern working life runs the other way — it invites you to make the job your identity, the company your family, and your performance review the currency you value yourself in. The back shop is the earliest and calmest resistance to all that: you can be superbly good at the job and still not have pledged yourself as collateral. A quick self-test: if the title vanished tomorrow, what would be left to evaluate yourself by? If the honest answer is "not much," the shopfront has swallowed the back room.

Bees: a head well made, not a head well filled

"Of the Education of Children" (I.26) is a landmark in the history of teaching, and its core compresses into a maxim: better a head well made (tête bien faite) than a head well filled (tête bien pleine). He mocks the schooling of his time, in which pupils are funnels — knowledge poured in and poured back out unchanged: "We know how to say 'Cicero says thus,' 'this is Plato's opinion' — but what do we say ourselves? What do we judge? What do we do?" (paraphrased)

His positive image is the bee: bees rifle flowers here and there, but the honey they make is entirely their own — it is no longer thyme or marjoram. The student forages across schools of thought, but must digest it into his own judgment; otherwise the quotations are just other people's property stored on his premises. (The man who wrote that had over a thousand quotations from the ancients in his own book, and knew the irony perfectly well: he quotes constantly, but every quotation is bent to his own use, often against what its author meant.)

The test of whether you have actually learned something is not whether you can repeat it, but whether it changed what you do and how you judge. Point that ruler at yourself: of the books you read this past year, how many actually altered one of your practices? Those are the ones you read.

The Skeleton

A book with no structure can still have its structure stated, in three moves.

First, he demolishes the foundation called certainty. The senses deceive; reason is parasitic on the senses; custom passes itself off as nature; different peoples' moralities flatly contradict each other. The "Apology for Raymond Sebond" spends hundreds of pages grinding human cognitive capacity into the dust — and arrives not at nihilism but at the question mark: what do I know? The point of pulling down the house is not to leave people homeless; it is to stop them using the house as a weapon. In an age of mutual slaughter over conviction, that step carried political weight.

Second, with the foundation gone, where does he stand? On the one thing available for close inspection: himself. If no general claim is safe, retreat to what you can personally verify — how this particular man thinks, changes, recants, and what dying actually felt like when it came near. What keeps this from collapsing into vanity is the bet: every man bears the whole form of the human condition. Writing "me" thus becomes the only reliable route to writing "us."

Third, what the specimen shows turns around and confirms the first move. He finds no stable soul but a loose heap, inconstant, swinging with circumstance; and his own positions get overturned by himself several times in fifteen years. What business does a man who cannot pin himself down have pronouncing on the world? Skepticism stops being a philosophical stance and becomes a field report.

All three together prove exactly one thing: given that this is what people are, the right way to live with others and with yourself is tolerance, no insistence on consistency, judgment held in suspension, duty at the shopfront and freedom in the back room, living along the grain of nature and dying along it too. That is the entire moral doctrine. The thousand pages are thick with examples, quotations, retractions and digressions — not with conclusions. Which is the lesson: conclusions are cheap; the process is the man.

Misreadings & Honest Criticism

  • Misreading 1: that he is a mellow, lovable dispenser of aphorisms. The Essays are hard going: digressive, stuffed with Latin, structurally loose, contradicting themselves inside a single chapter. Most circulating "Montaigne wisdom" consists of sentences lifted out of complicated contexts, with the thing they were hesitating over removed. The difficulty isn't in his sentences; it's that he refuses to close the argument for you.
  • Misreading 2: that "Of Cannibals" celebrates the noble savage. That figure arrives with Rousseau's generation. Montaigne does not make the Tupi innocent — they make war, take revenge, eat prisoners, and he writes it. His move is comparative, not adoring: their barbarity is the yardstick that measures ours as worse. Read as exotic romanticism, the essay loses its only edge.
  • Misreading 3: that suspending judgment means doing nothing and standing nowhere. He served a decade in public office, carried messages between two warring camps, and kept his estate and his tenants alive through a civil war. His doubt is aimed at "I am certain, therefore you must," not at action.
  • Pascal's objection (the weightiest hit). Pascal knew Montaigne nearly by heart, and long stretches of the Pensées are an argument with him — but he delivered a fatal line: "the foolish project he has of painting himself." The deeper charge is worse: that Montaigne's serene, take-it-as-it-comes attitude to death is, for a creature facing eternity, a lazy evasion — "he inspires indifference about salvation." If something in Montaigne's calm makes you faintly uneasy, Pascal has said it for you.
  • Did he actually believe? Still unsettled. The logic of the "Apology" is that reason can do nothing, therefore faith must come by grace alone — a position called fideism. The trouble is that the argument cuts both ways: it can equally be read as using skepticism to dissolve religion and leave a shell. He lived and died a Catholic and received the last rites; and in 1676 Rome put the Essays on the Index, which tells you the Church of the time wasn't convinced either. The honest verdict: the text cannot settle it, and both readings have strong evidence.
  • The "three stages" story is too tidy. Pierre Villey's influential scheme divided the work into Stoic, skeptical, and naturalist phases; later scholarship largely regards that line as drawn too cleanly — the A/B/C additions interleave, and he oscillates on the same questions rather than progressing through them. Treat the table above as schematic, not as a timeline.
  • Political and personal charges. Critics note that at the end of his second mayoral term in 1585, with plague breaking out in Bordeaux, he was outside the city and did not return to hand over office in person; and that he wrote against burning oneself up for public duty. Defenders point out that he had written to the city officers for instructions and that the city was already disintegrating. Either way the argument matters: an ethic that ranks self-preservation this high looks thin at exactly the moment self-sacrifice is called for. Unresolved — and the best entrance to the book's limits.
  • He is dated in identifiable ways. Several passages on marriage and on women carry the prejudices of his century, and his information about the peoples of the Americas is second-hand and mediated. Treating him as a man who got everything right four centuries early is its own way of not reading him.
  • Translation losses. His French is long, winding and spoken; every version sands something off. John Florio's gorgeously Elizabethan English of 1603 was read by Shakespeare — Gonzalo's speech about his ideal commonwealth in The Tempest is lifted almost directly from Florio's "Of the Cannibals." Which means the Montaigne you read is already not the Montaigne Shakespeare read.

Ten Sentences

1. Essai means trial — these are not treatises but sea trials of a judgment across many subjects, with no guarantee of arrival, and that lack of guarantee is the form itself.

2. His motto is a question, not a claim: "What do I know?" — "I know nothing" would eat itself, so the only honest posture is judgment held at the balance point.

3. Doubt leads not to paralysis but to tolerance: if I may be wrong, I have no standing to burn you for thinking otherwise — in a country killing over doctrine, that is a political position.

4. "Reader, I am myself the matter of my book" — he moved the "I" from the place one speaks from to the thing under study, which is the birth certificate of the modern essay.

5. The bet underwriting that move: "every man bears the whole form of the human condition" — so the backsliding and awkwardness you think are uniquely yours are the species spec, not your defect.

6. "I do not portray being; I portray passing" — so he adds and almost never deletes, leaving forty-year-old judgment beside its fifty-five-year-old rebuttal: recanting isn't a scandal, it's evidence.

7. "We are all patchwork" — the distance between us and ourselves often exceeds the distance between us and other people; consistent character is mostly narrative, not fact.

8. "Every man calls barbarism whatever is not his own practice" — the cannibals are not admired, they are a mirror held up to France: roasting a living man is worse than eating a dead one.

9. Young, he wrote that to philosophize is to learn to die; after nearly dying under a horse he took it back — don't rehearse, Nature will teach you on the spot — and kept both chapters in the book.

10. "The mayor and Montaigne have always been two": keep a back shop nobody may enter while the shopfront trades in full — you can be excellent at the job without pledging yourself as collateral.
