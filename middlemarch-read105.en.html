Middlemarch

Middlemarch: A Study of Provincial Life · George Eliot · 1871–1872

中文 →

In One Sentence

In a Midlands market town, a handful of ambitious young people — a girl who wants to live a great life, a doctor who wants to reform medicine — are worn down one by one by marriage, money, gossip and their own blind spots; Eliot neither mocks them nor excuses them, but pulls the camera back until you can see that everyone is caught in the same web. Her point: most noble longings never become history, but whether people can see that others are as much the centre of the world as they are decides whether the web drags everyone down or holds them up.

Where It Sits

George Eliot was the pen name of Mary Ann Evans (1819–1880), who translated Strauss and Feuerbach — the German critics who took Christian theology apart — and lost her faith without losing an ounce of moral seriousness. She wanted the novel to do the work the church had done: make people better at understanding one another. Middlemarch came out in eight parts, set in 1829–1832 around the Great Reform Act (the bill that widened the vote), and is regularly named the greatest novel in English; Virginia Woolf called it one of the few English novels written for grown-up people.

The Core Argument

  • Ardour needs an age and a channel worthy of it; without one, the hottest heart ends up trickling into ditches. Dorothea wants to be a Saint Theresa, but provincial England in the 1830s had no slot for a woman's "great work" — she fails not because she isn't good enough but because the medium doesn't exist.
  • We are all born in "moral stupidity", treating the world as an udder to feed our supreme selves. Almost none of the disasters in the book come from malice; they come from everyone seeing only from where they stand. The two people in each marriage are living in two stories, each invisible to the other.
  • Nobody lives alone; other people weave threads into your fate that you never see — which is why unrecorded goodness genuinely changes the world. The book's centre of gravity is its last page: the growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts.

The Key Concepts, One by One

1. "Later-born Theresas": ardour with no medium

The novel opens not with the story but with a Prelude about Saint Teresa of Ávila (the sixteenth-century Spanish nun who reformed the Carmelite order and wrote classic mystical works). As a child of seven she walked out hand in hand with her little brother to seek martyrdom among the Moors — a small girl already demanding an "epic life" worthy of her soul. Teresa found one: religious reform gave her a cause big enough to pour everything into.

Then Eliot says that many Theresas have been born since, with the same passion but no "coherent social faith and order" to receive it — so they became, in her phrase, the foundress of nothing, their ardour scattered among obstacles too petty to deserve it.

Dorothea Brooke is one of these later-born Theresas. Not yet twenty, rich and beautiful, what she actually wants is to build better cottages for tenant farmers, understand theology, live a life that means something. In her world, marriage is the only road by which a woman can reach greatness — so she marries the Reverend Edward Casaubon, a scholar well past forty-five, believing that wedding a man at work on The Key to All Mythologies (a magnum opus meant to trace every myth on earth to a single source) means wedding a great cause. She imagines herself reading and copying for him, like Milton's daughters.

An analogy: a born physicist delivered into a century with no universities or laboratories doesn't lose the gift — it just turns into "that odd one in the village who can't let things go." Talent is the seed, the age is the soil; the same seed in different ground grows into something else entirely.

Why it matters: it refuses two cheap explanations. One says "she failed because she was naive" — the book does let Dorothea blunder, but it insists the blunder grows out of how little knowledge and choice were available to her. The other says "society did it to her" — the book doesn't make her a pure victim either; her own idealising ardour is answerable too. Eliot's view braids the two: however strong a person's inner life, it is largely shaped by what lies outside it. The Finale says it outright: there is no creature whose inward being is so strong that it is not greatly determined by what lies outside it.

How it changes the way you see: next time you meet someone who "could have done so much more" — the brilliant relative stuck in a small town, the classmate who gave up research to support a family — you'll ask a different question. Not "why didn't they make it?" but "was there any channel for their ardour to flow through?" A great many "failures" are not failures of a person; they are the absence of a channel.

2. Moral stupidity and the scratched pier-glass: everyone is the centre of their own circle

In Chapter 21 Eliot delivers the book's sharpest judgement: we are all of us born in moral stupidity, taking the world as an udder to feed our supreme selves. "Stupidity" here isn't low intelligence; it is a structural blindness. You look out through your own eyes, so the world arranges itself around you automatically.

In Chapter 27 she gives this mechanism a physical image — the famous pier-glass parable. A polished steel mirror or a sheet of burnished metal, rubbed by a housemaid, is covered in tiny scratches running every which way. Hold a lit candle up to it and the scratches seem to arrange themselves in fine concentric circles around the flame — an illusion created by the light, since only the scratches facing the candle catch it. The scratches, Eliot says, are events; the candle is the egoism of any person you like. She aims the image at Rosamond Vincy, the mayor's pretty daughter, who takes the arrival of the new doctor, Lydgate, as a plot Providence has written expressly for her.

What makes the parable so brutal is that everyone is holding a candle, and every set of circles is a real feeling and a false structure. Rosamond thinks Lydgate came for her; Lydgate thinks Rosamond is the docile, admiring wife his career needs. Each sees in the other exactly the circles they want, and a marriage gets built on two stories that never intersect.

The novel's most celebrated swerve is a revolt against that candle. Chapter 29 opens mid-way through Dorothea's disappointment, and the narrator abruptly breaks off: "But why always Dorothea? Was her point of view the only possible one with regard to this marriage?" The camera swings to Casaubon, whom the reader has begun to loathe. He has a hungry heart too. He has spent his life afraid of being found out as an inadequate scholar; he married a young, ardent wife expecting reverence, and finds instead that her bright eyes are quietly appraising him — to him her enthusiasm is not comfort but a verdict. Later his young cousin Will Ladislaw, an aimless artistic type, lets slip the truth: Casaubon doesn't read German, and the German scholars have already done what he is attempting. The great work was obsolete before it began.

Why it matters: this move leaves the book without pure villains. Even the cold Casaubon, who later adds a spiteful codicil to his will, is someone the reader is made to see from the inside. For Eliot, sympathy isn't a soft feeling but an epistemic discipline: what you see is always the pattern of scratches centred on you, and to see what's really there you have to keep carrying the candle over to someone else.

How it changes the way you see: the next time a relationship or a colleague is "obviously" in the wrong, bring out the pier-glass. How much of the pattern you see is the structure of the events, and how much is just where your candle happens to stand? That doesn't abolish right and wrong; it means admitting, before you judge, that the other person has a candle too.

3. The web: no one is an island, and fates are woven where you can't see

In Chapter 15 Eliot describes her job as unravelling certain human lots and seeing how they were woven and interwoven; she calls her subject "this particular web." The image is the novel's structural principle. There is no single hero. Four storylines — Dorothea and Casaubon, Lydgate and Rosamond, Fred Vincy and Mary Garth, the banker Bulstrode — seem to run separately, and underneath they are all tied together.

Follow the neatest chain. Old Featherstone, a rich, spiteful miser, wakes in the night and orders Mary Garth, who is sitting up with him, to burn one of his two wills. Mary refuses — she won't be suspected of tampering. He dies before morning. Because of that single honest "no", Fred Vincy, who might have inherited, gets nothing, and the estate goes to Featherstone's illegitimate son Joshua Rigg. Rigg has a stepfather, Raffles, a drunken scoundrel, who turns up at the estate to cadge money, picks up a scrap of paper signed by Bulstrode — and happens to know the pious banker's buried past. So: Featherstone's malice → Mary's integrity → a scoundrel's chance visit → the most powerful man in town is ruined → and Lydgate, who borrowed a thousand pounds from him, is ruined alongside.

An analogy: a moral butterfly effect. Every choice is a thread that runs through the lives of people you will never meet.

Why it matters: the web makes Eliot's idea of personal responsibility grown-up and complicated. On one side, your fate is never wholly your own — half of Lydgate's fall comes from other people's pasts he could not have known about. On the other, every act travels further than you think, so small kindnesses and small cruelties can both be amplified. The subtitle, "A Study of Provincial Life," uses the language of the era's science on purpose: she watches a town the way a naturalist watches an ecosystem.

How it changes the way you see: when you ask how someone "ended up like that," the web tells you to look for the threads that were never in their hands. And when you make a choice that seems to matter to no one, it reminds you: you don't know whose cloth this thread is going to be woven into.

4. "Spots of commonness": ideals aren't knocked down, they're worn away

If Dorothea is ardour without a channel, Lydgate is the more painful case: he had the channel, the talent and the plan, and he still lost. He arrives at twenty-seven, trained in the newest medicine in Paris, with two ambitions. In practice, to reform backward country doctoring — diagnosing scientifically instead of earning his living by selling drugs. In research, to find the "primitive tissue" (the common basis he imagined underlying every tissue in the body, a live question in the biology of the day).

Eliot says he has certain "spots of commonness": a scientific idealist who carries the most ordinary prejudices of his class into the rest of his life. He assumes furniture, dinner services and wives should simply be tasteful and pretty, and that such "small things" are not worth the attention of his fine mind. So he marries Rosamond, because she is lovely, graceful and appears pliant.

What follows is not a dramatic fall but a chain of small compromises. The household overspends; debts pile up. Rosamond writes behind his back to a rich relative and quietly countermands his plan to give up their house. To keep his patron Bulstrode happy, he casts a vote on the hospital board against his own conscience. And to clear his debts, he accepts a thousand pounds from Bulstrode — at precisely the moment one of Bulstrode's patients dies under suspicious circumstances. Every step has its reasons; no step is that bad; added up, they trade a man's ideals away an inch at a time.

The ending is chillingly calm. He becomes a successful doctor, alternating between London and a fashionable spa, and writes a treatise on gout — a disease, Eliot remarks, with a good deal of wealth on its side. He dies at fifty, always regarding himself as a failure, because he had not done what he once meant to do. He once called Rosamond his basil plant — an allusion to Keats's poem "Isabella, or the Pot of Basil," in which Isabella buries her murdered lover's head in a pot of basil that then flourishes wonderfully. Lydgate's meaning: she flourished on a murdered man's brains.

Why it matters: most stories of ruined ideals find a villain or a single catastrophe. Eliot refuses the shortcut. What usually devours an ideal isn't an enemy but the part of life you never took seriously — the "small things" you handle by habit and assumption: whom to marry, how big a house, whether to go along with the room. They quietly set your cost structure, and one day they push the big work into a corner.

How it changes the way you see: it is a warning to anyone with ambitions. Your ideals depend not only on how clever you are in your own field but on how careless you are in the fields you don't care about. Check for your spots of commonness: the decisions you never really thought about, only copied.

5. Bulstrode: hypocrisy isn't acting, it's a story you tell yourself

Bulstrode the banker is the most devout man in town and the most eager to police other people's morals. His secret: as a young man he made his fortune with a London pawnbroker whose trade was largely in stolen goods. He then married the pawnbroker's widow — whose runaway daughter had been traced — and he concealed the fact, keeping for himself the fortune that should have gone to her. (That daughter was Will Ladislaw's mother.)

When Raffles comes back to blackmail him and then falls ill at his house, Lydgate gives strict orders: no alcohol, and opium only in carefully limited doses. Bulstrode first "forgets" to pass on the limit; then, in the night, when the housekeeper asks whether the patient might have a little brandy, he hands her the key to the wine cellar. Raffles is dead by morning.

This is the novel's deepest piece of psychology. Eliot is explicit that he was not a coarse hypocrite: he truly believed — only his belief had long since been trained into a machine for justifying his desires. He explains his wealth as Providence and his concealment as putting money to more godly use. He never needs to tell himself one big lie; he only needs, at each decisive moment, to pick the interpretation that suits him.

An analogy: what psychologists now call motivated reasoning — we don't reason first and conclude afterwards; we start with the conclusion we want and go looking for reasons. The horror of Bulstrode isn't that he is wicked but that his reasoning works perfectly the whole way through, aimed in a direction his wants have already chosen.

Why it matters: even he isn't left a monster. After his disgrace his wife Harriet — an ordinary, rather status-proud woman — learns everything, silently changes out of her finery into a plain dress, goes into his room and says, "Look up, Nicholas," and takes up the shame with him. Eliot gives the book's highest moral moment to an utterly unremarkable person, and spends it on a sinner.

How it changes the way you see: it gives you a better model of good people doing bad things, and a sharper suspicion of yourself. The danger isn't that we lie; it's that we are superbly good at choosing, in each concrete moment, the reading that lets us feel all right. People who habitually explain their interests in terms of grand principles are often the last to see through themselves.

6. "The roar on the other side of silence" and "unhistoric acts": sympathy as action

In Chapter 20, as Dorothea weeps alone on her honeymoon in Rome, the narrator writes the book's most beautiful passage: if we had a keen vision and feeling of all ordinary human life, it would be like hearing the grass grow and the squirrel's heart beat, and we should die of that roar which lies on the other side of silence. As it is, the quickest of us walk about well wadded with stupidity.

The point: every person's suffering is really happening and really deafening; we don't hear it because we can't afford to. Sympathy has a ceiling, but its direction can be trained — toward hearing a little more of that silence.

The book's moral climax is exactly that kind of hearing a little more. In Chapter 80 Dorothea, believing that Will, whom she loves, is entangled with Rosamond, weeps through the night. At dawn she opens her curtains and sees a man with a bundle on his back and a woman carrying her baby on the road, a shepherd and his dog further off — and she feels the largeness of the world and the manifold wakings of men to labour and endurance. She is one life among countless others and cannot hide inside her own pain. Then she does something very hard: she goes back to Rosamond, to speak up for Lydgate, now caught in scandal, and to help the couple mend things. Faced with that unselfish goodwill, Rosamond, who has only ever lived for herself, is moved for the first time — and tells the truth: Will has never loved anyone but Dorothea.

This leads to the famous last paragraph: Dorothea's full nature, like a river whose strength is broken into channels, spent itself in channels which had no great name on the earth; but the effect of her being on those around her was incalculably diffusive — for the growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts; and that things are not so ill with you and me as they might have been, is half owing to the number who lived faithfully a hidden life, and rest in unvisited tombs.

Its counterpart is the book's plainest good man, the land agent Caleb Garth, who treats "business" (meaning doing real work well) as something close to sacred, and gives up income rather than keep working for Bulstrode. To Eliot, draining a field properly or keeping honest accounts adds order to the world just as much as a theological treatise does.

Why it matters: the Prelude says these women founded nothing; the Finale says their goodness spreads beyond reckoning. The whole novel is the road from the first despair to the second consolation. It is not "ordinary is fine" pablum — it is unsparing about the possibilities Dorothea lost.

How it changes the way you see: it gives a more honest answer to "nobody notices what I do." Most of what actually holds the world up was never going to be recorded; whether something is worth doing doesn't have to depend on whether it leaves your name behind.

The Skeleton

Squeeze eight hundred pages into one line and you get three steps:

① The starting point: ardour out of step with its age. The Prelude's Theresa sets the question: what happens to a soul hungry for greatness born into an age with no room for it? Dorothea and Lydgate are two answers — one with no channel, one with a channel swallowed by the details of living.

② The mechanism: the web and the candle. The way they fail exposes two laws: each of us sees only the scratches circling our own candle (moral stupidity), and each of us has our fate woven by other people's threads (the web). Marriage is the lab where the two laws are most concentrated — Casaubon and Dorothea, Lydgate and Rosamond are each two egocentric stories colliding. Bulstrode shows egoism at its extreme: a man spending his whole faith on defending himself.

③ The way out: sympathy and unhistoric goodness. The only remedy the book offers is moving the candle toward someone else — Dorothea stepping out of herself on her worst morning, Harriet bowing her head beside a sinner, Caleb doing an honest job well. None of it enters history, but these are the knots that keep the web from strangling people.

What it establishes: our lot is shaped jointly by what is inside and what is outside us; greatness is often out of reach, but seeing other people is always within reach — and that, not greatness, is where the world's growing good actually comes from. It makes the case not by argument but by an almost scientific patience: laying out the lives of a dozen people in one town so they light each other up, until the reader sees the web for themselves.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

  • Misreading 1: "It's a romance about unhappy marriages." Marriage is the spine, but it's used as a microscope slide: Eliot cares less about who loves whom than about how two egos misread each other. And the book portrays a whole society — electoral reform, railways, cholera, the professionalising of medicine — with marriage as only its most intimate layer.
  • Misreading 2: "The moral is: settle for ordinary." No. The ending openly mourns the waste of Dorothea's gifts and blames an "imperfect social state." It consoles the ordinary without glorifying the society that kept them ordinary.
  • Criticism 1: Dorothea's ending disappoints. This is the most durable complaint. Many readers, feminist critics especially, point out that Eliot herself broke nearly every constraint — writing under a man's name, living openly with the married George Henry Lewes, becoming the most respected intellectual of her day — yet lets her heroine end as a good wife to the rather thin Will. Defenders answer that this is the book's honesty: Dorothea in the 1830s is not Eliot in the 1870s, and handing her an ending beyond her time would be the real lie.
  • Criticism 2: Will Ladislaw doesn't hold up. From Henry James's 1873 review onward, critics have found the man entrusted with the heroine's happiness weightless, almost an idealised shadow; James also called the book, roughly, a treasure-house of details but an indifferent whole, faulting its loose structure. F. R. Leavis (among the most influential English critics of the twentieth century), who crowned Eliot in The Great Tradition, still felt Dorothea carried the author's own self-idealising emotional needs (paraphrased).
  • Criticism 3: the narrator preaches. Eliot steps forward constantly to comment, judge and generalise, at odds with the later Flaubert-to-James ideal of the invisible author. Her defenders say the narrator is the method: the one who keeps carrying the candle to someone else, with the reader practising sympathy alongside.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① Many Theresas have been born who found no coherent social order to receive their ardour, and became the foundress of nothing. (paraphrased) Talent needs a channel, and failure where there is no channel isn't wholly a person's failure.

② We are all of us born in moral stupidity, taking the world as an udder to feed our supreme selves. Egoism isn't a vice so much as a factory setting — which is why resisting it is lifelong practice.

③ The scratches on the pier-glass run every which way; hold up a candle and they form circles around it. Part of the order you see is just where you are standing.

④ "But why always Dorothea?" Sympathy isn't softness but a discipline of truth: turn the camera on the person you least want to understand.

⑤ Eliot says she is studying "this particular web." No one's fate belongs entirely to them, and no one's acts touch only themselves.

⑥ Lydgate has his spots of commonness: an idealist in science, a copier of prejudice in life. What devours an ideal is usually not an enemy but the part of life you never took seriously.

⑦ Bulstrode is no coarse hypocrite; he simply chooses, every time, the interpretation that suits him. The most dangerous self-deception is reasoning that runs perfectly in a direction desire has already set.

⑧ If we could hear all ordinary human life, we should die of that roar which lies on the other side of silence. We have to be a little dull to survive — but we can choose to hear a little more of that silence.

⑨ There is no creature whose inward being is so strong that it is not greatly determined by what lies outside it. Before judging someone, count the threads that were never in their hands.

⑩ The growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts; that things are not so ill with you and me is half owing to those who lived faithfully a hidden life, and rest in unvisited tombs. Outside history's ledger there is another one, and it records every time you knocked on someone's door when they needed it most.
