DEEP READING · READ 64

Dream of the Red Chamber

also known as The Story of the Stone · Cao Xueqin · c. 1760

中文 →

In one sentence

A stone left over when the goddess repaired the sky is carried down into the world, born into a great aristocratic house at its peak, and made to watch a circle of gifted, feeling young women be married off, dismissed, sickened and driven to death one by one, while the house itself rots out from the inside and is finally raided — and the point is not that villains destroyed something good, but that the best things go anyway, usually not because anyone wrecked them, but because ordinary life, ordinary rules and ordinary time wear them away on schedule.

Coordinates

Cao Xueqin (c. 1715–1763) was born into a Chinese bondservant family attached to the Manchu imperial household. For three generations his family held the Imperial Textile Commissionership at Nanjing — a lucrative post supplying silk to the throne and quietly reporting back to it; the Kangxi emperor stayed at their residence on four of his six southern tours. In 1728 the family estate was confiscated, and Cao fell from that world into poverty on the western outskirts of Beijing, where a friend's poem describes the household living on rice gruel and drinking on credit (paraphrased). He died with the book unfinished. It is the summit of Chinese fiction and the only Chinese novel to have grown an entire academic discipline of its own ("Redology"): of the 120-chapter version read today, the first eighty chapters are securely Cao's, while the last forty were brought out by Cheng Weiyuan and Gao E in 1791–92 and remain contested.

The core claims

One: ruin needs no villain. There is nobody in this book you can point at and condemn. The raid on the garden is ordered by the lady of the house; the maid who drowns herself is destroyed by a moment's irritation; the maid who dies in disgrace is dismissed on gossip. What kills people here is the shared sense that this is simply how things are done — propriety, face, ledgers, seniority. That makes the novel harder to argue with than any protest literature: there is no target to overthrow.

Two: qing — feeling — is attention paid in full knowledge that it will change nothing. Jia Baoyu's one talent is that he extends the seriousness usually reserved for a lover evenly to maids, cousins, fallen blossoms and a crane. The book gives this a name, yiyin (discussed in section four below; it is not lust but lust's opposite). He saves nobody — and that is the argument: useless devotion has value even when it never once alters an outcome.

Three: the ending is published in advance, and the suspense moves elsewhere. Chapter five hands you the fates of a dozen girls in verse. Everything after that is not "what will happen" but "knowing it would happen, why could none of it be stopped." No other classical Chinese novel dares this.

The core ideas, one at a time

1. The stone that could not mend heaven: a book about not being chosen

It opens as myth. The goddess Nüwa smelts 36,501 stones to patch a hole in the sky. She uses 36,500. One is left over, abandoned at the foot of a mountain, where it laments day and night that it was found unfit. A monk and a Daoist shrink it into a piece of fine jade and carry it down "to taste for itself the sweet and the bitter." That jade is the piece Baoyu is born with in his mouth — hence the book's other title, The Story of the Stone: literally, the record inscribed on this rejected rock.

A verse cut into the stone sets the key for the whole novel: "No material to mend the azure sky — I wasted my years down in the red dust in vain" (paraphrased).

To see what this device is doing, you have to know what "mending heaven" means here: it is the highest form of useful public life — passing the examinations, holding office, carrying the family. Every adult in the book wants exactly that from Baoyu, and his father beats him nearly to death over it. And the stone has been graded, in the first pages, as the wrong size, surplus, unusable.

That self-placement does three jobs. It is the author's self-mockery — Cao was himself the stone that mended nothing, ruined and idle, with only a novel to show. It gives Baoyu's refusal to advance an origin rather than an excuse: he is not lazy, he is simply not that material and does not wish to be. And it suspends the criterion of usefulness altogether: if what turns out to be most valuable is precisely what the standard discarded, the standard is the thing in question.

One more irony sits in the detail. A stone is the commonest thing there is; only once it becomes jade do people prize it. The whole household fusses over that jade, calls it his lifeline — and nobody much attends to the boy. What they love is the jade, not him. Which is why, whenever he is wounded past bearing, Baoyu does the same thing: tears the jade off and hurls it at the floor.

How it changes the way you see: we price people, and ourselves, by usefulness. This book inverts that on page one. The part of you that the prevailing standard marks as surplus may be the only part that ever flowers. Not one of the 36,500 stones that mended the sky has a name. The one that produced this book is the leftover.

2. The Land of Illusion and the registers: the ending, given away in chapter five

Asleep after lunch, Baoyu is led by the fairy Disenchantment into a celestial precinct, the Land of Great Void Illusion. A couplet over its gate states the novel's thesis outright: "When the false is taken for true, the true becomes false; where nothing is treated as something, something turns to nothing."

Inside he finds albums — the registers of the Twelve Beauties of Jinling, main volume, supplementary volumes — each page a picture with four lines of verse. These are the panci: sentences already passed. He does not understand them and turns the page. Neither does the reader, but the reader remembers. Over the next eighty chapters they are redeemed one by one.

Then twelve songs are performed, titled A Dream of Red Mansions. The prologue names the book's business: it is "a dream performed in mourning for Gold and for Jade" (paraphrased) — Gold being Baochai, Jade being Daiyu. The tea Baoyu is served is called Thousand Red Flowers in One Cavern and the wine Ten Thousand Beauties in One Cup; both are puns, and what they actually say is a thousand red flowers weeping together, ten thousand beauties grieving as one. Even the refreshments are a forecast.

Why is this so powerful? It performs a deliberate transfer of suspense. An ordinary novel pulls you forward with "and then?" This one gives that up and bets everything elsewhere: because you already know they will die, every party, every poem, every crab-eating feast acquires a second light. When Daiyu buries fallen flowers and sings that when spring is spent and beauty fades, "flowers fall and the girl dies, and neither knows of the other" (paraphrased), it reads as sentiment in the moment and as a death sentence in hindsight. Nearly every poem, riddle and drinking game in the book is an epitaph its speaker has written for herself.

How it changes the way you see: knowing the ending does not cancel the weight of the middle; it magnifies it. All of us have read our own verdict — everyone knows they will die, and not one person concludes that this afternoon's sunlight is therefore not worth sitting in. The novel takes that condition, knowing and going on anyway, and makes it grave and beautiful.

3. Stone-and-flower versus gold-and-jade: not a love triangle, two moral worlds

Baoyu and Daiyu have a prior life. She was a crimson pearl flower on the bank of a spirit river, kept alive by a divine attendant — Baoyu's earlier form — who watered her daily with sweet dew. Having no way to repay him, she vowed that if he ever went down into the world she would follow, "and repay him with all the tears of one lifetime" (paraphrased). This is the debt of tears: their love is designed from the start to be completed by weeping until the tears run out, not by union. Daiyu does in fact cry her way through the book; her illness and her love are one thing.

Against that stands the gold-and-jade match: Baochai wears a golden locket whose inscription pairs, character for character, with the one on Baoyu's jade, and a monk has said she must marry someone who has jade. One bond is a promise from a former life; the other is a fit in this one.

Here is the point most often missed: Baochai is not a schemer and does nothing genuinely wicked in the entire novel. She is learned, steady, kind, the one who quietly cleans up other people's messes — the perfect candidate by every published standard. The song called A Life Misled settles the case:

All say the gold-and-jade match is the destined one; I think only of the vow made in a former life. Before me sits the crystalline snow of a saint in the mountains; and still I cannot forget that lonely fairy of the woods. Now at last I know what it means for a good thing to fall short: even with a marriage of perfect courtesy, something in the heart will not lie flat. (paraphrased)

"A marriage of perfect courtesy" translates an allusion to a Han-dynasty couple so respectful that the wife raised the meal tray level with her eyebrows when serving her husband — the classical shorthand for a model marriage. The line says: even when nothing whatever is wrong with the marriage, the heart still will not settle. It is the most exact sentence Chinese literature has produced about the difference between suitable and right.

So this is not a question of whom to choose but of two incommensurable values. Baochai stands for who you ought to become — worldly, measured, forever nudging you toward advancement. Daiyu stands for who you already are — sharp, oversensitive, badly timed, and the only person in the household who never once tells Baoyu to get on in the world. That is the plainest reason he loves her. Love, here, is defined as: someone who accepts the version of you that you refuse to trade in.

How it changes the way you see: much of adult unhappiness is not good versus bad but flawless versus irreplaceable. You can list a hundred reasons for the first and then spend a lifetime failing to make your heart lie flat. The book does not tell you how to choose. It writes that refusal so exactly that you have to admit it is real.

4. Yiyin and "feeling for the unfeeling": love that neither possesses nor collects

This is the term most damaged by literal translation. Disenchantment tells Baoyu he is "the most lustful creature of all time," then explains: what the world calls lust is skin-deep lust, greed measured in bodies; Baoyu's is yiyin — lust of the mind — which "can be grasped in the heart but not conveyed in words" (paraphrased).

Put in today's terms: yiyin is the capacity to direct the highest grade of seriousness, tenderness and aesthetic attention at another person, expecting no possession, no return, and without regard to their rank. Its opposite is not chastity. Its opposite is consumption — treating people as supply.

Watch it operate and the term stops being mystical:

  • He does for maids what maids are kept to do for him. When Ping'er is wrongly beaten, he fetches her powder and rouge and helps her dress, then goes off alone to cry — because nobody else will grieve that she was punished for a thing she never did.
  • He extends feeling to things that cannot return it. The commentator known as Red Inkstone — an early annotator close enough to Cao that the marginal notes are the best external evidence we have about the book — put it perfectly: Baoyu practises "feeling toward the unfeeling" (fallen petals, an old fan, a crane), while Daiyu practises "feeling toward feeling" and spends hers only on people. Two phrases, and the whole difference between them is said.
  • He treats girls as a higher order of being. His notorious line: "Girls are made of water; men are made of mud." Later he sharpens it (paraphrased): an unmarried girl is a priceless pearl; married, she turns into a dead pearl; grown old, she becomes a fish's eye. This sounds like a fetish for youth, but what he is describing is the process by which the world of men — office, calculation, filth — assimilates a person. He does not despise women; he despises the machine that grinds them into fish eyes.

Why does this amount to an ethic? Because in that household maids are property: beatable, sellable, dismissible. Yiyin is in practice a form of equal regard — Baoyu is the only master in the book who treats bondservants as people with complete inner lives. When Qingwen is thrown out and dies of the shame and the fever, he writes her a long formal elegy of the kind reserved for the noble dead. Using that register for a maid was close to a breach of decorum, and that is exactly the point.

But the weakness has to be said in the same breath, or this becomes flattery. He cannot protect a single one of them. The maid Jinchuan is slapped and expelled for joking with him and drowns herself in a well — and he runs. Qingwen is dismissed, and all he can manage is one stolen visit. His tenderness and his impotence are the same fact seen twice: inside that order, a man who refuses to be a man can weep but cannot rescue.

How it changes the way you see: we usually understand love as possession or as exchange — what I give, what you return. This book proposes a third thing: love as undivided attention that asks for nothing, and which, even when it saves no one, has already altered the quality of a few hours in the life of the person looked at. Qingwen, dying, cuts off her long fingernails and gives them to him. That is the whole argument.

5. Prospect Garden: a utopia scheduled for search from the day it was built

The family's eldest daughter, Yuanchun, is taken into the palace and made an imperial consort; the emperor grants her a visit home; and the family builds an enormous garden for it — Daguanyuan, the Grand View Garden. She stays one night. The garden that cost a fortune and was used for a single evening should have been shut up to gather dust, except that she issues an instruction: let the girls of the family, and Baoyu, move in.

What follows is the most famous stretch of exempt space in Chinese literature. The rules inside differ from those outside: the girls found poetry clubs, take literary sobriquets, rank Baoyu's verses politely last, roast venison in the snow, eat crabs, invent drinking games and give the visiting peasant grandmother a comic nickname. Within that wall, talent outranks birth and the rules of age and sex go temporarily slack.

The author never lets you forget it is on loan. The wall is held up by the money and rank of the system outside it — the garden was built for a consort, the food comes from rents on the family's farms, and the peasant visitor does the arithmetic on one crab banquet and observes that it would keep a farming family for a year (paraphrased). Any exemption financed from outside can be revoked from outside.

The revocation comes in chapter seventy-four, the night search of the garden: an embroidered pouch with an erotic picture is found on a path, and the lady of the house orders every maid's chest and bundle turned out after dark. That night divides the book. Afterwards the garden empties: Qingwen is expelled, Siqi driven out, the young actresses packed off, the timid cousin Yingchun married to a brute who kills her by degrees, and Baochai moves out before morning. The horror is not what the search turned up — it turned up almost nothing — but the establishment of the fact that this place can be searched. A sanctuary demonstrated to be revocable has already ceased to exist.

The hardest line in the novel is spoken that night by Tanchun. When they reach her rooms she lays out her maids' possessions herself, says search me first, slaps the woman leading the party, and then says (paraphrased): "A great house like this cannot be killed from outside in one blow. It has to start killing itself off from within — only then does it collapse completely."

How it changes the way you see: of any golden period — a rare team, a shared youth, a protected friendship — ask first: who is paying for the wall? If the shelter rests on conditions you do not control, its end requires no reason, only a pretext. One embroidered pouch is enough.

6. The Won-Done Song and the ledger of a dying house

In chapter one a ruined gentleman meets a lame Daoist singing what is known as the Hao Liao Ge — the Won-Done Song: "All men know that immortality is good, only fame they cannot forget. Where are the great ministers and generals of old? Grass has covered their graves" (paraphrased). Further verses take silver, wives and children in turn. The Daoist glosses the title: everything in this world, once it is won, is done; and only what is done can be called won. The listener understands on the spot and writes a commentary on the song that compresses the entire novel:

Bare rooms and empty halls, where once the court tablets crowded the bed; withered grass and dead willows, where once they sang and danced... In the clamour, one man finishes his song and the next steps up; taking another man's country for home. How absurd it all is — in the end you have only been sewing wedding clothes for somebody else. (paraphrased)

Read only that far and you would think this a devotional book about vanity. Its real distinction is that it never preaches the point; it audits it. The collapse of this house has line items:

  • Structural deficit, and widening. As early as chapter two an outsider sums the family up (paraphrased): the frame still stands, but the stuffing is long gone; mouths multiply, business multiplies, plenty of people enjoy the dignity and almost nobody does the planning, and the daily display is never reduced.
  • Income is fixed. In chapter fifty-three a farm bailiff arrives with the year's rents, ruined by weather, far short of what is expected — and the master's response is not to cut spending but to complain. A vast consumption engine whose revenue is a few estates and one harvest.
  • The imperial visit is the fatal entry. A garden built to host one night of glory empties the reserves. Somebody in the book says the silver was poured out like sea water (paraphrased).
  • Someone tries to reform it, and the plan is right. In chapter fifty-six Tanchun runs the household briefly and restructures it: the garden's flowers, bamboo and paddies are farmed out to individual servants who keep the profits and bear the losses, so the grounds are tended properly and several hundred taels a year are saved. She also finds duplicate line items — cosmetics drawn on the household account and bought again outside — and cancels them. The reform works, and it changes nothing. She is a concubine's daughter with borrowed authority; the moment the regular manager recovers and she is married away, everything reverts.

There is also a supernatural warning. In chapter thirteen a dead young wife appears to Wang Xifeng in a dream (paraphrased): the full moon wanes, full water spills, and the higher you climb the harder you fall; the family is at the moment of oil poured on fire, flowers heaped on brocade, and should be preparing for the day the tree falls and the monkeys scatter. Her advice is strikingly practical — while the money is still there, buy land and buildings near the ancestral graves and move the family school there, because sacrificial estates attached to graves are not confiscated when a household is stripped, so the ruined descendants would still have somewhere to go. Xifeng wakes up flattered by the visit and does nothing.

How it changes the way you see: "what rises must fall" is not mysticism; it is a sentence that can be broken out into a cash-flow statement. Institutions almost always die the same way: fixed revenue, rigid costs that swell with prestige, everyone aware and nobody both authorised and motivated to cut, and the one competent reformer on a temporary contract. By the day the confiscation order arrives, the thing has been insolvent for years.

The distilled skeleton

Compressed to a single line, the novel runs like this:

1. A useless beginning. A stone left over from mending the sky is taken down into the world because it grew curious about human life — guaranteed a season of splendour and then the sight of it dispersing. The frame's function is to give the whole book the vantage of hindsight: the reader always knows slightly more than the characters.

2. The verdicts fix the ending. Chapter five disposes of every major woman's fate, converting the suspense from outcome to process.

3. Building, and the peak. The consort, the garden, the visit: display at its maximum and reserves at their minimum. The rise and the ruin are the same act.

4. The garden years. The longest, loveliest and least eventful part of the book — poems, birthdays, crabs, snow, quarrels, reconciliations. Its job is to make you love these people, without which the losses to come would not be losses.

5. The fractures cluster. A maid in a well, a concubine swallowing gold, a cousin married to a brute, the night search, Qingwen dead, Siqi dead. Not one of these is a great event; every one is the result of somebody following the rules.

6. The tree falls, the monkeys scatter. The consort dies, the estate is confiscated, Daiyu dies, Baochai marries, Xifeng is broken, Tanchun goes to the frontier, the grandmother dies. Baoyu finally leaves the world — in Red Inkstone's phrase, "lets go at the cliff edge."

7. The stone is back on its mountain, with a book written on it. The novel is its deposition.

So what does it actually prove? That the destruction of a whole community can be completed without anyone doing evil. And how does it prove it? Not by argument, but by writing daily life at a density that cannot be disputed — the menu of a meal, the weight of a fabric, the cost of a funeral, the maids' monthly allowances, the girls' poems, all so specific that you cannot read them as allegory. When every detail is true, the ending those details add up to stops feeling like an author's decision and starts feeling like weather. That is why the last image of the twelve songs — "and all that is left is a great emptiness of white ground, wiped clean" (paraphrased) — takes the air out of you: you watched it come clean inch by inch, and you still cannot name the step at which it should have been stopped.

Misreadings, and the honest objections

Misreading 1: that it is a love triangle. Romance is one of its structures, not its subject. The subject is the disappearance of a whole group of people — a dozen principal women plus the maids, dozens of fates running in parallel, of which Baoyu and Daiyu are simply the most closely observed. Read as romance, two thirds of the book goes missing: the accounts, the marriage politics, the machinery of bondservice.

Misreading 2: that it is a manifesto against feudal ritual "devouring people." This reading hardened in a particular twentieth-century moment. It is not wrong so much as coarse. There is no villain to aim it at: the father's harshness comes from a father's sense of duty, the mother expels the maid to protect her son, and Xifeng's ruthlessness is what the machine requires of whoever manages it. The book depicts a cruelty with no cruel party — which is worse, and deeper, than an indictment.

Misreading 3: that Baochai is the antagonist and Baoyu's chief maid an informer. The text never returns that verdict. Baochai pays for a cousin's poetry party, redeems a poor relative's pawned clothes, and sends the sickly Daiyu bird's-nest broth. Her worldliness contains real kindness. Reading her as a schemer demotes the novel to palace intrigue. The author's actual proposition is colder: you can meet every standard perfectly and still end up as a golden hairpin buried in snow.

Misreading 4: that you must first work out who stands for whom. Both the allegorists (hunting political ciphers) and the strict autobiographers (reading it as the Cao family archive) can walk you into a ditch. It is fiction, and it says so in chapter one: the real events have been hidden away and the tale told in rustic invented language — and the two men whose names announce that pun appear on the first page. He told us he made it up; later readers insisted on a codebook.

Misreading 5: that "a great emptiness of white ground" is nihilism. It is the emptiness that arrives after the whole distance has been run, not instead of running it. If nothing was ever loved, the final emptiness is cheap. The weight here comes from the several hundred thousand words spent making you believe these people were worth loving before anything is taken away.

The strongest objections and the arguments still unsettled, given straight:

1. The last forty chapters: the great open question. Cao left eighty chapters, circulating in hand-copied manuscripts carrying Red Inkstone's commentary. In 1791–92 Cheng Weiyuan and Gao E printed a 120-chapter text, and that has been the standard version ever since. Against it: threads planted in the first eighty chapters go slack or resolve out of key, several fates do not square with the verdict-poems, the prose and the density of thought drop, and having Baoyu pass the examinations before renouncing the world looks like a face-saving ending the book had earned the right to refuse. For it: the death of Daiyu and the final renunciation are magnificently done, the 120-chapter text holds together as one work, and Gao E may have been an editor working from surviving drafts rather than an inventor — the novelist Kenneth Pai Hsien-yung is its best-known modern defender. The honest conclusion: we do not know how Cao meant to finish, and never will.

2. Redology became Cao-ology. In 1904 Wang Guowei read the novel through Schopenhauer's theory of tragedy — the first treatment of it as literature rather than as history. In 1921 Hu Shi's evidential study established the author and his family, an enormous contribution with an awkward side effect: vast energy moved from how the book works to how many properties the Cao family owned and who the real Baoyu was. Add the allegorical school, which read the whole thing as an anti-Manchu cipher, and a novel became an archive and a puzzle. The complaint is blunt: the more Redology flourished, the fewer people were reading the book.

3. The feminist double bind. The author says he wrote to preserve the memory of women whose conduct and understanding exceeded his own (paraphrased), and the women here are consistently more capable than the men — astonishing for the eighteenth century. The other half is equally true: the garden's poetry rests on an entire apparatus of bondservice. Qingwen, Jinchuan and Siqi are killed by that apparatus, and Baoyu's tenderness neither shifts nor is capable of shifting it. Sympathy and complicity are two things that live in the same character.

4. It is genuinely hard going. The volume of verse, lantern riddles, drinking games, opera citations, prescriptions and named fabrics is a real barrier for readers without classical training; the middle third moves very slowly; and the cast, plus the kinship terms, calls for a family tree. One line of criticism holds that much of the poetry is the author showing his range. In fairness, most of those poems are load-bearing prophecy — but "I could not get through it" is an honest report and there is no need to explain it away.

5. There is no settled text. The commented manuscripts differ among themselves and differ further from the printed edition; the marginal notes imply that material for the later chapters once existed and was lost. We are discussing a book that has no final version and was never finished. Which has become part of it: a novel about the impossibility of completion did not manage to complete itself.

Ten sentences

1. "No material to mend the azure sky." The book begins with a rejected stone: what the prevailing standard files as surplus is often the only part of you that ever flowers.

2. "When the false is taken for true, the true becomes false." The couplet over the gate of the Land of Illusion is also the method: true and false, something and nothing, are not two kinds of thing but one thing named twice from different positions.

3. The ending is handed over in chapter five. What follows is not "what will happen" but "knowing it would, why could none of it be stopped" — foreknowledge does not lighten the middle, it puts a second light on every gathering.

4. "Even with a marriage of perfect courtesy, something in the heart will not lie flat." The hard choices are rarely good against bad; they are flawless against irreplaceable, and the rest of your life is spent digesting the difference.

5. "Girls are made of water; men are made of mud." What he loathes is not men but the machine that grinds clear people into fish eyes: office, calculation, respectability.

6. Yiyin is not lust; it is lust's opposite. Full seriousness and tenderness given away without possession, without return, without regard to rank — and even when it rescues no one, the person looked at that way has been changed.

7. "It has to start killing itself off from within — only then does it collapse completely." Tanchun, on the night of the search. Great houses are seldom destroyed by enemies; they come apart from inside. So do teams, firms and families.

8. "The full moon wanes, and the higher you climb the harder you fall." Read the dream-warning alongside the short rent harvest, the cost of the imperial visit and Tanchun's failed reform: decline is not mysticism, it is a cash-flow statement.

9. "Once won, it is done" — "in the end you have only been sewing wedding clothes for somebody else." The emptiness of the Won-Done Song is the emptiness that comes after the whole distance is run; love it all the way first, or the emptiness weighs nothing.

10. "A great emptiness of white ground, wiped clean." The final image contains no murderer: nobody was destroyed by anyone, everyone simply did things by the book, and then it was all clean — which is the deepest and least forgiving sentence in the novel.
