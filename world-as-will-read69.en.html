The World as Will and Representation

Die Welt als Wille und Vorstellung · Arthur Schopenhauer · 1818

中文 →

In one sentence

The solid world in front of you is an image your brain has stamped out using three moulds — time, space, causality — and what lies behind it is something you have known all along, because you touch it from the inside every time you lift an arm: a blind, groundless, aimless, never-satisfied craving that Schopenhauer calls will. Once you grant that this is what reality is made of, suffering stops being bad luck and becomes structural; and happiness turns out to be nothing but the short blank left when the craving lets go.

Where it sits

Schopenhauer (1788–1860) picks up where Kant left off and then turns Kant's conclusion inside out. Kant said the thing-in-itself is unknowable; Schopenhauer said there is one back door, and what you find through it is not reason, not God, but blind craving. The book went to press in December 1818 (the title page says 1819). He was thirty, convinced he had solved philosophy entire — and almost nobody bought it; most of the print run was pulped. Lecturing in Berlin, he deliberately scheduled his course against Hegel's and lectured to an empty room. Fame arrived only with the far lighter essay collection Parerga and Paralipomena in 1851, when he was sixty-three. Its place in intellectual history is peculiar: this is the first complete Western system to argue — not merely grumble — that the ground floor of the universe is irrational, and the first to take the Upanishads and Buddhism seriously as philosophical partners rather than exotica.

The core claims

Three claims, each derived from the one before:

  • The world has exactly two aspects: world as representation, and world as will. Not two worlds — one world seen from two sides: from outside it is representation, from inside it is will.
  • That inside is a blind will-to-live (Wille zum Leben): groundless, purposeless, never satisfied. Everything that exists is this one will objectified at some grade — gravity, crystallisation, a plant turning toward light, ambition, love.
  • Therefore suffering is structural, not accidental. The exit is not to get more (that only restarts the craving) but to suspend the will (art) or to turn it against itself (compassion, renunciation).

The key ideas, one by one

"The world is my representation" — not solipsism, but an inventory of what you can possibly know

The book opens with the sentence: "The world is my representation." Representation (Vorstellung) literally means "what is placed before you" — anything that can be an object of consciousness: a table, a galaxy, your own hand. Every representation, Schopenhauer says, necessarily has two halves, an object known and a subject knowing, and neither can exist without the other: no object without a subject, no subject without an object.

He takes this from Kant, but he pares it down brutally. Kant said we cannot know the thing-in-itself (Ding an sich) — what a thing is independently of our perceiving it — because all experience must pass through the forms of the mind. Schopenhauer cuts Kant's twelve categories down to three items: time, space, causality. These are not properties of the world but functions of an organ. He says it flatly: intellect is a physiological function of the brain the way digestion is a function of the stomach. The "objective world," then, is what that organ stamps out.

This is the point most often misread, so let it be nailed down: "the world is my representation" does not mean the world is your private dream, and it does not mean the table isn't there. It means that everything you can know has already been processed by the conditions of knowing, and that asking "what did it look like before processing" gets no answer at this level. His image: it is like walking round and round a castle looking for the entrance, sketching every wall — until you discover you have been living inside it all along.

The other half of the wall is his doctoral dissertation of 1813, On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason, which the book demands you read first. The principle of sufficient reason says that nothing is without a ground for being as it is. Schopenhauer's point is that this principle has four distinct forms, and confusing them makes a mess: ① the ground of becoming — causality (heat, therefore boiling); ② the ground of knowing — logic (premises true, therefore conclusion true); ③ the ground of being — position in space and time (a triangle's angles sum to 180° not because something caused it but because space is built that way); ④ the ground of acting — motivation, which is causality seen from within. These four "whys" are not the same question.

Why it matters: the principle fences off the territory where explanation works at all. It holds only inside the world as representation, because it just is the way the intellect operates. So questions like "why does the world as a whole exist?" or "why does the will want anything?" are not hard problems for him — they are malformed: you have taken a coordinate query that only makes sense inside the map and aimed it at the map itself. This is why he can say without embarrassment that the will is groundless: not that we have yet to find its reason, but that "reason" does not apply to it.

The body is the back door: what the will actually is (and why it is not willpower)

If everything is representation, how can the book go anywhere? The whole system hangs on a single hinge: in the entire universe there is exactly one object you do not merely observe from outside — you are it. Your own body.

Your body is given to you twice. Once as representation: a lump with weight that bleeds, that others can see and dissect. And once from within: when you raise your arm, there is not first a mental act of willing that then strikes the muscle like one billiard ball hitting another — the act of will and the movement of the body are one and the same thing, apprehended in two different ways. Hence his formula: the body is the will made visible. Teeth, gullet and gut are hunger given shape; the genitals are sexual desire given shape.

Then comes the boldest and most-attacked move in the book. If in my own case the other side of representation turns out to be will, and if I have no reason to think I am the single exception in the universe, then the inner nature of everything is that same will, appearing at different grades: as gravity in the stone, as the north-seeking of a magnet, as the directed growth of a crystal, as phototropism in a plant, as instinct in an animal — and only in the human being does it finally acquire a lamp called intellect.

Strip the everyday sense off the word. Will here is not willpower, not self-control, not even conscious wanting. It is a knowledge-free, goal-free, endless urge — in his phrase, a blind, unstoppable striving. It does not crave in order to anything; craving is what it is. Intellect is a searchlight it grew later, for finding food and mates, not its master. This is the first time in the history of philosophy that unconscious drive is placed ahead of reason. Freud acknowledged more than once that the picture of instinct as primary and consciousness as a thin crust had been drawn by Schopenhauer before him.

The metaphysics of sexual love: you think you are in love — it's the unborn child

The chapter "The Metaphysics of Sexual Love," added to the second edition of 1844, is the sharpest instance of the will using an individual for its own business. Schopenhauer asks: why does an affair that any bystander can see is substitutable feel, to the person inside it, like the end of the world — enough to make them act flatly against their own interests?

His answer: the passion is not yours at all. It is the will-to-live of the next generation, using your body to select its other half. You believe you are calculating your own happiness; what is actually under scrutiny is what kind of child this particular pairing would produce — which is why people are so reliably drawn to whoever compensates for their own deficiencies (the short prefer the tall, the frantic prefer the calm). And once the purpose is served, the halo evaporates and two rather unfamiliar people are left staring at each other: because the thing that bound them was never their affair in the first place.

Why it matters: it is a complete demonstration of will operating through intellect. Your judgment, your reasons, your certainty that it simply had to be this person — all of that is dubbed in afterwards; the order was placed by a blind urge. The chapter fed directly into Freud's theory of libido and is the ancestor of every evolutionary-psychology explanation since. The price is that Schopenhauer states it with real coldness, and the book carries throughout the prejudices about women of his age and of himself — more on that below.

The principle of individuation and the veil of Maya: "you" and "I" are a surface effect

Since the will lies outside time and space, it cannot be divided or counted — plurality is itself a product of time and space. Borrowing a scholastic term, Schopenhauer calls space and time the principium individuationis: it is what slices one will into countless apparently separate individuals. Borrowing from Indian thought, he calls that slicing the veil of Maya — which does not deceive you into thinking the world is unreal, but into thinking the world consists of many mutually indifferent things, and that you are one of them.

Two startling consequences follow. First, about death: what dies is this individual at the level of appearance, not the will, which was never in time and so has no birth or death to undergo. Fearing death, he says, is like the setting sun lamenting that it is falling into eternal night — only this side has turned away. Second, about morality: if you and I are one will at bottom, then the torturer and the tortured are the same thing — the world is a beast biting its own tail. Here he quotes the Upanishadic formula tat tvam asi, "that art thou": the one you harm is you. His entire ethics grows out of this crack in the metaphysics.

Happiness is negative: the pendulum, lack, and boredom

This is his most famous stretch and the most often misread as mere gloom-mongering. His pessimism is not a mood; it is derived from the steps above. If the thing-in-itself is insatiable craving, then:

Every act of willing arises from a lack, and therefore from pain. You want something precisely because you do not have it and the not-having is chafing. Satisfaction merely removes the chafing stone, so satisfaction is always negative — the temporary absence of a pain, with no positive content of its own. Pain is the positive thing, the thing directly felt. The evidence is everywhere: you cannot feel your nine healthy organs, only the one aching tooth. Happiness is not an item you can obtain; it is a blank left over after pain has been subtracted.

Worse follows. Once the craving is satisfied, the blank does not sit still. It becomes boredom (Langeweile): with nothing to push against, the will grinds on itself. Hence the famous image — life swings like a pendulum between pain and boredom (paraphrased). The poor are tormented by want, the rich by emptiness; holidays are peaks for suicide and violence precisely because, with the external goad removed, people must face the machine idling. On this basis he mocks Leibniz's "best of all possible worlds": on the contrary, this is roughly the worst world that could still manage to exist — make it any worse and it would fall apart (paraphrased). He also offers a crude but hard-to-dismiss piece of arithmetic: weigh the suffering of the animal being eaten against the pleasure of the animal eating it, and tell me the books balance.

Why it matters: it converts "why am I still not happy" from a personal failure into a structural fact. That familiar flatness after the new job, the raise, the move is not ingratitude; satisfaction has no positive content by definition. Seeing this changes how you manage expectation: rather than adding more "once I have X" clauses, subtract pain and budget in advance for boredom — which is exactly the metaphysical source of the practical advice in his late Wisdom of Life.

Ideas and art: why staring at a landscape suddenly stops the ache

If pain comes from craving, then stopping the craving stops the pain. And Schopenhauer points out that everyone has already experienced this without naming it: it is called aesthetic experience.

The mechanism: normally you look at everything through the principle of sufficient reason — what is it, what is it for, does it help or hurt me — which is the will using the intellect to hunt. But occasionally something takes you over completely: a sea at dusk, a melody, a painting. For that moment you forget its use and forget your own situation; you stop being a person with objectives and become what he calls the pure will-less subject of knowing. Let the machinery of craving idle for even a moment and the pain that came from lack loses its power supply — not an anaesthetic but a genuine shutdown.

And what do you see in that state? Not this particular tree, but an Idea in Plato's sense, borrowed: the eternal pattern of the will at a given grade — treeness itself. Individual trees come and go in time; the Idea does not. Genius, accordingly, is defined not as cleverness but as the capacity to sustain this pure contemplation; his well-known distinction (paraphrased): talent hits a target no one else can hit; genius hits a target no one else can see.

From this he ranks the arts — not by quality but by which layer of the will each lets you see:

The hierarchy of the arts (Book Three): further down, closer to the will. Music is not on the ladder at all — it gets its own rung.

That last rung is the book's most original judgment and its most influential. Every other art works through the Ideas as a medium — paint a tree and you paint treeness; music bypasses the world of representation altogether and is the will's own likeness. This explains something everyone has felt and nobody can state: why a melody can land precisely on longing, or defiance, or release, while pointing at no person and no event. Because it renders not one instance of longing but the shape of longing as such. Hence his much-quoted line (paraphrased): music is an unconscious exercise in metaphysics in which the mind does not know it is philosophising. Wagner read this chapter and adopted him as master.

Compassion: the only real basis of morality (and why you cannot change your character)

One premise first: he thinks moral philosophy should not tell you what you ought to do but explain why people do what they do. He openly ridicules Kant's categorical imperative — act only on a maxim you could will to be universal law — as a theological commandment with the vestments swapped for academic dress; "ought," he says, means something only where there are rewards and punishments.

So what actually drives people? He counts three basic incentives and only three: ① egoism — wanting one's own good, the background colour of nearly all behaviour, and he is unsparing: everyone would rather see the world destroyed than suffer a scratch to their own skin; ② malice — wanting another's harm as an end in itself, with nothing to gain, which is what evil really is; ③ compassion (Mitleid) — wanting another's good, the one genuinely moral motive.

What matters is that he grounds compassion not in feeling but in metaphysics. Compassion is possible because for that instant the veil of individuation tears: you do not imagine another's pain, you recognise yourself in it. "That art thou" is not an edifying line of verse; it is an ontological claim. Virtue then comes in two grades: justice is negative, "harm no one" (neminem laede); loving-kindness is positive, actively reducing another's suffering. It is also why he was the first major European philosopher to extend moral concern explicitly to animals — close to heresy at the time, and the reason animal ethicists still cite him.

A hard corollary comes with it: you cannot change your character. He distinguishes the empirical character — the stable pattern your actions reveal over a lifetime — from the intelligible character, what that pattern is outside time. Every particular act is strictly determined by character times motive; there is no slack. Hence the much-quoted line (paraphrased): a man can do what he wills, but he cannot will what he wills. Freedom lies not in your operari (your doing) but only in your esse (your being). Which means self-change never comes from resolve; it comes from a change in knowledge — all you can alter is what you see, and character will take a different road under different light. The salvation offered in the second half of the book runs along exactly that road.

Denial of the will, and the last word of the book

If suffering comes from the will, and the will is what you are, then there is only one real exit: the will turns and denies itself.

Two paths lead there. One is knowledge: a very few see through the principle of individuation, recognise the suffering of the whole world as their own, and the urge to live, to have, to continue simply loses its grip on them. The other is suffering, and it is the only path most people can actually walk: after life has run them over often enough, a person stops struggling — he calls this the purifying effect of suffering. The end state is resignation: voluntary poverty, chastity, no more procreation, no resentment even toward those who harmed you. His models are the Christian saint, the Indian ascetic and the Buddhist monk; he says outright that his conclusion is the same as theirs, arrived at by argument.

One correction of the standard misreading is essential here: suicide is not his exit; on the contrary, he holds it to be an affirmation of the will. The reasoning is clean: the suicide does not reject life, he wants it too much — he only refuses these particular terms. What he destroys is one appearance; the will is untouched. Denial of the will is not about killing a body but about extinguishing craving.

The book ends on an almost untranslatable note. For someone whose will has turned, what is left of this solid world of suns and galaxies? Schopenhauer's answer is his last word: nothing. But he adds a crucial qualification (paraphrased): it is nothing for us, who are still full of will; conversely, for those in whom the will has turned, our world with all its suns and galaxies is the nothing. He does not say what lies on the far side — he says that from this side you can see only a blank. That is not the triumph of nihilism; it is the point where the language of the will runs out.

The argument in outline

Four books: the same world walked around twice, two books per circuit.

  • Book One · Representation (1): the world as representation. Time, space and causality are forms of the brain; the principle of sufficient reason fences in what explanation can do. The epistemological floor — and a wall.
  • Book Two · Will (1): through the back door of one's own body, straight through that wall. The thing-in-itself is blind will-to-live, and everything is its objectification at some grade. The metaphysical heart.
  • Book Three · Representation (2): representation again, this time with the principle of sufficient reason set aside. What appears are the Ideas, and what you enter is the aesthetic — the suspension of the will, a rehearsal for salvation.
  • Book Four · Will (2): the will again, this time with self-knowledge in hand. Affirmation of the will means egoism and suffering; denial means compassion, resignation, and that final "nothing" — the turning of the will, salvation itself.
  • Appendix: a long critique of Kantian philosophy. He insists throughout that without Kant first, not a line of this book can be read.

In one chain: the world is my representation (I can only know it processed) → but on myself I have touched its other side, and it is will → the will is aimless and insatiable, so suffering is structural → therefore art suspends it, and compassion and renunciation turn it.

Misreadings and honest criticism

  • Misreading 1: "will" means willpower. Exactly backwards. His will is a blind urge without knowledge or purpose; the "strong will" of self-help is at most a thin surface manifestation of it. Read the title as a manual for getting what you want and you are already in the wrong building.
  • Misreading 2: pessimism means a low mood, or giving up. His pessimism is the conclusion of an argument, not a temperament — and it leads to an extraordinarily demanding asceticism (voluntary poverty, chastity, no children), not to lounging about. He admits almost nobody manages it. Including —
  • His own life contradicted his doctrine, and that should be said plainly. Schopenhauer ate well, invested shrewdly, went often to the theatre, played the flute daily, had a number of affairs, and was irascible enough to shove a woman neighbour down a staircase and be made to pay her for years afterwards. His defence, roughly: a moralist need not be a saint any more than a sculptor need be beautiful. You may accept that, or take it as a serious dent in an ethics of renunciation.
  • The gravest objection: does the extrapolation hold? Moving from "I experience my own body from within as will" to "the essence of the universe is will" rests on analogy, not proof. Worse, there is an internal tension: he stipulates that the thing-in-itself lies outside time and space, untouched by the forms of knowing — yet the inner sense in which we feel the will is given in time. Schopenhauer half concedes this, saying our knowledge of the will is the most immediate we have but still not wholly exhaustive. Which is to say the back door he hands us opens only a crack. Most later Kantians conclude that the moment you say the thing-in-itself is anything, you have broken its definition.
  • Nietzsche's reversal (his most important heir and gravedigger). Nietzsche found the book by chance in a Leipzig second-hand shop and took it as revelation, then inverted its conclusion entirely: granting that the world is aimless striving, he says affirm it — will to power, eternal recurrence, amor fati. He charged Schopenhauer's renunciation with decadence: exhaustion dressed up as wisdom.
  • His writing on women. The judgments in the essay "On Women" — that women are deficient in reason, fit only for nurture, naturally inclined to dissimulation — were contemptible even by the standards of his time, and they are not a detachable footnote: they connect directly to the "individual as instrument of the species" argument in the metaphysics of sexual love. Reading him today, this part should not be smoothed over.
  • Did he read Indian thought accurately? His Upanishads came through a second-hand Latin translation of a Persian version (the Oupnek'hat), and his Buddhism from the scattered European reports then available. He reads nirvana as denial, as nothingness, which sits some distance from Buddhism's own middle way — a number of Buddhist scholars think he is really doing his own metaphysics in Indian vocabulary. In fairness, though: he was the first major Western philosopher to treat Eastern thought as an equal interlocutor rather than a curiosity.
  • What survives? Almost nobody accepts the system whole. But the diagnosis survives and has seeped everywhere: unconscious drive before reason (Freud); the limits of language and the unsayability of ethics (the young Wittgenstein's bible was this book); art as temporary release (Wagner, Tolstoy, Thomas Mann, Proust); and a pessimist line running from Beckett to today's antinatalism. Schopenhauer's answer was not adopted, but nobody has managed to walk around his question.

Ten sentences

1. "The world is my representation" — everything you can know has already been stamped by three internal moulds, time, space and causality; asking what it looked like before the stamping gets no answer at this level.

2. But there is one exception: your own body. It is both an object you observe from outside and the thing you directly are from inside — the only back door to the thing-in-itself.

3. What you find through that door is will: a blind urge without knowledge, ground or purpose, never satisfied. Not willpower — intellect is only a lamp it lit later, to find food.

4. Time and space are the principle of individuation; they slice one will into countless individuals. So the difference between "you" and "me" exists only at the level of appearance, and torturer and tortured are the same thing.

5. All willing arises from lack, that is, from pain; satisfaction only removes the pain, so happiness is always negative — a blank left by subtraction, never an item you can hold.

6. Once satisfied, the will has nothing to push against and starts grinding on itself — hence life as a pendulum swinging between pain and boredom (paraphrased).

7. Aesthetic experience is the one rehearsal for salvation everybody has already had: absorbed by a landscape or a melody, you briefly become the pure will-less subject of knowing; craving idles, and the pain cuts out.

8. Other arts copy the Ideas; music is a direct copy of the will itself — which is why a melody lands exactly on longing while naming no one.

9. The only real basis of morality is not duty but compassion: in it the veil of individuation tears, and you do not imagine another's pain, you recognise yourself in it — "that art thou."

10. The exit is not suicide (which affirms life and merely rejects its terms) but the will turning against itself; at the end of that road this world of suns and galaxies leaves only the book's last word — nothing — to which he adds: nothing only for us, who are still full of will.
