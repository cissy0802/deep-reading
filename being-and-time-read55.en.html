Being and Time

Sein und Zeit · Martin Heidegger · 1927

中文 →

In One Sentence

You use the word "is" dozens of times a day—the cup is on the table, the meeting is at three, I'm fine—and nobody has ever asked what it means. Western philosophy spent two and a half millennia taking inventory of what there is (atoms, forms, God, consciousness) and skipped the question of what it is for anything to be at all. Heidegger reopens it, and there is only one door in: the being that does the asking—you. He calls it Dasein, and then spends the book showing that you are not a mind sealed inside a skull reaching out toward an external world; you are always already soaked in a world woven out of what-things-are-for, and most of the time you live as "one lives"—the way people do. What breaks the spell is not an argument but an experience nobody can have on your behalf: you are going to die, and it could be any moment. And the answer he arrives at is still sending out shockwaves: the horizon within which anything at all can be understood as being is time—and time is not a container you sit inside. Time is what you are.

Where It Sits

Heidegger (1889–1976) was born into a poor Catholic family in southern Germany and was meant for the priesthood; instead he became the student of Edmund Husserl, the founder of phenomenology, and in 1927 hurried this book into print to secure a chair. It is dedicated to Husserl—and it reroutes his teacher's method so completely that it became the watershed of twentieth-century continental philosophy. Existentialism (Sartre), hermeneutics (Gadamer), the ethics of the other (Levinas), the phenomenology of the body (Merleau-Ponty), deconstruction (Derrida), and the sharpest philosophical strike ever landed on symbolic AI (Dreyfus) all fork off from this one book. It is also among the century's hardest, and it is a torso: six divisions were planned, two were written. And its author's Nazi record leaves a splinter in every rereading (see the criticism section—this piece does not step around it).

The Central Claims

Four hundred pages circle three claims, each nested in the last:

  • Being has been forgotten—and forgotten under cover of the excuse that it is too ordinary to be worth asking about. Everything turns on separating Being (Sein) from beings (Seiendes: stones, numbers, customs, you)—a distinction later named the ontological difference: Being is not itself one of the beings, so no amount of searching the inventory will turn it up. Whenever metaphysics has pressed the question, it has reflexively produced some supreme being (the Forms, God, substance) as the answer, and the answer has crowded out the question.
  • The only way in is through the one who asks. Dasein is peculiar in being the only entity that has some understanding of its own being and is always concerned about it. So step one is not to build a system but to describe honestly how such an entity actually exists day to day—and the description yields this: the basic structure of a human being is being-in-the-world, not a subject facing objects. The entire Cartesian stage set of epistemology is a derivative that flaked off something more original.
  • The whole of Dasein's being is "care," and the meaning of care is temporality. The reason you can have a world at all, the reason anything can show up as being what it is, is that your kind of being is stretched: running ahead into your future, carrying your having-been, absorbed in your present. Welding "being" to "time" is the whole ambition of the book.

The Core Concepts, One at a Time

The question of Being: why the plainest word is the darkest

The book opens with a line from Plato's Sophist (paraphrased): you have long been aware of what you mean when you say "being"; we once thought we knew, but now we are perplexed. Heidegger adds (paraphrased): Do we today have an answer to the question of what we mean by the word "being"? Not at all. So the question must be raised again.

He first clears away three prejudices standing in the doorway. "Most universal" is not the same as clear—precisely because it covers everything, it is the vaguest. "Indefinable" only means it cannot be pinned down by genus-plus-difference (it is not a kind of thing); that is no argument against asking after its meaning. And "self-evident" is the most suspicious of all: everyone grasps the "is" in "it is raining," yet ask what that "is" amounts to and the room falls silent.

The stakes show up in four sentences: this stone exists; there's a meeting at three; the number 7 exists; I'm fine. The stone's being is sitting-there. The meeting's being is a convention—untouchable, yet able to walk twenty people into the same room at the same hour. The number's being neither occupies space nor changes. And the "am" in "I'm fine" points to no thing and no location. One word doing radically different work, and we slide over the difference without noticing—that is what he means by the forgottenness of Being. It is like asking a fish what water is: the fish has no shortage of experience of water; it is because it has spent its life submerged that it has no standpoint from which water could become a topic.

Methodologically he inherits Husserl's slogan—"to the things themselves": start not from inherited theory but from how things show themselves. But he adds a twist: phenomenology has to be hermeneutic (in the business of drawing out what is implicit). Being does not lie about waiting to be looked at like a table; it is the sort of thing that is always already understood and never yet said. This is also why the book reads so strangely: he is not arguing, he is describing—and coining words nonstop, because the available philosophical vocabulary has been marinated for centuries in the subject–object prejudice.

Dasein: its essence lies in existing

Why not just say "human being"? Because the word arrives carrying two thousand years of luggage—rational animal, creature of God, conscious subject—each of which has already settled what kind of thing we are. He wanted a blank name, so he took the flattest word in German: Dasein, literally Da (there) + sein (to be)—the place where being is held open. You are not a container of world-images; you are a lit clearing in which things can show up as the things they are.

Two sentences matter most. First (§9, paraphrased): "The 'essence' of Dasein lies in its existence." A hammer's essence is settled before it is made; it only has to go on being a hammer. What Dasein is, by contrast, gets determined by how it goes about being. You do not have a nature that you then realize; you are a possibility that has yet to be. Hence he refuses the grammar of essence and property and says instead that Dasein has to be. (Existenz is reserved for Dasein alone: stones and bacteria do not "exist" in this sense—they are merely present-at-hand, lying there.)

Second: mineness (Jemeinigkeit)—Dasein's being is in each case mine. You can study average lifespans, cognitive biases, fertility rates; what you live is never an average, it is this one non-transferable stretch. Which is why death becomes the pivot of the whole book: everything else can be delegated.

Being-in-the-world: how the subject–object split gets cancelled

Here comes the book's hardest blow to modern philosophy. Since Descartes the standard stage set has been: a thinking I in here, an extended material world out there, and a chasm labelled "how do I know the outside is real" in between. Kant went so far as to call it the scandal of philosophy that no rigorous proof of the external world had ever been produced. Heidegger's reply is one of the great reversals in the history of the subject (§43, paraphrased): "The scandal is not that the proof is still outstanding, but that such proofs are expected and attempted again and again."

Why is no proof needed? Because being-in-the-world is one unbreakable structure, not "I" plus "world" bolted together—he hyphenates it into a single word precisely to stop you reading it as one thing sitting inside another. Water in a glass is insideness, the spatial nesting of two present-at-hand items. Dasein's "in" takes the archaic sense of the German in: to dwell, to reside, to be familiar with. When you say "I'm in London," you are never reporting coordinates; you are reporting a whole repertoire of things you know how to do, routes you recognize, ways you deal with people.

So "world" is not the sum of things either. The world is a web of significance, and its knots are made of "in-order-to." Chalk is for writing, writing for teaching, teaching for holding down this job—and the chain finally hooks onto some possible way for Dasein itself to be: for the sake of the person I am trying to be. This is why people whose marriage collapses or whose child dies say the world has ended. Nothing physical has gone missing; what has gone is the net that gave every item its point. The coffee machine is still on the counter—it is just no longer for anything.

Ready-to-hand and present-at-hand: the phenomenology of a hammer

The most famous passage in the book. Picture yourself driving a nail. While the work is going well, you are precisely not looking at the hammer: your attention is on the nail, on the wall you're repairing; the heft of the head and the grain of the handle have withdrawn, and the tool has become an extension of your arm. Heidegger's formulation (paraphrased): the more we seize hold of a piece of equipment and use it, the more primordially we encounter it—and in the using it must withdraw, precisely in order to be ready-to-hand. That mode of being is readiness-to-hand (Zuhandenheit).

Its counterpart is presence-at-hand (Vorhandenheit): the hammer as an object with mass and material, laid out before you for inspection. Science and traditional philosophy have always assumed presence-at-hand is what things really are, with usefulness painted on afterward. Heidegger reverses the order: presence-at-hand is derived from readiness-to-hand. Derived how? Through three kinds of breakdown: conspicuousness (the head flies off, and the hammer leaps into view for the first time), obtrusiveness (it's missing when you reach—nothing is more visible than the tool that isn't there), and obstinacy (the scaffolding blocks the doorway and forces itself on you as an obstruction).

Only in the moment of interruption does the normally invisible web of references light up whole—you suddenly see that hammer–nail–board–wall–house–"I want to live somewhere decent" is a single chain. That is the methodological core of the book: breakage is a phenomenological instrument. (The keyboard under your fingers is the live case: you cannot recite the letter layout, but your hands can—and the instant one key sticks, the whole board flips from transparent to object.)

Why does this matter so much? Because it drags theoretical knowing off its throne: our most original relation to the world is not knowing but knowing-how; not representation but skill. The blow carries all the way into engineering. In What Computers Can't Do (1972) the philosopher Hubert Dreyfus used exactly this to predict the failure of symbolic AI: you can hand a machine every property of a cup and still never finish writing down the background competence behind "when it is time to refill a guest's glass"—because that background does not exist as a list of facts inside anyone's head. It lives in trained bodies and shared practice.

The "they" and falling: who is living my life?

Dasein is never alone. Being-with (Mitsein) belongs to its very constitution rather than being inferred from an "I"—even solitude is possible only within a world of being-with: solitude is the absence of others, and absence is a way of being present.

But being-with has a default setting, and it is the book's most stinging concept: das Man, "the they." The German man is the subjectless "one" of "one doesn't do that," "people are all buying it." Heidegger writes (paraphrased): we take pleasure as one takes pleasure; we read and judge literature and art as one does; and we shrink back from the mass as one shrinks back. Note the venom in that last clause: even "I refuse to be like everyone else" is one of the they's own settings.

The they is not "other people," and it is nobody in particular. The definition is frighteningly exact (paraphrased): the they is not this one, not that one, not some people, not the sum of them all; it is the neuter—it is no one, and yet every Dasein in its everydayness already is it. It works in three ways: levelling down (anything exceptional, difficult, or original gets planed flat to what everyone can accept), public interpretedness (having an opinion on everything without ever getting close to anything), and, decisively, disburdening—since one does it this way, no one has to answer for it. The they lives your life for you, and the price is that you need not be anyone. It hands you every default you need to function (what time to be at work, what to study, what counts as success), and quietly takes away the fact that this existence is yours.

This condition is falling (Verfallen), and it wears three faces. Idle talk: speech passed along until it floats free of what it is about, where what matters is not whether it is right but that it is already being said. Curiosity: looking not in order to understand but merely to have seen, the gaze skipping and never resting (the modern feed is practically an illustration of this section). Ambiguity: everything seems already understood and already discussed, so that nothing is left that genuinely needs working out. It has to be stressed: falling is not moral degeneracy. Heidegger says explicitly that it expresses no negative evaluation—it is Dasein's most ordinary and least escapable mode, and nobody could sustain authenticity about everything. It means only this: by default, the one choosing is not you.

Thrownness, projection, mood: how the "there" is opened

What holds this clearing open? Three things, none dispensable.

First, attunement—concretely, mood (Stimmung). The German word also means the tuning of an instrument, and that pun is the key: a mood does not paint colour onto a neutral world; it tunes the world to a pitch in advance, and things show up in that key. Afraid, the noise in the stairwell simply is threatening. In good spirits, the same workload simply is doable. There is no moodless moment; calm is a mood too. And mood discloses something no amount of cool observation can reach: thrownness—you are already here, in this body, this decade, this mother tongue, these parents, none of it chosen, and you have to start from here.

Second, understanding (Verstehen). Not knowledge, but the sense in "I understand engines"—understanding is first of all a can, a potentiality-for-being. Its operation is projection: Dasein has always already thrown itself toward certain possibilities (I can fix this, I can have that conversation, I can do this kind of work). Which gives the awkward but exact formula for what a person is: thrown projection—setting out from what you did not choose, toward what you are trying to become; neither a free origin nor a fixed destination.

Third, discourse (Rede): the articulability of the whole web of significance—the world can be put into words, which is also why it can be distorted into idle talk.

The practical payoff is immediate. Next time nothing seems to be going right, don't go hunting for which thing went wrong. Most likely nothing changed but the pitch you were tuned to, and the world is showing up in that key. A mood is not inside you; it is between you and the world.

Anxiety and care: the formula for a whole life

With the they wound this tight, how does anyone ever wake up? Not through an idea, but through a mood: anxiety (Angst).

He separates it from fear, and this is the most usable distinction in the book. Fear is always fear of something definite—the test result, the layoff, that dog. Anxiety has no object. You cannot say what you are anxious about; pressed, you can only manage "nothing, it's just…". And the nothing is the answer: what anxiety is anxious about is being-in-the-world as such.

When anxiety strikes, the entire web of significance goes slack. Every arrangement that normally goes without saying—this job, these plans, this whole business of how one lives—shows its other face all at once: none of it matters. Nothing inside the world can help, because no particular thing has gone wrong; what has come loose is mattering itself. And in that moment Dasein is individualized: the they can handle your particular problems, but it cannot handle the fact that you are. So anxiety is not a pathology but an enforced lucidity—this existence cannot be delegated; you have to be it yourself. (That three-in-the-morning emptiness with no cause attached—not "something is wrong" but a shudder running through the whole "what is any of this"—is what he is pointing at.)

Gather all of it up and you get the structural whole of Dasein's being, in his famous formula (§41, paraphrased): "ahead-of-itself—already-in (the world)—as being-alongside (entities encountered within the world)." He calls that whole care (Sorge). Each hyphenated stretch has a source: ahead-of-itself = projection (you have always already overshot the present toward some possibility); already-in-the-world = thrownness (you are always already in a situation you did not pick); being-alongside = falling (you are always already buried in what's at hand).

Care is not anxiety or busyness in any psychological sense. It is an ontological structure: to be this kind of entity is for things to be at stake for you. A stone does not care where it lands. Dasein cannot not care—even opting out is a stance toward its own being. And these three moments will map one-to-one onto the three dimensions of time, which is where the real machinery is.

Being-toward-death: the one possibility nobody can take over

Division Two opens on a technical problem: Dasein is always incomplete—so long as it lives there are possibilities outstanding—so how can it ever be grasped whole? Only one thing supplies a whole: death.

He immediately rules out the familiar candidates. Death is not the death of others (you witness their death; what you undergo is loss). It is not the last event of a life (that would make it something not yet arrived and hence irrelevant now). It is not biological shutdown. Death is a possibility—and a very strange one: the possibility of the impossibility of any further possibility. The definition is precise to the point of coldness (§52, paraphrased): death is Dasein's ownmost possibility, non-relational, certain and yet indefinite as to when, and not to be outstripped. Item by item:

  • Ownmost: it hands Dasein back entirely to itself. No one can do it for you.
  • Non-relational: every social tie fails here. Your roles, your network, your titles—not one of them counts.
  • Certain and indefinite: it will come, and it can come at any moment. This is the detonator of the whole concept: not the statistical fact that all men are mortal, but the any moment, including this one.
  • Not to be outstripped: it is the end of possibility; you cannot plan around it.

The they has a highly effective muffler for all this: turn death into an event that occurs—"we all have to go sometime." The sentence appears to concede death while actually doing this (paraphrased): "one dies," and that "one" is nobody; death is thereby transferred to public affairs, and it is not my turn yet. Add a layer of good manners—the dying are assured they'll pull through, as though dying were a breach of social etiquette—and the job is complete. The they does not deny death. It does something cleverer: it makes death into something not worth changing anything for.

The counter-move is anticipation (Vorlaufen, running ahead into death). Not suicide, not morbid brooding, and emphatically not "seize the day"—that last is simply the they's other evasion, converting death into a reason to spend time well. Anticipation means holding death open as the possibility that is always possible and never transferable, without dressing it up. Three consequences follow. My existence becomes whole—for the first time I can regard this life as a single thing. I am thoroughly individualized, the ground cut out from under the they, since no "everyone does it this way" can carry this for me. And most concretely, my possibilities get re-sorted: under the light of "this could end at any moment," the things I have been fretting over that were never really mine—the title I was owed, the posture I was maintaining, the argument I had to win—reveal their contingency on the spot. Death supplies no content; it does not tell you what to do. It does exactly one thing: it hands back the fact that you have to choose.

What makes this concrete is the call of conscience. Conscience here is no moral judge and no inner rulebook: the call has no content at all—it is silent, and it does nothing but summon Dasein back out of the noise of the they. The caller is Dasein itself, yet not the everyday busy self—the call comes from me and yet from beyond me. What it wakes is guilt (Schuldigsein), stripped of every moral and religious sense: it means being the ground of something without being the ground of that ground—you must answer for your existence, yet you chose neither its beginning nor could you ever redeem all its possibilities at once. Take one road and you are permanently in arrears on the others. Hearing that call and shouldering all of it as it stands is resoluteness (Entschlossenheit—literally un-closedness, being opened up). Together they make the book's pivot term, anticipatory resoluteness: knowing clear-eyed that you could end at any moment, that nothing is guaranteed and no answer comes ready-made—and choosing anyway, and carrying it.

One thing must be nailed down about authenticity / inauthenticity: this is not a moral ranking, and not the contempt of the awakened for the herd. Eigen simply means "own": authentic means this existence is actually taken up as mine, inauthentic means living it the way one does. Heidegger says outright that authenticity is only a modification of the everyday, not a fresh start somewhere else. You will not change your job, your family, your habits because of it. What changes is not the content but your relation to it—whether you are the one choosing.

Temporality: you are not in time; you are time

Now the second half of the title arrives, and with it the answer. Why does Dasein have that odd triple structure—ahead-of-itself, already-in, alongside? Because its being is temporal through and through. The three moments of care map one-to-one onto the three dimensions of time:

First, what an "ecstasis" is. The word is Greek for standing outside oneself (the root of "ecstasy"): the three dimensions of time are not three positions but three directions in which Dasein stands out—right now you are reaching toward some future, carrying a having-been, and encountering a present, all at once and interpenetrating, not queued up in a line. Hence the most counterintuitive sentence in the book (paraphrased): temporality is not an entity; it does not exist, it temporalizes. Time is not a thing but a happening.

Second, the primacy of the future. Common sense says time flows from past to future with the present as the moving point. Heidegger inverts it: Dasein comes toward itself from its future. Take a concrete scene: you are sitting here working through a difficult book because you have thrown yourself toward being a certain kind of person; and because you are reaching that way, your past (what you studied, what you missed) shows up as useful or as a gap, and your present gets organized into what to do next. The past is not pushing you along; where you are headed reaches back and arranges your past and present into their current shape. "Having-been" is therefore not what is gone—it is what you are still carrying (he deliberately uses the perfect participle gewesen rather than "the past," to insist that it is still at work).

Third, the time we all know is derivative. Clock time—a string of uniform, countable, endlessly extending nows—he calls the vulgar understanding of time, and traces it back to Aristotle's Physics. It is not wrong, and it is indispensable (without it there are no appointments, wages, or train timetables), but it is levelled down from original temporality: flatten "I don't have much left" into "one equal now after another" and death stops being conspicuous—clock time is exactly how the they keeps finitude out of view. Note the elegant symmetry here: present-at-hand is the levelling of ready-to-hand; clock time is the levelling of temporality. The same move, performed twice.

The last step is historicity: since Dasein is thrown, its possibilities are not conjured from nothing but handed down by the tradition it carries. Taking that up authentically is repetition—not antiquarian copying, but reclaiming a possibility out of the tradition and putting it to work now. At the individual level he calls this fate; at the level of a community, destiny. That second concept grew extremely dangerous political consequences six years later, and the next section says so plainly.

The Argument in Outline

Four hundred pages run in a straight line: question → point of entry → everyday structure → structural whole → becoming authentic → the answer → and then it stops halfway.

  • Division One (preparatory analysis of Dasein): Dasein = being-in-the-world (the subject–object split is cancelled) → world = a referential totality of significance, where equipment is ready-to-hand first and only becomes present-at-hand when it breaks → being-with others, whose everyday form is domination by the they, showing up as idle talk, curiosity, ambiguity = falling → the "there" is opened by attunement (thrownness), understanding (projection), and discourse → anxiety collapses the web of significance and individualizes Dasein → the structural whole is care.
  • Division Two (Dasein and temporality): grasping Dasein whole requires facing death → death as ownmost, non-relational, certain-yet-indefinite, not to be outstripped, while the they files it under public events → the authentic stance is anticipation → attested by the silent call of conscience and by guilt → yielding anticipatory resoluteness → all of which is possible because the meaning of care is temporality, with the future primary → from which everydayness and historicity are reinterpreted, and clock time is exposed as a levelled-down derivative.
  • What was never written (you need to know this): two parts of three divisions each were planned; what exists is the first two divisions of Part One. Division Three, "Time and Being"—which was to turn around and answer the actual question of Being from the side of time—would not come, and was abandoned; Part Two (the ontological dismantling of Kant, Descartes, and Aristotle) was never written either. So the whole book is a "preparatory analysis": it prepares a question in exhaustive detail and then halts in front of it.

What does it establish, then? Not a doctrine about how to live, but a replacement of the foundations: "human being = subject that knows a world" is swapped for "human being = existing in a world, absorbed, thrown, finite, temporal." Change that foundation and knowledge, truth, other people, everydayness, anxiety and death all have to be re-sited—and this book performs the first two steps of the re-siting.

Misreadings & Real Criticism

  • Misreading 1: this is existentialism, a guide to living. The author rejected that bluntly. His 1947 Letter on Humanism breaks openly with Sartre: "existence precedes essence" is, to Heidegger, merely an inversion of a metaphysical proposition, and inverted metaphysics is still metaphysics. The book's subject is ontology, not ethics; "authenticity" is not life advice but the name of an ontological structure.
  • Misreading 2: authentic = superior, the they = the masses; and "being-toward-death" = make your time count. The text explicitly rejects the first (falling "expresses no negative evaluation"; authenticity is a modification of the everyday), and the second is precisely the evasion he names—death converted into a productivity tip. In fairness, though, the book's tone does keep implying a hierarchy of worth (idle talk, curiosity, ambiguity are pejoratives to begin with), and that is where later criticism aims.
  • Criticism 1: profundity, or a smokescreen of words? In The Elimination of Metaphysics Through Logical Analysis of Language (1932), the logical positivist Rudolf Carnap used Heidegger as his teaching specimen of meaningless sentences, citing "the nothing itself nothings"—for accuracy: that line is from the 1929 lecture "What Is Metaphysics?", not from this book, though the charge targets the same manner of writing. Heidegger's defence was that ordinary language is already saturated with a present-at-hand ontology, so saying anything new requires new words. The case has never been settled.
  • Criticism 2: the political toxicity of authenticity-talk. Adorno's The Jargon of Authenticity (1964) is an entire book on this charge: the vocabulary of authenticity, falling, and resoluteness dresses up a contentless posture of seriousness as depth, and thereby lends tone and legitimacy to irrational political mobilization—resolve itself is elevated into a virtue while "resolved to do what?" is left hanging. It is the hardest criticism to answer, because anticipatory resoluteness genuinely does not prescribe any content.
  • Criticism 3: the Nazi question, which cannot be walked around. The facts: in April 1933 Heidegger became rector of Freiburg; in May he joined the Nazi Party and delivered his rectoral address; he resigned in April 1934 but kept his party membership until 1945; he was barred from teaching until 1951; and he never issued a clear apology. The 1941 printing of this book dropped its dedication to the Jewish Husserl (restored in 1953). The Black Notebooks, published from 2014, show that antisemitic claims entered his philosophical formulations and were not merely private prejudice. The dispute is whether the 1927 book already contains the seed. Emmanuel Faye and others say it does, pointing especially at §74—the destiny of a people, the repetition of a community's heritage. Most scholars refuse to reduce the book to politics, but nearly all concede that "the authentic destiny of a community" is structurally open—it can be filled with any content whatsoever, which is exactly Adorno's point. The honest reading refuses both moves: don't discard the book because of the man, and don't pretend the book is politically innocent.
  • Criticism 4: the other is just another me. Levinas—one of Heidegger's most fervent early readers, who later lost almost his entire family to the Nazis—pressed the sharpest objection: the others of being-with remain fundamentally "Dasein like me," with no genuine alterity. Hence his counter-thesis that ethics precedes ontology: the face of the other makes a claim on me before I understand anything about being. Feminist critics have added that Dasein, though declared sexless, quietly assumes a male existence unencumbered by a body—and the body is precisely what is thinnest here, and what Merleau-Ponty went on to supply.
  • A technical note. The terminology is heavily invented, and the two main English translations (Macquarrie & Robinson 1962; Stambaugh 1996) frequently disagree on how to render it. Add that it is unfinished, and every claim beginning "Heidegger thinks one ought to live…" should be discounted—the part he most wanted to say was never written.

The Heidegger Outside This Book

Read only this and you have the early Heidegger; the centre of gravity moved after the 1930s in what he called the turn (Kehre). Being and Time asks about Being starting from Dasein; after the turn he judged that road still too much like a subject grasping an object, and set out instead from the happening of Being itself, with the human being merely the place summoned to guard it. Three things to know:

"The Origin of the Work of Art" (1935/36) gives truth another face: truth is not the correspondence of a proposition to a fact but unconcealment (aletheia)—things stepping out of hiddenness. Art does not represent; it sets up a world (Van Gogh's peasant shoes are not a picture of footwear; they open a whole world of labour, earth, and a life). And the Letter on Humanism (1947) left his single most quoted line: "Language is the house of Being." We are not language's masters but its listeners, the ones addressed—the projecting Dasein of the early work has receded into the one who is called upon.

"The Question Concerning Technology" (1953) is the piece most worth reading today. The essence of technology, he argues, is not tools or machines but a way of letting everything show up as a resource on call—for which he coined enframing (Ge-stell). Under that gaze the Rhine is no longer that river but standing-reserve for a hydroelectric plant; forests become timber supply; people become "human resources." The danger is not machines running amok but this gaze becoming the only available gaze, until human beings too appear only as dispatchable stock. He closes with Hölderlin (paraphrased): "Where the danger is, there grows the saving power also." This is the final form of the ready-to-hand/present-at-hand blade—and one of the sharpest old texts we have for thinking about data, algorithms, and the attention economy.

Discourse on Thinking (Gelassenheit, 1955) supplies the late practical stance, in pointed contrast to early resoluteness: not decisive seizing but letting be—using the technological world without being defined by it, in "releasement toward things and openness to the mystery" (paraphrased). If the keyword of Being and Time is "shoulder it," the keyword of the late work is "let go." Miss that turn and you will mistake Heidegger for a philosopher of will and decision—which is exactly what he spent his second half dismantling.

Ten Sentences

1. "Being" is the word you use every day and have never once questioned. It is not the emptiest concept but the darkest—it is not itself one of the beings, so it can never be found on the inventory of things; and every time philosophy has pressed the question it has substituted some supreme entity (the Forms, God, matter) for the answer.

2. "The essence of Dasein lies in its existence"—you do not have a nature that you then live out; what you are is settled by how you go about being. And this existence is in each case mine: non-transferable.

3. You are not a subject reaching across a chasm to an external world. "The scandal is not that the proof of the external world is still outstanding, but that such proofs are expected again and again" (paraphrased)—you were in the world from the start, and that "in" means dwelling, familiarity, absorption, not location.

4. The world is not the sum of things but a web of in-order-tos. Which is why the bereaved say the world has ended: nothing physical is missing—what's gone is the net that gave every item its point. The coffee machine is still there; it is just no longer for anything.

5. The better the hammer works the less you see it; only when it breaks, goes missing, or blocks the way does it leap out as an "object." So present-at-hand is derived from ready-to-hand: our most original relation to the world is not knowing but skill. (Dreyfus used exactly this to predict the ceiling of symbolic AI.)

6. The "they" is not other people; it is no one—and every one of us already is it in everydayness. We take pleasure as one does, judge as one does, even differ from the crowd as one does. And since one does it this way, nobody has to answer for it: the they lives your life for you, and the price is that you need not be anyone.

7. A mood does not colour a neutral world; it tunes the world to a pitch in advance, and things show up in that key. What it discloses is thrownness. Hence the formula for a person: thrown projection—setting out from what you did not choose, toward what you are trying to become.

8. Fear always has an object; anxiety has none. What anxiety is anxious about is being-in-the-world itself: the web of significance goes slack, everything shows its "none of this matters" face, and the they is no help—it can handle your problems but not the fact that you are. Anxiety is not a pathology; it is an enforced lucidity.

9. Death is ownmost, non-relational, certain yet indefinite as to when, and not to be outstripped. The they softens it into "we all go sometime"—"one dies," and that "one" is nobody. Anticipation tells you nothing about what to do; it only hands back the fact that you must choose. Authenticity is no moral rank: what changes is not the content but your relation to it.

10. The answer is time—and time is not a container you sit inside; time is the way you temporalize, with the future first: the past is not pushing you along, what you are heading toward reaches back and arranges your past and present into their present shape. The clock's string of equal nows is a levelled-down derivative, and it is precisely that levelling that keeps "I don't have much left" from ever becoming conspicuous.
