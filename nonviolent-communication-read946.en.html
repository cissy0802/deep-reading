A Language of Life · Marshall B. Rosenberg · first edition 1999

In One Sentence

When people are hurting, what comes out of their mouths is almost always a verdict on somebody else — "you don't care about me," "you're so selfish." This book's claim is that underneath every one of those verdicts sits an unmet need, expressed in the clumsiest and most counter-productive way available. Nonviolent Communication is a craft for translating verdicts back into needs: say what happened (not how bad it was), then what you feel, then which need that feeling comes from, and end with a concrete request the other person is genuinely free to refuse.

Where It Sits

Marshall Rosenberg (1934–2015) was an American clinical psychologist who trained under Carl Rogers — the humanistic psychologist whose unconditional positive regard and empathic listening are this book's most direct ancestry. Rosenberg was a boy in Detroit during the 1943 race riots, with his family shut indoors for days, and was beaten at school for having a Jewish name. He said two questions organised his whole life: what disconnects us from the compassion that seems native to us, such that we hurt one another — and what allows some people to stay connected to it even under the worst conditions? The word "nonviolent" is borrowed from Gandhi's ahimsa. In 1984 he founded the Center for Nonviolent Communication and took the method into schools, prisons, corporations, and mediation work in post-conflict settings including Rwanda and the Middle East.

The Central Claims

  • What produces your feelings is not other people's behaviour but your own needs. What they say or do is a stimulus, not a cause. The same "why isn't this done yet" lands on different needs and grows into completely different emotions.
  • Conflict happens at the level of strategies, not at the level of needs. Two people's preferred courses of action may be flatly incompatible, but one layer down — to be seen, respected, safe, rested, autonomous — needs almost never genuinely oppose each other. Argue strategies and someone must lose; reach needs and a third option becomes possible.
  • Ordinary language carries violence in it. Rosenberg calls these habits life-alienating communication: moralistic judgments (sorting people into right and wrong), comparison, denial of responsibility ("I have to"), and making demands. What they share is that they move attention off "who needs what" and onto "who deserves punishment."

The Core Concepts, One by One

The four components: observation, feeling, need, request

The whole book compresses into a four-step skeleton, usually abbreviated OFNR: what I see or hear (observation) → what I feel (feeling) → which need of mine that comes from (need) → what I'd like to ask you to do (request). The difficulty is not remembering the four steps. It is that each one has a highly convincing counterfeit, and under stress you will reach for the counterfeit every time.

The four components and their most common fakes — nearly every exercise in the book is one of these four acts of discrimination.

Observation vs evaluation: strip out what a camera can't record

Rosenberg quotes the Indian philosopher Krishnamurti (paraphrased): observing without evaluating is the highest form of human intelligence. That sounds like an aphorism, but it names a very concrete operation: delete from your sentence everything a camera could not have recorded.

"You're so irresponsible" — no camera captures irresponsibility. "You arrived more than twenty minutes late to the last three stand-ups" — that, a camera gets. "He's lazy" is unfilmable; "he came in after eleven on four days this week" is not. The difference is not politeness, it is checkability. An evaluation asserts something about a person's essence, so the only available responses are to accept or deny it — and they will deny it. An observation describes an event, so the other person can add to it, correct it, explain it. And adding, correcting and explaining are what a live conversation is made of.

Rosenberg flags one especially sneaky class of evaluation: turning a process into an essence. "He failed this exam" is a process; "he's bad at studying" is an essence. The moment you pin a person to an attribute with the verb "to be," you have eliminated the possibility of change — and eliminated your own exit, because every solution you propose now requires them to first concede what kind of person they are. Which is why most arguments have their outcome fixed by the first sentence.

Feelings vs pseudo-feelings: don't disguise an indictment as a mood

This is the least intuitive of the four. "I feel like you don't respect me." "I feel abandoned." "I feel used." These all sound like feelings and all are judgments. The test is simple: "I feel [ignored / manipulated / misunderstood / betrayed]" never reports what is happening inside your body; it reports what you have concluded the other person did to you. Grammatically the subject is you; functionally the subject is them.

Actual feelings are: sad, scared, anxious, irritated, lonely, exhausted, disappointed, relieved. Their defining property is that they cannot be argued with. If you say you're sad, nobody can dispute that you are sad. If you say "you don't respect me," the first response will be "when have I ever disrespected you?" — and the next half hour goes to litigation.

What makes pseudo-feelings so destructive is the contraband: formally you are exposing vulnerability, substantively you are filing charges. The listener receives the charges and defends, and you conclude that you made yourself vulnerable and they still stonewalled. The practical payoff of this step: when you change "I feel ignored" into "I'm sad, and a bit anxious," you do two things at once — you say something true, and you remove their reason to defend themselves.

Needs as the source of feelings: the book's actual lever

The pivotal sentence in the book: what others say and do may be the stimulus of our feelings, but it is never the cause; the cause is our own needs and expectations. This is easily misheard as "your feelings are your own fault," which is not the mechanism. The mechanism inserts a link into the causal chain: event → the state of my needs → emotion. The same "why isn't this done yet" is humiliating to someone who needs to be trusted and a relief to someone who needs help carrying the load. The intensity of an emotion reports the urgency of a need, not the severity of the event.

Rosenberg offers a very usable fork. On hearing something harsh, a person can do one of four things: blame themselves, blame the other, attend to their own feelings and needs, or attend to the other's feelings and needs. The first two are automatic; the last two are trained. And "attending" is a concrete action — you translate the sentence internally. They said "you never think about this family"; translated, that becomes "he may be exhausted, he needs someone to share the load, and he needs to be seen doing it." This is not forgiveness. It is decoding — you are not excusing the noise, you are recovering the signal in it.

Why does this link matter so much? Because needs can coexist and strategies cannot. A couple fights about the weekend: one wants the mountains, one wants to lie on the sofa. Those two strategies cannot both happen, so the conversation can only end in someone conceding. Dig one layer down and one of them needs a complete break from work while the other needs rest without having to deal with anybody — two needs in no conflict at all, which is precisely when a third option (a quiet place nearby, nobody to entertain) becomes thinkable. Almost every "irreconcilable" conflict is two people grinding away at the level of strategies, having never descended to needs.

There is a version to run on yourself: rewrite "I have to do this" as "I choose to do this, because I want ___." Rosenberg's own example is that he loathed writing clinical reports, forced himself through them for years, and one day completed the sentence honestly — the reason turned out to be "because I want the money." Having seen that clearly, he stopped writing them. The rewrite is not a motivational trick; its effect is the opposite. It converts an invisible compulsion back into a visible choice, which is the only condition under which you can discover that some choices aren't worth making.

Request vs demand: watch what you do after the "no"

The last step is where most attempts collapse. "Could you care a bit more?" is not a request, because there is no action in it. "Stop being so busy all the time" is not one either, because it names what you don't want rather than what you do. A workable request has three hard properties: concrete, positively stated, doable now. "Could we have one evening this week where you're home by seven and we eat together?" — that is a request.

What separates a request from a demand is not how politely it is phrased. It is what you do when they say no. If "no" is met with coldness, with a rehearsal of past grievances, or with reasoning continued until they feel guilty, then no matter how soft the syntax was, it was a demand. Rosenberg is blunt: only when the other person can say no without paying for it are you making a request; otherwise you are issuing an order in polite packaging.

The value of this rule is that it protects information. Under a demand, their "yes" carries no signal — they may agree, or may simply not want a fight. Over time you stop hearing anything true, and the relationship becomes a standoff between compliance and rebellion. Under a genuine request, a "yes" is real, and a "no" is usable data: it tells you some other need of theirs is more urgent right now, which means the conversation can continue instead of detonating.

One small companion move is worth stealing: ask for a reflection — "I'm not sure I said that well; could you tell me what you heard?" That is not a quiz. It is an admission that what you transmitted and what they received are frequently not the same message.

Empathy: not comfort, not advice — just don't do something, stand there

The second half of the book turns to listening. Rosenberg's definition of empathy is unusually narrow: full presence with what the other person is feeling and needing right now, with nothing of your own added. Almost everything we do while believing we are comforting someone is in fact something else. He lists the standard empathy-blockers: advising ("what you should do is"), one-upping ("that's nothing, when I —"), educating, consoling ("it wasn't your fault"), story-telling, interrogating for detail, defending the other party, correcting the facts, and sympathy ("you poor thing" — which is looking at someone from across the room, not standing with them).

Nearly all of these come from goodwill, and all of them share one motion: they move the topic off the person who is hurting. Someone in distress does not need a solution first; they need confirmation that they were understood. Rosenberg's phrase for it (paraphrased): don't just do something, stand there.

Concretely: translate what they said into feelings plus needs and offer it back as a question. "Are you frustrated because you wanted the progress you made here to be noticed?" Note that it is a question — a guess. If you guess wrong they will correct you, and the act of correcting is itself how a person sorts a tangled feeling into a clear one. Here is the book's single most useful empirical-sounding claim, and it is worth testing on your own life: people cannot take in reasoning until their feelings have been caught — and once caught, they very often produce the reasoning themselves. So empathy is not concession. It is the only route to the state in which the actual problem can be discussed.

One piece that gets skipped: self-empathy. When you've botched something, the "I'm such an idiot" running in your head is a textbook moralistic judgment, and the shame it generates makes you rigid. The substitute is to translate the self-blame into a need: "I'm gutted, because being reliable matters a lot to me." Same event; the first sentence paralyses, the second one points at a next step.

Anger: nobody lit it but the sentence in your own head

This is the hardest chapter and the most easily overlooked. Mainstream anger management offers two roads — suppress it or vent it. Rosenberg says both are wrong, because both assume the anger came from outside. His claim: the cause of anger is never what the other person did; it is the sentence in your head that says "he shouldn't have." The judgment, not the act.

His four steps are specific: (1) stop, say nothing, breathe; (2) identify the judgment currently running ("he just doesn't take me seriously"); (3) convert the judgment into the need ("I need to be taken seriously on this"); (4) express the feeling and the need instead of the judgment. His framing is vivid: anger is an alarm clock, and what it means when it rings is "a need isn't being met, and you are currently thinking about it in judgments." The alarm is not the problem; slapping it off or smashing it solves nothing. The point is to go look at what it is announcing.

Crucially, this is not a ban on expressing anger. Rosenberg's version is fully expressing it — only the content changes, from "how could you" to "I'm furious, because I need ___." That is harder than swallowing it and more effective than shouting: shouting makes the other person deal with your attack instead of your need.

The protective use of force: this book does not ask you to be nice

The most-skipped section, and the key to knowing where the method stops. Rosenberg distinguishes two uses of force: punitive (intended to make the other suffer, repent, pay) and protective (intended only to prevent harm). A child runs toward traffic and you grab them — protective. Hitting them afterwards so they remember — punitive.

The distinguishing mark is intent, not intensity. His critique of punishment is sharp: it works in the short run at the cost of moving the other person's attention from "what this action actually causes" to "how not to get caught," and it converts goodwill into a transaction. He offers two self-checks (paraphrased): what do I want this person to do — and what do I want their reason for doing it to be? Once the second question is asked, most punitive options disqualify themselves, because nobody has ever extracted sincerity by threat.

The existence of this section settles something: NVC is not conflict-avoidance. It is a tool for having the conflict, not a code of manners for suppressing it. Rosenberg's working sites were prisons, gangs, racial conflict and post-war mediation, not tea parties.

Even appreciation gets unpacked: "you're great" is also a verdict

The final chapter is short and quietly excellent. Rosenberg points out that praise is a judgment too: "you're such a good employee" has the same grammar as "you're useless" — one person sitting in the judge's seat and scoring another person's essence. It feels good only because the score is positive. And it is routinely used as a management instrument, which is exactly why its effect decays with repetition.

The NVC form of appreciation also has three parts: what you did (observation) + which need of mine it met + how I feel now. "Yesterday you redid that data set before I even asked — I was so relieved, because I really needed someone on this deadline with me." The difference: the first form labels the other person, the second hands over something of yourself. Which is also why it is harder to say — good evidence that it is the truer of the two. He adds a neat observation: many people are equally unable to receive appreciation, deflecting it ("it was nothing") or inflating on it ("obviously") — both of which are ways of dodging the connection.

The Distilled Spine

The argument is one line: most of the damage in interpersonal conflict comes not from the disagreement but from the language we habitually use to voice it — a language that automatically translates "I have a need" into "you have a defect."

Step one: name the pathology. Rosenberg bundles several everyday habits as "life-alienating communication": moralistic judgment (good/bad, right/wrong, should/shouldn't), comparison, denial of responsibility ("I have to," "those are the rules"), and demands backed by reward and punishment. Their common effect is to route attention away from needs and toward blame.

Step two: supply the replacement. The four components are not a script but four forced acts of discrimination: demote evaluation to observation, judgment to feeling, strategy to need, demand to request. Each demotion removes one of the other person's reasons to defend themselves.

Step three: install the lever. "Feelings come from needs, not from what others did" is the hinge of the whole system. It returns authorship of your emotions to you (you can audit what you're short of instead of waiting for someone to change), and it opens the solution space (strategies are mutually exclusive; needs are not).

Step four: run the same craft in reverse. Outward it is honest expression; inward it is empathic listening — and the operation is identical in both directions: take the ugly sentence and translate it into what this person feels and lacks. Only both halves together make the method; NVC that can speak but not listen is half a tool.

Step five: handle the extreme cases. Anger is redefined as a product of judgmental thinking and given a four-step disassembly. Force is split into protective and punitive, which draws the boundary where this method does not apply. Even gratitude gets decomposed into observation, need and feeling, because praise is also issued from the judge's bench.

So what it establishes is quite specific — not "be nicer," but: underneath any accusation there is a need that can be stated out loud; find it, and the conflict converts from a fight about persons into a problem to be solved. The entire training in this book is making that translation fast and accurate.

Misreadings, Criticism, Controversy

  • Misreading one: NVC means speaking softly, never fighting, being nice. The opposite. The book explicitly retains "fully expressing anger" and the protective use of force, and Rosenberg worked in prisons, with gangs, in racial conflict and in post-war mediation. It is an instrument for having the conflict, not an etiquette for swallowing it. Using NVC to mean "I'm not allowed to be angry" is the most common and most harmful deformation.
  • Misreading two: you must speak in the four-step formula. Rosenberg himself insisted NVC is a consciousness, not a formula — but his teaching (and a great deal of second-hand training) is heavily templated, and the result is people producing "when you ___, I feel ___, because I need ___, would you be willing to ___" out loud, which sounds like reciting a manual and puts listeners on guard. The workable practice: run the four components in your head, and speak them out as normal human sentences.
  • Criticism one: the evidence base is thin. This deserves saying plainly. NVC rests on clinical experience, workshops and case reports rather than randomised trials. As far as I'm aware, the existing empirical work is mostly small-sample, often uncontrolled pre/post studies relying on participants' self-report, frequently conducted by parties connected to the training; reviews generally rate the quality of evidence as low and insufficient for strong causal claims. That doesn't make it useless. It means its standing is "a persuasive body of practical wisdom," not "a tested intervention." Try it as a tool; don't cite it as a finding.
  • Criticism two: the needs list is not an empirical taxonomy. The universal-needs inventory (autonomy, connection, meaning, safety, rest, being seen, contribution…) is Rosenberg's own distillation, not a structure recovered from data, and cultures do not necessarily carve "needs" at the same joints. It is a genuinely useful handle for thinking; it is not the periodic table of the psyche.
  • Criticism three: under power asymmetry it can do harm. This is the weightiest objection. "Your feelings come from your needs, not from what they did" is liberating between equals and can shade into victim-blaming in domestic abuse, workplace bullying or systemic discrimination — redefining structural injustice as a personal communication problem, and thereby keeping someone in a dangerous place longer. Rosenberg does write about protective force, but it occupies very little of the book, whose centre of gravity is overwhelmingly on what I can do from my side. Use it with a prerequisite attached: assess safety and the power relation first, then decide whether this is the right tool at all.
  • Criticism four: it becomes a new form of manipulation, and a new form of judgment. Both deformations are common. First, wrapping what is substantively a demand in a compliant "I observe… I feel…" shell, which is formally unimpeachable and therefore harder to refuse. Second, graduates who start policing how other people speak ("that's an evaluation, not an observation") — using a theory about not judging in order to judge others is the method's most ironic failure mode. The test remains the same one: what do you do after they say no?
  • Criticism five: it assumes both parties want to cooperate. Facing someone who intends to win, or who profits from the situation, laying out your needs is showing your hand. On this point the two negotiation books already covered here make good companions: Getting to Yes's "interests vs positions" is nearly the same structure as NVC's "needs vs strategies," but it additionally demands that you work out your BATNA (your best alternative if talks fail); Never Split the Difference simply assumes the other side is not rational. NVC fits situations where the relationship must survive and both sides are broadly well-intentioned; the closer you get to a one-shot adversarial game, the less it is enough.

Ten Sentences

1. Every accusation is an unmet need expressing itself in the clumsiest available way. The entire craft of this book is translating verdicts back into needs.

2. Four components — observation, feeling, need, request. The hard part isn't remembering them but that each has a convincing fake: evaluation, pseudo-feeling, strategy, demand.

3. The observation test is "could a camera record it?" "You never listen" can't be filmed; "you were on your phone all three times I raised the budget" can — and a statement that can be corrected is a conversation that is still alive.

4. "I feel ignored / used / misunderstood" are not feelings; they are indictments dressed as moods. Real feelings — sad, scared, irritated — cannot be disputed, which is exactly why they don't trigger a defence.

5. What others do is the stimulus of your feelings, never the cause. The cause is your needs — and the intensity of an emotion reports the urgency of a need, not the severity of the event.

6. Strategies are mutually exclusive; needs are not. "The mountains" and "the sofa" can't both happen; "a complete break from work" and "rest without having to deal with anyone" don't conflict at all. Most deadlocks are two people who never went down a level.

7. Request or demand isn't decided by how politely you phrased it, but by what you do after they say no. Sulking, guilt, grievance-rehearsal — that was an order. Only under a real request does their "yes" carry information.

8. Rewrite "I have to" as "I choose to, because I want ___." Not a motivational trick — it converts an invisible compulsion back into a visible choice, which is the only way you ever discover that some choices aren't worth making.

9. Empathy is not advice, comfort or sympathy: don't just do something, stand there. People can't take in reasoning until their feelings have been caught — and once caught, they usually produce the reasoning themselves.

10. Anger is not caused by what they did but by the sentence in your head that says "he shouldn't have." Anger is an alarm clock: when it rings, a need is unmet and you are currently thinking about it in judgments.
