Overcoming Organizational Defenses

Facilitating Organizational Learning · Chris Argyris · 1990 | Folded in: "Teaching Smart People How to Learn" (HBR, 1991) and his work with Donald Schön on espoused theory vs. theory-in-use

中文 →

In One Sentence

Organizations fail to learn not because people aren't smart or hardworking enough, but because everyone skillfully follows an invisible rule, "don't let anyone be embarrassed." That rule protects face in the moment at the price of making the real problems undiscussable, and, worst of all, the fact that they're undiscussable is itself undiscussable.

Where It Sits

Argyris (1923–2013) taught at Yale and then Harvard and was one of the founders of the field of organizational learning. The ideas he developed with the MIT philosopher Donald Schön, single- and double-loop learning and espoused theory vs. theory-in-use, went into the "mental models" chapter of Peter Senge's The Fifth Discipline and sit upstream of Amy Edmondson's research on psychological safety.

The Core Argument

  • What people say and what they do follow two different theories, and they can't see the gap. Nearly everyone espouses openness, candor and a willingness to be challenged. Watch what they actually do and nearly everyone is controlling the situation, playing to win, suppressing negative feelings and protecting their own face and other people's.
  • At the level of the organization, that theory-in-use hardens into "defensive routines." They spare people embarrassment and threat, and they also stop people from finding, let alone removing, whatever causes the embarrassment and threat.
  • The more successful and clever people are, the worse they often are at learning. Because they've rarely failed, they've never practiced learning from failure. Point out a problem and their reasoning snaps into defense: blame the client, blame the fuzzy goals, blame someone else. And they don't notice they're doing it.

Put together: an organization's trouble is often not that it doesn't know, but that it knows and can't say so, and can't say that it can't say so. Argyris set out to take that nesting doll apart.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Espoused theory vs. theory-in-use: the you that you think you are, and the you that acts

What it is. In Theory in Practice (1974), Argyris and Schön separated two kinds of "theory of action." An espoused theory is what you say when someone asks what you'd do in a situation and why: the values and methods you sincerely believe in. A theory-in-use is the one you can infer from what you actually do: the rules really running your hands and feet. You can state the first. You usually can't state the second, and you may deny it when someone points it out.

An example. A manager says, "I welcome challenges from my team. I can't stand a one-man show." Record his meetings and you see that whenever someone disagrees, he smiles, says "great point," and then leads them back to his own conclusion with a string of guiding questions, before announcing, "Looks like we have consensus." He isn't lying. He sincerely believes he's open. It's just that the rule he's actually running is "control the conclusion while appearing open."

Argyris's working method was to gather exactly this kind of material, recordings, transcripts and cases, and lay the two theories side by side in front of the people involved. He claimed that across large samples spanning age, gender, culture and rank, the gap was close to universal. (That's his conclusion from years of consulting and teaching cases, not the result of controlled experiments.)

Why it matters. If the gap were deliberate, we'd call it hypocrisy, and education could fix it. Argyris's key finding was that the gap is unconscious, and a product of skill. You're so practiced at your theory-in-use that you don't need to think about it, the way a good cyclist doesn't know how they keep their balance from second to second. It changes how you read people: when someone's words and deeds don't match, assume they can't see it rather than that they're a hypocrite. And when someone points out the mismatch in you, your first reaction, "I'm not like that," may be your theory-in-use protecting itself.

2. Single-loop vs. double-loop learning: fix the move, or fix the goal too

What it is. Argyris's favorite image was a thermostat. Set to 68 degrees, it turns the heat on when the room drops below 68 and off when it goes above. It detects an error and corrects it. That's single-loop learning: adjusting actions within fixed goals and assumptions. If the thermostat could ask, "Why 68? Is that the right setting?", it would be doing double-loop learning: not just changing actions but going back to examine and revise the "governing variables" behind them, the goals, standards and underlying assumptions.

Argyris and Schön openly took the "double loop" from the cybernetician W. Ross Ashby, who described a system with two feedback loops: an inner one that handles routine regulation and an outer one that changes the inner loop's settings when it fails.

An example. A product misses its sales target three quarters running. The single-loop response: more promotion, a new ad agency, bigger sales commissions, all inside the assumptions that the product is worth selling and the pricing logic is sound. The double-loop response asks: is our assumption about what customers need wrong? Is the metric we use to define "on target" rewarding the wrong behavior?

Why it matters. Most organizations are decent at single-loop learning. Process improvement, KPI tracking and retrospectives are all single-loop. Double-loop is hard because what it questions is exactly what makes the organization, and the people with power in it, feel safe. Questioning a goal can mean questioning the boss who set it. Questioning an assumption can mean admitting the last few years were a mistake. That's why defensive routines keep blocking double-loop learning. It changes how you see problems: when the same problem keeps coming back and gets "fixed" every time, you're probably not looking at an execution problem. You're looking at a governing variable nobody has ever taken out and examined.

3. Model I and Model II: two operating systems

Argyris boiled down the theory-in-use most people fall back on under pressure to Model I, built on four governing values:

  • Achieve the purpose as I define it.
  • Win, don't lose.
  • Suppress negative feelings, yours and other people's.
  • Be "rational", meaning act the way I consider reasonable and treat emotion as unprofessional.

Under those values, the typical strategies are to design and manage the situation unilaterally, protect yourself and others unilaterally (saving other people's face for them), keep your reasoning private, and make untested attributions about other people's motives ("he just wants the credit"). The results are misunderstanding, self-sealing reasoning, mutual defensiveness and conclusions nobody can test.

His alternative, Model II, has just three governing values: valid information, free and informed choice, and internal commitment to the choice. In practice that means putting your reasoning on the table and inviting others to test it, combining advocacy (stating your position and your reasons) with inquiry (genuinely asking how others see it and where you might be wrong), and, when you attribute motives to someone, saying what the attribution rests on and checking it out in the open.

Model I vs. Model II: the difference isn't good people vs. bad people. It's whether reasoning stays hidden or gets put out to be tested.

Two crucial clarifications. First, Model II isn't the reverse of Model I. The reverse, giving up control, letting everyone win, venting every feeling, is what Argyris called "opposite Model I," and it doesn't produce valid information either. It's just a different kind of manipulation ("I'll go with whatever the group wants" can be a way of dodging responsibility). Second, Model II isn't "nicer." It's "more testable." You can advocate forcefully, as long as you give your reasons and genuinely welcome being proven wrong.

Why it matters. It moves "communication problems" from the level of attitude to the level of reasoning. Plenty of people think improving a team means getting everyone to be more candid or warmer. Argyris says the issue isn't temperature, it's whether your conclusions can be tested by anyone else. It changes how you sit in meetings: next time you hear "I just don't think he's reliable," you'll notice it's an attribution offered with no data and no way to test it, a textbook Model I move.

4. Defensive routines, and the undiscussability of the undiscussable

What it is. This is the heart of the book. Argyris defines an organizational defensive routine, roughly, as any action or policy that keeps individuals or parts of the organization from experiencing embarrassment or threat, and at the same time keeps them from identifying and reducing the causes of that embarrassment or threat. The first half is the protection, the second half the cost, and they always come bundled.

The classic defensive routine is the "mixed message," which Argyris breaks down into four rules everyone follows without saying so:

  1. Craft a message that contradicts itself.
  2. Act as if it doesn't.
  3. Make the contradiction, and the pretense that there isn't one, undiscussable.
  4. Make the undiscussability itself undiscussable.

An example. A CEO tells the heads of business units: "Be bold, innovate, make your own decisions. Of course, check with me first on anything major." What counts as major? Nobody says. Every unit head understands it as "I'm giving you freedom, just don't actually use it." None of them asks, "What exactly do you mean by major?", because that would look distrustful and inflexible. The CEO doesn't ask, "Do you think I'm saying one thing and meaning another?", because that's too awkward. So everyone guesses, nods in the meeting and talks in the corridor afterward. If a newcomer says in the meeting, "I don't think we actually have any autonomy," the room goes quiet and someone smooths it over. That's rule four at work: you can't even talk about the fact that we don't talk about this.

The nesting makes defensive routines "self-sealing": they reinforce and protect themselves and are almost impossible to spot from the inside. Argyris called the moves people develop to live inside them "fancy footwork," clever ways of talking around a problem while looking responsible. Over time the organization sinks into what he called "organizational malaise": cynicism about the organization, fault-finding without solutions, and a shared sense that "that's how it is here, nothing changes."

Why it matters. It explains something that happens again and again: in the post-mortem, everyone says, "Honestly, I knew this would go wrong." The information was in the organization all along. Defensive routines just kept it from ever reaching the table where decisions get made. It changes how you see organizations: the most dangerous problem usually isn't the one nobody knows about. It's the one everybody knows about and nobody can say.

5. Skilled incompetence: doing the wrong thing very well

What it is. In a 1986 Harvard Business Review article, "Skilled Incompetence," Argyris coined this grating phrase: managers reliably produce outcomes they don't want precisely because they're so good at certain communication skills. "Skilled" because the behavior is automatic, fluent and polished; "incompetence" because what it produces is exactly what blocks learning.

An example. A senior team discusses a strategy that failed. Everyone is articulate: "We need to look at this more holistically." "I agree with the direction, but the details could use polishing." Nobody says, "The premise of this strategy was wrong, and you set it." Their tact is a real skill, and that skill is exactly what lets the meeting end "smoothly" with the problem untouched. Another move Argyris often dissected is "easing in": instead of stating your view, you ask a series of questions so the other person "discovers" the problem on their own. It looks respectful; in fact it's covert control, and the other person usually senses they're being steered and gets defensive too.

The X-Y case. A classic exercise from Argyris's teaching: participants read a conversation in which a boss, Y, criticizes a subordinate, X. Y is blunt, telling X his performance is unacceptable and that others have complained about him. Participants almost unanimously criticize Y as heavy-handed, insensitive, guaranteed to make X defensive. Then Argyris points out that their critique of Y uses exactly the method they're faulting Y for: untested attributions ("Y doesn't really want to help X"), conclusions without evidence, and no thought for how Y would respond if he were in the room. The class has just demonstrated Model I on the spot.

Why it matters. It punctures a common illusion, that the problem lies with people who can't communicate. The best communicators are often the best at keeping problems out of sight. It changes how you think about "high emotional intelligence": smoothness and learning are often at odds, and a team where everyone is perfectly tactful may be a team that can't learn.

6. Why smart people don't learn: defensive reasoning and the "doom loop"

What it is. This is the subject of his famous 1991 HBR article, "Teaching Smart People How to Learn." Argyris studied professionals at management consulting firms: highly educated, high achievers, successful all their lives. He found these were exactly the people worst at learning. Because they rarely failed, they had never practiced learning from failure. They were superb at solving problems out in the world and poor at examining how their own behavior contributed to problems.

An example. In a project review, consultants discussed openly what had gone wrong, which sounded very candid. But look at the causes they listed: an uncooperative client, unclear goals, too few resources from the partners. Every cause was "out there." When Argyris asked them to look at how their own behavior had contributed, the conversation tensed up at once: they defended, explained, and argued that their judgment at the time had been sound. Argyris called this defensive reasoning: premises kept tacit, inferences kept private, conclusions shielded from testing. It's the exact opposite of the rigorous reasoning they used on their clients' problems.

He also described these people's "doom loop." When they fell short of their own high standards, they didn't treat it as material to learn from. They slid into intense self-reproach and despair, because their whole sense of self rested on success. The result was a brittle kind of person: fine as long as things went well, shattered at the first real knock.

Why it matters. It turns learning from a problem of knowledge into a problem of reasoning. What smart people lack isn't information but the habit of treating their own reasoning as something to be tested. It changes how you see "top performers": someone who never fails isn't necessarily someone who learns. They may just never have hit anything that shook their premises.

7. The ladder of inference and the left-hand column: pulling hidden reasoning into view

What it is. Argyris didn't stop at diagnosis. He offered tools. The first is the ladder of inference. We start from directly observable data (what he said in the meeting, the words he used), add culturally shared meanings ("'let's revisit this' means no around here"), add our own interpretation ("he doesn't support the project"), and arrive at conclusions and actions ("I won't invite him next time"). We think we're standing on the facts when we've already climbed to the top of the ladder, and none of the rungs below has been checked. Model II means walking down the ladder with the other person, rung by rung: "I'm thinking this because I saw A and B. Did you see the same thing?"

The second is the left-hand column exercise. Split a page into two columns. On the right, write what was actually said in a difficult conversation. On the left, write what you were thinking and didn't say. The left column is almost always full, and usually sharper and more informative than the right. That's the valid information the defensive routines swallowed. The point isn't to blurt out the whole left column. It's to see why you felt you couldn't say it, and whether that "couldn't" is itself something that could be discussed.

Why it matters. These two tools make the abstraction of Model II something you can practice: make reasoning visible and make attributions testable. Senge's team later built them into The Fifth Discipline and its fieldbook, and they became standard kit in organizational-learning workshops. It changes how you deal with people: when you're certain "he's just like that," ask yourself which rung you're on and how many below it you never checked.

The Spine of the Argument

The whole book circles a single causal chain:

(1) Everyone has two theories of action: in words they espouse openness and testing (Model II); in deeds they run control, winning and face-saving (Model I), and they can't see the gap. (2) At the individual level, Model I produces skilled incompetence: the more fluent the communicator, the more smoothly they route around awkward truths. (3) At the organizational level, those individual strategies harden into defensive routines, above all the four rules of the mixed message, nested until even the undiscussability is undiscussable and the system seals itself. (4) So the organization can only manage single-loop learning, patching things without examining goals and assumptions, and its smart, successful people, never having failed and reasoning defensively, are the least able to break out. (5) The way out isn't a slogan about "more honesty" but Model II as a practiced skill: putting reasoning and attributions on the table, pairing advocacy with inquiry, and starting at the top to make the undiscussable discussable.

What does he prove it with? Mainly with field material gathered over decades, recordings, cases and his own experiments as an intervener inside companies, plus a theory built up from that material. He called this approach "action science": the researcher doesn't just observe but intervenes, records what happens, and uses the results to test the theory. Its strength is cases so true to life that readers recognize themselves in them. Its weakness is the same: most of the evidence comes from interventions he ran himself.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: double-loop learning means thinking outside the box. No. Double-loop isn't about being more creative. It's about examining and revising the goals and assumptions that govern your behavior, especially the ones about yourself. A team can brainstorm piles of new ideas every week and stay entirely single-loop.

Misreading 2: Model II means total disclosure and no defenses. No. Argyris didn't tell people to dump out the whole left-hand column, and he granted that defenses make sense in some settings, such as genuinely hostile ones. What he opposed were defenses that can't be discussed. And as noted, giving up control and pleasing everyone is just "opposite Model I," which produces no more valid information than Model I does.

Misreading 3: it's an attack on "hypocritical" managers. Quite the reverse. Argyris stressed again and again that the gap is unconscious and universal, himself included. Treating it as some people's moral failing is itself an untested, Model I attribution.

Criticism 1: thin evidence. Argyris claimed Model I is nearly universal, but the support comes mostly from his own cases and interventions, with little independent, replicable quantitative work. The methodology of action science has long been contested: when the researcher both proposes the theory and runs the intervention, confirmation bias is hard to rule out. Edmondson later turned related intuitions into a measurable psychological-safety scale backed by empirical studies, partly filling this gap.

Criticism 2: it neglects power and incentives. Scholars in critical management studies point out that Argyris treats defensiveness as a product of individual reasoning, when in many organizations keeping quiet is rational: telling the truth can cost you your job. If the problem comes from power structures and incentive design, training individuals to reason differently only scratches the surface. Argyris's reply was that structures are themselves sustained by people's theories-in-use, and that change has to start at the top. The reply doesn't fully dissolve the objection.

Criticism 3: too hard to learn. Argyris himself admitted that even executives with long training and a sincere wish to change struggled to sustain Model II under pressure. They could describe Model II and still slipped back into Model I at the decisive moment. That confirms his theory (theories-in-use run deep), and it also raises doubts about how far it travels as a management method.

Criticism 4: dense and repetitive writing. Like most of his books, this one is packed with concepts, long cases and heavily repeated jargon, and reads far less easily than Senge or Edmondson. That's why his ideas reached a wide audience mostly through other people's retellings: the ladder of inference, the left-hand column, psychological safety.

Crossovers

  • Neuroscience · Gazzaniga's "interpreter": in split-brain patients, when the right hemisphere starts an action, the left instantly invents a plausible but false reason for it. Michael Gazzaniga called this the "interpreter." It mirrors the gap between espoused theory and theory-in-use: we sincerely give reasons that aren't the real ones. Unlike: split-brain is disconnected hardware, while Argyris's gap is learned and can be narrowed with practice.
  • Psychology · Festinger's When Prophecy Fails: in 1956 Leon Festinger and colleagues watched a doomsday group whose prophecy failed; members proselytized harder to ease cognitive dissonance. Like defensive reasoning, the more evidence threatens the self, the tighter the grip. Unlike: the group's belief was public; Model I is invisible.
  • Eastern philosophy · Wang Yangming: the Ming philosopher taught that "to know and not act is not yet to know." That's the espoused/in-use split exactly: being able to say it isn't knowing it. Unlike: Wang worked through inner realization; Argyris through tape recordings and testing by others.
  • Distributed systems · blameless postmortems: in 2012 Etsy's John Allspaw championed the "blameless postmortem," later central to Google's SRE culture. It deliberately dismantles the scapegoat routine so real information can surface. Unlike: blamelessness is a process rule; Argyris would say rules don't get past theories-in-use.
  • Philosophy of science · Popper's falsifiability: Karl Popper said the mark of science is that its claims can be refuted. Model II nearly carries that into the meeting room: make your reasoning public so others can falsify it. Unlike: science has shared testing procedures; interpersonal reasoning has no referee.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① What people say and what they do follow two different theories, and they can't see the gap.

② Single-loop learning corrects actions; double-loop learning corrects the goals and assumptions behind them.

③ Under pressure almost everyone defaults to Model I: control the situation, win, suppress feelings, look rational.

④ Model II isn't nicer, it's more testable: valid information, informed choice, internal commitment.

⑤ Defensive routines protect you from embarrassment and keep you from finding its causes.

⑥ The last rule of the mixed message: make the undiscussable undiscussable too.

⑦ Skilled incompetence: the best communicators are the best at keeping problems out of sight.

⑧ Smart people rarely fail, so they never learned to learn from failure; challenged, their reasoning turns defensive.

⑨ You think you're standing on the facts; you're already at the top of the ladder of inference.
