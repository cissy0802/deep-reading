The Prince

Il Principe · Niccolò Machiavelli · written 1513, published posthumously 1532

中文 →

In One Sentence

This is not a book that teaches wickedness; it is the first book to move politics wholesale from how people ought to behave to how people actually behave. It answers exactly one question — how power is seized and how it is kept — and on whether the answer is moral, Machiavelli is perfectly clear: it isn't; and if you insist on doing only what morality permits, you will lose, and take everyone you meant to protect down with you.

Where It Sits

Machiavelli spent fourteen years as secretary to the Second Chancery of the Florentine Republic — a working official handling diplomacy and military affairs, sent as envoy to the King of France, the Pope and the Holy Roman Emperor, and close enough to Cesare Borgia to watch him assemble a state out of the Romagna piece by piece. When the Medici returned in 1512 he was dismissed, implicated in a conspiracy, jailed, and tortured with the strappado (arms bound behind the back and the body hoisted — standard interrogation of the day). Released, he was banished to a small farm outside the city. The Prince was written there in the autumn and winter of 1513 — half political theory, half a job application that never worked. Dedicated to Lorenzo de' Medici, unpublished in his lifetime, printed in 1532, and placed on the Church's Index of Prohibited Books in 1559. For four centuries afterward "Machiavellian" has served as an insult, while these seventy-odd pages have been treated as the starting point of modern political thought.

The Central Claims

  • Look only at the effectual truth. Political analysis must describe how things actually work, not how an imagined republic would work; a plan derived from how people ought to behave shatters on contact with people as they are.
  • The virtues of ruling and the virtues of being a good person are two different sets, and they collide. The claim is not that morality is irrelevant. It is that a ruler who insists on being good in everything, among so many who are not good, will be destroyed — so he must learn not to be good when necessary, and learn to tell when it is necessary.
  • Fortune governs about half; the other half is yours — provided you have virtù and are willing to change with the times. And almost nobody can change their own temperament. That is the book's deepest and least-quoted pessimism.

The Core Ideas, One by One

The effectual truth (verità effettuale): prying "is" apart from "ought"

Chapter 15 opens with the methodological declaration that is the real original contribution of the book. In paraphrase: many have imagined republics and principalities that have never been seen or known to exist in truth; but I intend to go straight to the effectual truth of the thing, rather than to the imagination of it. The effectual truth is the truth that actually operates — not what a situation should be, but what it is and what laws it moves by.

To see where the knife lands, you need to know what he was cutting. From the Middle Ages through the Renaissance there was a very stable genre called the mirror for princes (Latin speculum principis): a manual of self-improvement for young rulers, urging them chapter by chapter to be generous, merciful, faithful and pious, on the promise that heaven favors the good and a well-run state follows. Machiavelli keeps the entire shell of that genre — he too takes generosity, mercy and good faith one chapter at a time — and inverts every conclusion. Generosity burns through the treasury, forces you into taxation and confiscation, and ends in hatred. "Mercy" that tolerates disorder kills far more people than decisive severity does. And a ruler who keeps faith gets eaten by rulers who don't.

He kept the genre and swapped the criterion — which is exactly why it read as monstrous at the time.

The consequence of that cut is that political science comes away from ethics. Before him, any discussion of power had to be seated inside a doctrine of the good. After him there exists a whole class of inquiry that asks only how does this work and defers should it — realist international relations, organizational power analysis, and the line you hear in any modern company, "first let's look at how the incentives are set up." A medical analogy fits: he wrote politics as pathology rather than as a regimen for healthy living. Pathology doesn't moralize about a disease; it describes how the disease progresses and when it can still be treated. Which gives you a habit worth keeping: whenever you read a proposal about an organization, a state, or a relationship, split it in two — is this describing what people will actually do, or prescribing what they should do? The proposals that blend the two are usually the first to fail.

Virtù and fortuna: half yours, half the world's

Neither of the book's two key words survives literal translation. Virtù is not "virtue." Its root is the Latin vir, an adult man; it means capacity, nerve, decisiveness, adaptability, and the willingness to strike hard when striking hard is what the moment requires. A ruthless but highly effective tyrant can be full of virtù; a kind and hesitant prince has none. Fortuna is heavier than "luck" — it descends from the Roman goddess turning her wheel, and it names the whole external run of events that you cannot control but must answer to.

Chapter 25 gives the book its finest image: fortune is the arbiter of half our actions, but leaves the other half, or nearly so, to be governed by us. Fortune, he says, is like one of those violent rivers that floods, tears down banks, carries off buildings — nothing stops it. But that does not license fatalism, because when the water is calm men can build embankments and channels, so that the next flood is either diverted or at least less ruinous. Notice the temporal structure of the metaphor: virtù is not heroics in the torrent, it is building the dikes beforehand. Italy, he adds, had become a battleground for every foreign army precisely because it was "a country without embankments."

Then comes the deepest layer, the one motivational quotation always drops. Chapter 25 also asks: why does the same man succeed today and fail tomorrow? His answer — because success depends on whether your way of proceeding happens to match the temper of the times. The cautious man fails in an age that demands impetuosity. Pope Julius II was impetuous in everything and happened to live in an age that rewarded it, so everything he did came off; had he lived into a time that required caution, he would have been ruined, because a man cannot change his own nature. That pushes the book's pragmatism to the edge of fatalism: the people we call good at seizing the moment were mostly just temperamentally well matched to their moment, and Machiavelli does not believe anyone truly learns to switch. It also explains the urgency of his final chapter — a window is not a capability you get to hold on to.

In fairness, chapter 25 also contains the least defensible passage in the book: fortune is a woman, he says, and must be beaten and struck down if you want to keep her under, and she favors the young because they are less cautious, fiercer, and command her more boldly. That is naked misogyny and there is no point laundering it. But see what sits inside the ugly shell: hesitation gets you flattened by events, while decisive action more often catches the opening — a claim we now phrase as "action generates information."

The problem of new orders: why reformers die halfway

Chapter 6 holds the most-quoted passage in the book (paraphrased): there is nothing more difficult to take in hand, more perilous to conduct, or more uncertain in its success, than to lead in the introduction of a new order of things. For the reformer has enemies in all who profit by the old order, and only lukewarm defenders in all those who would profit by the new — lukewarm partly from fear of their adversaries, who have the law on their side, and partly from the incredulity of men, who do not truly believe in anything new until they have had actual experience of it.

The power of that passage is the asymmetry in the ledger it exposes. The opponents' losses are concrete, immediate and certain; the supporters' gains are abstract, deferred and uncertain. Certain losses mobilize people far harder than uncertain gains do — so in any reform, before results arrive, the opposition is always better organized, harder-working, and more willing to pay costs. It is the same finding behavioral economics now calls loss aversion, arrived at four and a half centuries early.

From which he draws a hard conclusion: all armed prophets have conquered, and all unarmed ones have been destroyed. "Prophet" is not a figure of speech here; he has a specific corpse in mind. Girolamo Savonarola, the Dominican friar whose preaching gave him effective control of Florence after 1494 and produced the "bonfire of the vanities," held nothing but the enthusiasm of his followers — no force of his own. When the mood turned in 1498 he was condemned, hanged and burned in the main square. The moment people stopped believing, he had nothing left. Machiavelli's point is not that prophets should be violent; it is that you need some form of power that survives the ebb of enthusiasm, or your entire project is mounted on something guaranteed to fade.

So the next time you look at a failed reform — a reorganization, a product pivot, a policy — don't start with "was the direction right?" Count three things instead: how certain are the losses to those who oppose it, how vague are the gains to those who'd benefit, and do you have the strength to survive until results become visible? Most reforms don't die of direction. They die because nobody did that arithmetic.

The lion and the fox: force, fraud, and appearance as an asset

Chapter 18. There are two ways of contending, he writes: by law, which is proper to man, and by force, which is proper to beasts. Because the first is often insufficient, a prince must know how to use the second. And among the beasts he must choose two: the fox recognizes traps, the lion frightens off wolves — a lion alone walks into snares, a fox alone cannot hold off wolves; you must be both, and know which to be when.

Then the notorious doctrine on promises (paraphrased): a prudent ruler cannot and should not keep his word when keeping it works against him, and when the reasons that made him give it no longer exist. The justification is glacial: if all men were good, this precept would be bad; but because they are not, and will not keep faith with you, you need not keep faith with them. He is framing breach of promise not as a vice but as a symmetrical move in a game.

What is genuinely modern, though, is the half-chapter that follows: seeming matters more than being. A prince should appear merciful, faithful, humane, upright and devout — and ideally be so — but must be prepared to turn to the opposite when required. The supporting observation is about communication (paraphrased): everyone sees what you appear to be, few touch what you are; and those few dare not contradict the many.

This is the earliest text that analyzes reputation as a manageable political asset: not a by-product of morality but a resource with an acquisition cost, a depreciation schedule, an investment case, and the possibility of a run on the bank. Which is why the image-work of politicians, companies and platforms is not decorative padding — it is asset management. It also exposes where the whole method is brittle: it rests entirely on few touch what you are. Once verification gets cheap — records, screenshots, logs, a searchable past — the fox's room to move shrinks. Machiavelli's politics of appearance is a product of an age when information was expensive.

Love, fear, and the line that actually matters: never be hated

Chapter 17 is the most famous chapter and the most brutally half-quoted. What he actually argues: it is best to be both loved and feared; but since the two rarely go together, if you must choose, being feared is the safer bet. The reasoning is an account of human nature: love is held by a chain of obligation, and men are ungrateful, fickle and quick to avoid danger — in good times they will die for you, and when the danger is real the chain snaps. Fear is held by dread of punishment, and that never fails you. The decisive difference is control: whether you are loved is someone else's choice, whereas whether you are feared is your instrument. He is looking for a foundation that does not depend on other people's goodwill.

But the constraint that follows is the actual point of the chapter, and precisely the part the quoters amputate: a prince should make himself feared in such a way that, if he is not loved, he at least avoids being hated. How? By a very concrete rule: don't touch your subjects' property, and don't touch their women. The reason is cold enough to remember for life — men forget the death of a father more quickly than the loss of a patrimony — because executing a man at least requires a presentable justification, while a pretext for seizing property can always be found, and a ruler who once starts living off confiscation cannot stop.

Why that limit is the essence of the doctrine: fear produces obedience; hatred produces conspiracy. A feared ruler faces scattered individuals each running their own calculations. A hated ruler manufactures a coalition with a shared objective — and that is what actually kills regimes. Chapter 19 says it plainly: what a prince truly has to guard against is not the foreign enemy but the domestic plot, and the only reliable soil for plots is general resentment. This travels well. To judge how long a coercive organization can last, don't look at how harsh it is; look at whether it has crossed the line — whether it has taken away what people regard as theirs. A company that pays out severance and a company that withholds wages face two completely different grades of employee ferocity.

The economics of cruelty: injuries all at once, benefits drop by drop

Chapter 8 draws a distinction many readers find repellent and which is nonetheless structurally exact: cruelty can be well used or badly used. Well used means done out of the necessity of self-preservation, done all at once, not repeated afterward, and converted so far as possible into benefit for the subjects. Badly used means beginning small and increasing with time rather than diminishing. He pairs it with a symmetrical rule about timing: injuries should all be done together, so that being tasted less they offend less; benefits should be handed out little by little, so that they are savored longer. Invert either half and you can see why: cruelty on the installment plan leaves everyone braced for the next blow and lets resentment compound, while a benefit delivered all at once is instantly reclassified as the baseline and gratitude drops to zero.

The starkest case is in chapter 7. Cesare Borgia — son of Pope Alexander VI, known as Duke Valentino — had just taken the Romagna, a province plundered into chaos by its petty lords. He sent in Remirro de Orco, a man famous for cruelty and decisiveness, with full authority. Order was duly restored, and so was public loathing. Then one morning the people of Cesena found Remirro in the town square in two pieces, with a block of wood and a bloody knife beside him. Machiavelli's entire comment is one sentence (paraphrased): the ferocity of that spectacle left the people at once satisfied and stupefied.

Why he offers this as a model is worth spelling out. He is not teaching you to kill people; he is dissecting a structure you can still see everywhere: how power books the cost of harm to somebody else's account. With one hand Borgia outsourced the violence and collected "order"; with the other he offered up the enforcer and collected "justice." Both entries went into his own ledger, and the resentment stayed with a corpse. Swap the props and the structure is ubiquitous: an outside consultancy issues the report that authorizes the layoffs, a middle manager announces the bad news, a single executive "takes responsibility" and resigns to close the file on a systemic failure. The next time you watch a clean, decisive reckoning, ask: who is Remirro here, and whose account just got settled?

Your own arms: the one thing you cannot outsource

Chapters 12 to 14 deal with armies — the part most readers skip and the part its author cared about most. The foundation stone: the main foundations of every state are good laws and good arms; and since there cannot be good laws where there are not good arms, I shall speak only of arms. He then rules out the two labor-saving options of his day. Mercenaries (mercenari, the professional companies brought by a condottiere) are "useless and dangerous" — beyond their pay, they have no reason to die for you. Auxiliaries, troops borrowed from another power, are worse: if you lose you are undone, and if you win you are still undone, because you have fallen into the hands of the man who lent them. The only reliable force is your own: raised from your own subjects, sharing your fate.

Here honesty requires an addition, and it is the key to the tone of the whole book: this prescription had already failed in his own hands. In 1506 it was Machiavelli himself who organized a citizen militia for Florence to replace hired troops; in 1509 that militia took part in the recovery of Pisa, the proudest achievement of his career. Then in 1512 Spanish professionals attacked Prato, the militia broke, the town was sacked, the Republic fell that same day — and he lost his post, went to prison, and was tortured. So The Prince is not a winner's summary of his methods. It is a loser in exile taking his own defeat apart layer by layer, and a good deal of its mercilessness is aimed at himself.

One excellent corollary sits back in chapter 3: political trouble behaves like consumption — in the beginning easy to cure and hard to detect, later easy to detect and impossible to cure. This is why the Romans never postponed a war to avoid one, since trouble does not disappear, it merely relocates to a moment that suits your opponent better. Put the two together and you have unusually hard practical advice: never outsource a capability your survival depends on to people who don't share your fate; and the problem you're already uneasy about but can still "live with" is at its cheapest to fix right now.

The Machiavelli Outside This Book

Read only The Prince and you get half a Machiavelli — and precisely the half that has been most heavily cropped.

The Discourses on Livy (Discorsi sopra la prima deca di Tito Livio, c. 1513–1519) is his longest political work and the one that actually carries his own position, and it is republican. It argues that republics outlast and out-expand principalities; that a people taken as a whole is more reliable in judgment than a single prince, harder to buy and less prone to error (there is a near-counterintuitive line to the effect that the people are wiser and more constant than a prince). Most striking is what he says about conflict: Rome was free and strong precisely because of the endless tumults between the plebs and the Senate. Strife is not the enemy of order; it was because neither side could crush the other that good laws protecting liberty had to be forced into existence. This is one of the earliest full statements of what we now call checks and balances. Which is why Rousseau wrote in The Social Contract, in paraphrase, that in pretending to instruct kings Machiavelli was in fact teaching the people great lessons — that The Prince is the book of republicans.

How to hold the two books together is the key to the man. The Prince addresses starting from rubble — how one person rebuilds order in a situation already corrupt, disordered and trampled by foreign armies. The Discourses addresses what keeps an order alive once it exists — and the answer there is institutions, law and the people, not the new prince. He never held that one-man rule was good in itself, only that at certain moments it is a necessary piece of surgery. Treating the scalpel as ordinary cutlery is what later readers did, not what he proposed.

One more is worth knowing: the comedy La Mandragola shows the other side of him — he was a first-rate, deliciously vicious playwright. The same pair of eyes, having seen exactly how people are led by appetite and vanity, produces The Prince when it writes politics and a farce when it writes for the stage.

Finally, the letter. On 10 December 1513 he described his life in exile to his friend Francesco Vettori (paraphrased): by day he knocked about with woodcutters and travelers and squabbled over cards at the inn; in the evening he came home, took off his mud-caked clothes, put on robes of court and palace, entered the ancient courts of ancient men, and there conversed with them, asking the reasons for their actions, and they in their humanity answered him — and for four hours he felt no boredom, forgot every worry, did not fear poverty and was not terrified by death. In that letter he mentions that he has been composing a little book on principalities. Keep the letter in mind and you will never read The Prince as the arrogant handbook of a man in power: it was written by a man politics had thrown away, out of memory, humiliation and refusal.

The Argument, Stripped to Its Frame

Twenty-six chapters are really four blocks on one line: what kind of situation are you holding → do you have your own force → how then should you conduct yourself → and the moment is now.

  • Ch. 1–11 · Taxonomy. The kinds of principality (hereditary, mixed, civil, ecclesiastical) and how each is acquired — by one's own arms and virtù, by another's arms and fortune, by crime, or by the favor of fellow citizens. The conclusion in one line: what is won by your own strength is hard to get and easy to hold; what is won by someone else's is easy to get and hard to hold.
  • Ch. 12–14 · Arms. Everything rests on having your own; the one art a prince should study is war, kept sharp in peacetime by hunting, history and drill.
  • Ch. 15–23 · Conduct (the center of gravity). Liberality and parsimony, cruelty and mercy, love and fear, keeping faith, avoiding contempt and hatred, winning reputation, choosing ministers, escaping flatterers — the whole block is the mirror-for-princes turned over item by item.
  • Ch. 24–26 · Close. Why the princes of Italy lost their states (the verdict: blame your own indolence, not fortune); the half-and-half of fortune and virtù; and then the last chapter changes key entirely, from cool technical analysis into a burning manifesto calling on the Medici to free Italy from the "barbarians" — the French and Spanish armies then fighting across the peninsula — and closing with lines from Petrarch (paraphrased): virtue will take up arms against fury, and the fight will be short, for the ancient valor is not yet dead in Italian hearts.

How does it prove anything? Almost entirely by induction, cross-cutting two bodies of evidence: ancient history (Rome, Livy, Xenophon's Cyrus) and contemporary events he lived through (Borgia, Julius II, the string of blunders Louis XII made in Italy, the death of Savonarola). He never derives "how people ought to act" from first principles; he reads the rules backward off the record of who kept a state and who lost one. That is the source of its force — it is concrete, and every rule can be pinned to a specific man and the specific way he was finished. It is also the source of its weakness: small sample, narrow period, and the cases were chosen by someone who already had his conclusion.

Misreadings and Real Criticism

  • "The ends justify the means" is not in the book. The nearest thing is a line in chapter 18: in the actions of men, and especially of princes, people "look to the end" (si guarda al fine). That is a description of how the world judges — the public scores you on outcomes — not a moral license to do anything you please. Its conversion into a moral principle is the largest misquotation attached to this book in four hundred years, and the main source of the word "Machiavellianism."
  • It is not a pamphlet for monarchy. As above, he was a republican, and both Spinoza and Rousseau read the book as a warning written for the people. The "satire" school goes further and reads the whole thing as parody — that is a stretch, since the advice is far too specific and operational to be a joke — but the narrower point, that he is not recommending princely rule as such, holds up.
  • Two powerful modern readings, flatly opposed. Leo Strauss (Thoughts on Machiavelli, 1958) argues we should stop making excuses: the man is "a teacher of evil," openly and deliberately, and the modern predicament begins there. Isaiah Berlin (The Originality of Machiavelli, 1972) reads deeper: the real discovery is not that immorality is permitted but that there exist two moralities that cannot be reconciled — the Christian one (humility, mercy, forgiveness, the salvation of the soul) and the classical pagan-civic one (glory, strength, the vigor and liberty of the community). Each is internally coherent; both cannot be cashed at once. Choose one and you are committed to sacrificing what the other holds dear. If he is right, the mainstream Western conviction that all genuine values can in principle be harmonized into a single system is false — which, Berlin argues, is what is truly disturbing about him, far more than any lesson in wickedness.
  • His own exemplar collapsed. Cesare Borgia, the model he holds up, was destroyed too — and Machiavelli concedes the reason was that Borgia happened to be gravely ill when his father the Pope died, and had not foreseen a hostile successor. That leaves a hole: if even the man who did every step right can be undone by one accident, how much of the "half" does fortune really leave you? The system cracks here, and he does not repair it.
  • "Machiavellianism" as a personality label is a cartoon of the book. Psychology has the Mach-IV scale (Christie & Geis, 1970) and later the Dark Triad (Paulhus & Williams, 2002), which turn it into an individual trait of manipulation, emotional coldness and instrumental treatment of others. But the book is about public conduct under the specific constraint of keeping a state, not private character; equating the two reads a work of political theory as a personality quiz.
  • Some conclusions were narrow to begin with. His sample is the Italian city-states of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries: small-scale, personalized rule, with no standing bureaucracy, no mass media, no elections. Drop "feared but not hated" into a transparent system where leaders can be replaced on a schedule and the cost structure is entirely different. Read it for the method, not the checklist.
  • Say the ugly parts plainly. The passage comparing fortune to a woman to be beaten into submission is genuinely repellent, and his composure about cruelty is genuinely cold. Defending him as "really a moralist underneath" is over-restoration: he did hold that to keep a state a prince must be ready to do what is not good, and he does not apologize for it. The book is worth reading not because it is harmless but because it says honestly something we still don't like to admit: good intentions, by themselves, produce no effect at all.

Ten Sentences

1. He moved politics from "how people ought to be" to "how people are" — refusing to imagine republics no one has ever seen, and going straight to the effectual truth of the thing; that cut separated political science from ethics.

2. "A man who wishes to be good in every respect, among so many who are not good, must come to ruin" (paraphrased) — the virtues of ruling and the virtues of being a decent person are two sets, and they fight.

3. Virtù is not virtue but capacity, decisiveness and adaptability; fortuna governs only half, and the half that is yours has to be earned by building dikes while the river is calm.

4. The book's deepest pessimism is in chapter 25: those who succeed are mostly those whose temperament happened to match their era — and almost nobody can change their temperament.

5. Nothing is more perilous than introducing a new order: those who profit from the old are committed enemies, those who would profit from the new are lukewarm friends — hence "all armed prophets conquered, all unarmed ones were destroyed."

6. A prince must be both lion and fox: the lion alone walks into traps, the fox alone can't hold off wolves — and what matters even more is to seem virtuous, since "everyone sees what you appear to be, few touch what you are" (paraphrased).

7. If you cannot be both loved and feared, choose feared: love hangs on a chain of obligation that men snap the moment their interests are touched, while fear rests on dread of punishment, which never fails.

8. But the real line is never be hated — don't touch people's property or their families, because "men forget the death of a father more quickly than the loss of a patrimony" (paraphrased). Fear produces obedience; hatred produces conspiracy.

9. Injuries all at once, benefits drop by drop — and power's favorite maneuver is to book the cost of harm to somebody else's account: that morning in the square at Cesena, Remirro in two pieces beside a block of wood and a bloody knife.

10. "The ends justify the means" is not his line; his line is that the world judges by outcomes. And anyone who reads only The Prince has met only half of him — the Machiavelli of the Discourses is a republican who thought the people more reliable than any prince, and who located Rome's liberty in the endless quarrels between the plebs and the Senate.
