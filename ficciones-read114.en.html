Ficciones

Jorge Luis Borges · 1944

中文 →

In One Sentence

A handful of stories short enough to read in one sitting, each doing exactly one thing: it picks up a philosophical idea — idealism, infinity, branching time, perfect memory, the question of who an author is — takes it completely seriously, pushes it to the end, and watches what kind of monster it becomes inside a concrete world. By the last page you realize that everything we use to impose order on the universe — encyclopedias, libraries, detective logic, history, memory — is a labyrinth of our own making. And we cannot live outside it.

Where It Sits

Borges (1899–1986) was an Argentine poet and librarian who went blind in later life. Ficciones is his central story collection (first edition 1944, three stories added in 1956), and it is one of the fountainheads of the twentieth-century novel of ideas: Calvino, Eco, much of postmodern fiction, even the early debates about hypertext all find a prototype here.

The Core Argument

  • Metaphysics is a branch of fantastic literature. In "Tlön," the philosophers of an invented planet seek "not truth, nor even plausibility, but astonishment" (paraphrased) and treat metaphysics as a genre of fantasy. Borges turns this around and uses it as a method: he writes philosophical systems as the premises of stories, so the reader lives through the consequences of an idea instead of memorizing its definition.
  • The universe is a labyrinth to us, and order is man-made. The infinite library, the lottery state, the forking garden — the world may be meaningless or drowning in too much meaning. Reason is the thread we hold to find our way through, but the same thread can lead us straight into a trap.
  • Author, reader, text and identity are all unstable. The same words mean something else under another author's name; a dreamer discovers he is being dreamed; the traitor and the hero are one man. "Who am I, and who wrote this?" turns out to be a question of vantage point, not of fact.

The Key Ideas, One by One

First, a detail with a certain irony for a site like this one. In the prologue to the first half of the book, Borges calls the writing of vast books "a laborious and impoverishing extravagance" — spreading over five hundred pages an idea that could be told aloud in a few minutes. The better course, he says, is to pretend those books already exist and offer a summary, a commentary (paraphrased). So several of these stories take the form of a book review, an encyclopedia entry, a scholarly obituary — he writes distillations of books that were never written. Keep that in mind and every idea below opens up more easily.

1. Tlön: what happens when an idealist world is taken seriously ("Tlön, Uqbar, Orbis Tertius")

What it is. The narrator and his friend Bioy Casares (a real person, Borges's close friend and collaborator) find an entry on a country called Uqbar in a pirated encyclopedia — and in no other copy anywhere. Following the trail, they uncover a secret society, centuries old, that has been compiling, volume by volume, the complete encyclopedia of an imaginary planet called Tlön. The premise of Tlön: its people are born idealists (idealism: the view that the world exists only in perception, and there are no "things" independent of minds) — the position of the eighteenth-century philosopher George Berkeley, for whom "to be is to be perceived."

The mechanism: language follows philosophy. Since nothing persists as an object, the languages of Tlön's southern hemisphere have no nouns, only impersonal verbs with adverbial suffixes. Where we say "the moon rose above the river," they say something like "upward, behind the onstreaming, it mooned" (paraphrased) — "moon" is a verb, an event that happens. The northern languages build "things" by piling up adjectives: an object is just a momentary bundle of "round, bright, airy." Because the language has no slot for an independently existing object, the whole civilization cannot even conceive of matter.

Best of all, materialism on Tlön is a famous sophistry. Someone loses nine copper coins on Tuesday; on Thursday someone else finds a few on the road. To say they are the same coins and that they went on existing in between strikes the people of Tlön as absurd — nobody saw them exist. Another invention, the hrönir: on Tlön, two people searching for the same lost pencil can each "find" one. Expectation manufactures objects.

The turn, and the point. The story's postscript is dated 1947, years after the book appeared — a deliberate anachronism. It reports that artifacts from Tlön have begun turning up in the real world, the full forty volumes have been found, and schools are teaching Tlön's language and history: "the world will be Tlön." Why do people give in? Because, the narrator says, any system with a semblance of order — dialectical materialism, anti-Semitism, Nazism — was enough to entrance the minds of men; how could they not submit to an entire orderly planet? (paraphrased). He adds the crucial line: Tlön may be a labyrinth, but it is a labyrinth devised by men, destined to be deciphered by men — whereas the order of the real world we will never see through.

Why it matters / how it changes your view. Written in 1940, this is one of the coolest diagnoses of ideology ever made: people are not conquered by lies so much as by stories that are tidier than reality. The real world is messy, contradictory, never fully explained; a self-consistent system — political, corporate, conspiratorial — offers cognitive comfort. The next time a theory that explains everything has you hooked, ask: is it compelling because it is true, or because it is neat?

2. The Library of Babel: having everything is having nothing ("The Library of Babel")

What it is. The universe is a library of endless hexagonal galleries. Every book has 410 pages, each page 40 lines, each line about 80 characters, drawn from just 25 symbols (22 letters, the space, the comma and the period). The library holds every possible arrangement of those symbols — each exactly once.

The mechanism: combinatorial explosion. At first it sounds like paradise. If every combination is there, then somewhere on a shelf sits every true book: a detailed diary of your tomorrow, the answer to every riddle, the faithful story of your life. The librarians are ecstatic. But the same logic guarantees that next to the true book stand countless copies that differ by a single letter, countless books "proving" the true one false — and that the overwhelming majority of volumes are gibberish (the narrator's father once saw a book that was nothing but the letters MCV repeated to the end). People have done the math: the library holds 25 to the power of 1,312,000 books (410 × 40 × 80 character slots, 25 options each). The truth is in there — and you can never tell it apart from its infinite counterfeits.

So the librarians' history slides from euphoria into despair. Some hunt for the legendary catalog of catalogs (which exists in endless false versions). "Purifiers" destroy "useless" books by the million (each burned book has near-identical twins). Suicides multiply. The narrator's final consolation is a conjecture: the library is "unlimited and periodic" — an eternal traveler walking in any direction would, after centuries, find the same volumes repeating in the same disorder, and that repetition would itself be an order (paraphrased).

The analogy. Today the obvious parallel is the internet and generative AI: the sheer volume of text approaches "everything," but information is not meaning; meaning comes from filtering. A database containing every possible statement contains, as knowledge, nothing — because knowledge is fundamentally the result of ruling things out.

How it changes your view. You start to see that value lies not in having it all but in rejecting most of it. A good book is good precisely because its author threw away countless other ways of writing it; a judgment is useful because it excludes other judgments. Completeness is not the end point of knowledge. Choice is.

3. Branching time: every possibility happens at once ("The Garden of Forking Paths")

The plot. 1916, mid-war. Yu Tsun, a Chinese man spying for Germany in England, has been exposed; the British officer Richard Madden is closing in. Before he is caught he must get one word to Berlin: the name of the French town where a British artillery park is massed — Albert. He flips through the phone book, finds a man with that name, the sinologist Stephen Albert, and takes a train to see him.

Albert, as it happens, studies Yu Tsun's great-grandfather Ts'ui Pên — a provincial governor who resigned his office vowing to write a novel and build a labyrinth, and who left behind only a chaotic, self-contradicting manuscript. Albert has solved the riddle: the book is the labyrinth, and the labyrinth is made of time. In an ordinary novel, a man facing several options picks one and discards the rest; in Ts'ui Pên's, he picks all of them at once — each choice splits time into separate futures, and those futures split again. That is why a hero dies in chapter three and is alive in chapter four. Ts'ui Pên's note, "I leave to several futures (not to all) my garden of forking paths," refers to time, not space (paraphrased). Albert adds that the whole vast work is a riddle whose answer is "time" — which is why the word "time" never appears in it, just as the one word forbidden in a riddle whose answer is "chess" is "chess."

Then Yu Tsun shoots Albert dead. The next day the papers report that the sinologist Stephen Albert was murdered by a stranger, and his handler in Berlin reads the message: bomb Albert. Yu Tsun is arrested and condemned, and speaks of his "innumerable contrition and weariness" (paraphrased). At the very moment he grasps that time forks without end, he chooses the one path that can never be undone.

Why it matters. The story is often set beside the "many-worlds interpretation" of quantum mechanics that the physicist Hugh Everett proposed in 1957 — the idea that every quantum measurement splits the universe into branches where each possible outcome comes true. Borges got there first, but as a literary intuition, not a physics prediction. Its real force is ethical: if every possibility is realized somewhere else, does this choice still weigh anything? The story's answer is cold. For the person actually living, only one path is real — and on this one he killed a man.

How it changes your view. It hands you a counterfactual lens: every stretch of history, every decision, is one line selected from a field of unrealized alternatives. You take what happened a little less for granted — and your own choices a little more seriously.

4. You must forget in order to think ("Funes the Memorious")

What it is. Ireneo Funes, a young man in a small Uruguayan town, is thrown from a horse and paralyzed — and wakes with perfect perception and perfect memory. He remembers the shape of every cloud at every instant; he can reconstruct an entire day, but reconstructing a day takes a whole day. "My memory, sir," he says, "is like a garbage heap" (paraphrased).

The mechanism: abstraction requires discarding. Funes is troubled by something strange: he cannot see why the single word "dog" should cover so many individual dogs of different shapes and sizes. Worse, the dog seen in profile at 3:14 and the dog seen from the front at 3:15 share one name. He even plans a language in which every stone and every branch would have its own proper name. The narrator delivers one of the most quoted lines in the book: "To think is to forget differences, to generalize, to abstract. In the teeming world of Funes there were only details, almost immediate in their presence" (paraphrased). Funes dies of pulmonary congestion in 1889, at twenty-one. A man who remembered everything thought nothing through.

Analogy and real case. There really are people close to Funes. The Soviet psychologist Alexander Luria spent decades studying the mnemonist Solomon Shereshevsky, who could retain long strings of meaningless numbers for years yet struggled with poetry and metaphor and had trouble grasping the gist of a passage. Machine learning has a name for the same failure: overfitting — a model memorizes every speck of noise in its training data and so cannot generalize to anything new. Funes is an overfitted brain.

How it changes your view. We treat forgetting as a defect. Borges flips it: forgetting is the precondition of thought, not its price. Every concept, law and theory is bought by deliberately throwing away detail. It also explains why distillation is worth anything: understanding is not remembering everything, but knowing what to let go.

5. The same words in another age are another work ("Pierre Menard, Author of the Quixote")

What it is. The story is written as a fussy, eulogizing review of the work of an invented twentieth-century French writer, Pierre Menard. His masterpiece: he produced, word for word, chapters nine and thirty-eight of Part One of Cervantes's Don Quixote, plus a fragment of chapter twenty-two. Not by copying. He first considered becoming Cervantes — learning Spanish, recovering the Catholic faith, fighting Moors and Turks, forgetting European history after 1602 — then rejected that as too easy, and resolved to arrive at the same text as Pierre Menard, carrying all his own experience.

The mechanism: meaning is generated by context. The reviewer earnestly compares two identical passages. Cervantes wrote: "…truth, whose mother is history, rival of time, storehouse of deeds, witness of the past, exemplar and adviser to the present, and the future's counselor" (paraphrased). From a seventeenth-century Cervantes, this is mere rhetorical praise of history. From Menard — a contemporary of the pragmatist philosopher William James — "history, the mother of truth" is an astonishing claim: truth is not what history discovers but what history produces. Menard's style, the reviewer adds, is archaic and affected, whereas Cervantes simply wrote the Spanish of his day.

At the end, the reviewer announces that Menard has given us a new technique of reading: "deliberate anachronism and erroneous attribution" — read the Imitation of Christ, the medieval devotional classic, as if it were by Céline or Joyce (paraphrased).

Why it matters. This is a revolution in literary theory in a few thousand words, decades ahead of Roland Barthes's "death of the author" and reader-response criticism (the school holding that a work's meaning is completed only in the act of reading). It nails down an intuition: a text is not a container holding meaning; meaning happens where the text collides with a reader and an era.

How it changes your view. You start listening for who is speaking and when. "We must respect tradition" from an 1800 reactionary and from a 2020 reformer are two different sentences. Every time you quote, repost or reread a classic, you are doing what Menard did: writing a new work out of the same words.

6. Labyrinths and detectives: how reason becomes a trap ("Death and the Compass," "The Circular Ruins," "The Lottery in Babylon")

"Death and the Compass" is an anti-detective story. The detective Lönnrot is a pure rationalist who disdains ordinary explanations. A Jewish scholar is murdered in a hotel; Inspector Treviranus says a thief probably got the wrong room (he is right). Lönnrot notices that the victim studied the Kabbalah (Jewish mysticism), finds a note reading "The first letter of the Name has been spoken," and decides this is a series of sacrifices keyed to the four letters of the name of God. Two more killings follow, on the third of successive months, at points forming an equilateral triangle on the map. Lönnrot calculates the fourth point and date and goes alone to an abandoned villa outside the city — where the killer, Red Scharlach, is waiting. The first death really was an accident. Scharlach read about Lönnrot's kabbalistic theory in the papers and staged everything after it according to Lönnrot's own reasoning, precisely to lure him there.

The mechanism is strikingly modern: the cleverest people are the easiest to snare with a pattern. The better you are at finding regularities, the easier it is for an opponent to feed you one. Before he dies, Lönnrot says that Scharlach's labyrinth has three lines too many; he knows of a Greek labyrinth that is a single straight line (an allusion to Zeno's paradox — to cross any distance you must first cross half, then half the rest, and so on forever). Scharlach promises him that labyrinth the next time he kills him (paraphrased).

"The Circular Ruins": a man comes to the ruins of a circular temple, determined to dream a complete human being and set him loose in the world. After immense labor he succeeds, and erases his son's memory of where he came from. At the end a fire sweeps in and the flames do not burn him — and "with relief, with humiliation, with terror" he understands that he too is an illusion, being dreamed by someone else. The infinite regress of creators and created removes reality's last foundation stone.

"The Lottery in Babylon": an ancient state's lottery evolves from paying out money to also handing out penalties, then becomes compulsory for everyone, with draws deciding promotion, prison, death — until every event of life is decided by lot, and no one can tell whether the Company running it exists at all. "Like all men in Babylon," the narrator says, "I have been a proconsul; like all, a slave" (paraphrased). Once chance is fully institutionalized, chance and order can no longer be told apart. How much of what you call "deserved" in your own society is a lottery nobody calls a lottery?

Taken together, how they change your view: the labyrinth is the book's master image. It is the shape of the world (too big, too intricate) and also the shape of human reason (the tools we use against complexity are themselves complex). Borges is no enemy of reason. He is a skeptic who loves reason deeply and sees its limits with total clarity.

The Skeleton

The whole book on one line:

1. Starting point: the world is inexhaustible to us. The library's combinations, the lottery's chance, the forking of time — reality vastly exceeds what any mind can process.

2. Our response: we build order. Encyclopedias, taxonomies, detective reasoning, historical narrative, memory, authorship — threads we run through the labyrinth.

3. But order is fiction, and it bites back. It can be more seductive than reality (Tlön), turned against us by an enemy (Death and the Compass), drowned in its own completeness (Babel), or paralyze thought (Funes).

4. Borges's stance: enjoy the fiction with your eyes open. He is not saying "everything is fake, so nothing matters." He is saying: we can only live in the labyrinths we build, so build them beautifully — and never forget that we built them. How does the book "prove" this? Not by argument, but by walking you into a premise, again and again, and letting you watch it march, with flawless logic, into absurdity. Each story is a thought experiment; the reader draws the conclusion.

The form makes the same argument. Fake reviews, fake footnotes, real people's names stirred into invention: the border between true and false is deliberately worn smooth. Reading the book is itself an exercise in finding your way through a library where fact and fiction can't be told apart.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "Borges is a postmodern relativist who thinks nothing is real." Not quite. He is a skeptic, but he never cheerfully declares that truth doesn't exist; he has a strong nostalgia for order. He once said he tended to value religious and philosophical ideas for their aesthetic worth (paraphrased) — appreciating philosophy as art, not declaring philosophy void. The ending of "The Garden of Forking Paths" makes the point: for the living, the consequences of a choice are real.

Misreading 2: "Borges predicted the internet / hypertext / many-worlds physics." Popular, but discount it. The Library of Babel is a superb metaphor for information overload, and pioneers of hypertext fiction did cite "The Garden of Forking Paths" as an ancestor. But Borges was working on old philosophical problems — meaning, time, infinity — and technology simply made his metaphors look familiar after the fact. Reading him as a tech prophet makes him smaller.

Misreading 3: "It's just clever puzzles with no feeling." The surface is cold; underneath lie loneliness and mortality. Funes is a young man trapped in his own body and memory. In "The Secret Miracle," a playwright condemned to a Nazi firing squad, Jaromir Hladík, asks God for a year to finish his play — and between the order to fire and the bullets, time stops for him alone for a full year. He finishes the play in his head, and dies. This is feeling held in check to the breaking point, not the absence of feeling.

Criticism 1: cold, bloodless, cut off from society. Many critics note that the stories have almost no rounded characters, that women are nearly absent from Ficciones, and that poverty, class and Latin America's political realities barely enter the frame. In the 1960s and '70s, parts of the Latin American left accused him of escapism and Eurocentrism. The charge has teeth: his labyrinths are in the library, not in the street.

Criticism 2: the East as stage set. The Yunnan governor, the labyrinth and the Chinese spy in "The Garden of Forking Paths" are the Orient as imagined from a Western study. Through a postcolonial lens — Edward Said's "Orientalism," the West's habit of casting the East as a mysterious, exotic other — the exoticizing is plain.

Criticism 3: his politics. He opposed Perón, and by his own account was "promoted" in 1946 from a municipal library post to inspector of poultry and rabbits in a public market, as a humiliation. But in 1976 he accepted an honor from Pinochet's regime in Chile and publicly praised Argentina's military junta, only later turning to condemn its "disappearances." It is widely believed this cost him the Nobel Prize, though that cannot be proved.

Criticism 4: the same props, over and over. Mirrors, labyrinths, libraries, tigers, infinity — the toolbox is small, and read in bulk the stories can feel like variations on one machine. His defenders reply that this is the method: arranging a finite set of symbols into endless variation, exactly like the Library of Babel.

Borges Beyond This Book

The Aleph (1949) is the companion collection, and it pushes infinity further. In the title story, the Aleph is a point of light a couple of centimeters across in a cellar, in which every place in the universe can be seen at once from every angle — and the narrator's problem is that language is sequential while what he saw was simultaneous; writing cannot hold the infinite. "The Immortal" is harsher still: a race granted eternal life sinks into apathy, because in infinite time everyone eventually does and suffers everything. Immortality drains every act of weight; it is death that makes human action precious. ("I have been Homer; shortly, like Ulysses, I shall be no one" — paraphrased.)

His essay "Kafka and His Precursors" makes an influential claim: every writer creates his own precursors. Only after reading Kafka do we recognize something "Kafkaesque" in passages by Zeno, Han Yu or Kierkegaard; without Kafka, those passages have nothing to do with one another. It is the Pierre Menard idea again: what comes later changes what came before. In another essay he cites (or invents) a "Chinese encyclopedia" that divides animals into categories such as "belonging to the Emperor," "embalmed" and "that from a long way off look like flies" — and Michel Foucault opened The Order of Things with his laughter at that passage, asking how arbitrary our own classifications are.

In "A New Refutation of Time" he uses idealist arguments to show that time does not exist, then overturns himself: "Time is the substance I am made of. Time is a river which sweeps me along, but I am the river… The world, unfortunately, is real; I, unfortunately, am Borges" (paraphrased). That line is nearly the key to his whole mind: he uses philosophy to push reality away, then honestly admits it won't budge. In the short piece "Borges and I," he splits the writer "Borges" from the "I" who lives, and ends by saying he does not know which of the two has written the page (paraphrased).

The Book in Ten Sentences

1. Writing a five-hundred-page book is a laborious extravagance; better to pretend the book exists and write its summary.

2. Metaphysics is a branch of fantastic literature — a philosophical system can be used as a story's premise, and tested by its plot.

3. People are not conquered by lies but by systems tidier than reality; Tlön is a man-made labyrinth, which is why we can read it — and why we surrender to it.

4. In a library holding every possible book, the truth is present but indistinguishable from its countless counterfeits: having everything is having nothing.

5. In Ts'ui Pên's novel every choice is made at once and time forks forever; but for the living, only this one path is real.

6. "To think is to forget differences, to generalize, to abstract" (paraphrased) — forgetting is not the price of thought but its precondition.

7. Menard wrote the Quixote word for word and produced a different work: meaning lives not in the words but in who says them, when, and to whom.

8. The cleverest detective dies of his own deduction: the better you are at finding patterns, the easier it is to feed you one.

9. The dreamer discovers he is dreamed; once chance is fully institutionalized, chance and order can no longer be told apart.

10. "The world, unfortunately, is real; I, unfortunately, am Borges" (paraphrased) — we can only live in labyrinths we build ourselves: build them beautifully, and never forget they are built.
