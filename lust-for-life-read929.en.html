Lust for Life

A Novel of Vincent van Gogh · Irving Stone · 1934

中文 →

In One Sentence

A biographical novel about a man who took up drawing at twenty-seven with almost no training, produced roughly two thousand works in the decade he had left, sold essentially nothing, was written off by his family and his century as a failure — and refused, the entire time, to paint anything that would sell. He painted miners, peasants, weavers, a postman, and his own increasingly ravaged face. What the book really poses is not "misunderstood genius" but a harder question: when a thing pays you nothing, wins you nothing, and makes everyone treat you as a lunatic, what keeps a person doing it?

Where It Sits

Irving Stone (1903–1989) more or less established the biographical novel in English. In 1927 he saw a Van Gogh exhibition in Paris, was knocked sideways by it, and afterwards physically retraced the route — the Netherlands, the Borinage coal district in Belgium, Paris, Arles, Saint-Rémy, Auvers. His primary source was Vincent's correspondence with his brother Theo: some six hundred letters, and the true skeleton of the book. The manuscript was rejected by more than a dozen publishers before appearing in 1934; it then sold steadily for decades, was filmed in 1956 with Kirk Douglas, and effectively defined Van Gogh for several generations.

So the first thing to be clear about is genre: this is a novel, not a scholarly biography. The dialogue is Stone's invention, timelines are compressed, figures merged, and some episodes — the early love story especially — are heavily dramatised. For strict fact, read the letters themselves, or Naifeh and Smith's 2011 Van Gogh: The Life, built on the Van Gogh Museum's archives. None of which diminishes the book: it remains the most forceful account of why a person would live this way. What follows distils what holds up against the record — and flags plainly where the novel has done its own work.

The Central Claims

  • Van Gogh was not a born genius; he was a career-changer. He decided to draw at twenty-seven, after failing as an art dealer's clerk, a bookshop assistant, a supply teacher and a trainee preacher. The decade that followed was not a decade of inspiration; it was a decade of drawing until his hand cramped.
  • His refusal to serve the market was not vanity but a stated position: truth outranks prettiness. He wanted the working hand, the face yellowed by an oil lamp, the back bent by soil — and he said repeatedly that he wanted the picture to smell of earth and labour.
  • His persistence was held up by an external umbilical cord. Theo sent money every month, sent paints, kept sending when nothing sold. No Theo, no Van Gogh — the fact the romantic version skips most reliably, and the one that matters most.

The Core Ideas, One by One

The Borinage: failing all the way down to nothing, then growing from the bottom

The heaviest passage in the book, and the one that explains everything after it, is the Belgian coal district of the Borinage (1878–1880). Van Gogh came from a pastor's family, wanted to preach, could not pass the entrance examinations for theology in Amsterdam, fell back on a missionary training course in Brussels, and was finally posted to the mines as a lay evangelist.

What he did there exceeded everyone's expectations of the role: he gave away his clothes, his bed and his money to the miners, slept on straw, smeared his face with coal dust, and lived worse than the men he served. The result was that the evangelical committee dismissed him — the stated grounds amounting to an excess of zeal unbecoming the dignity of the office. That is documented history, not a novelist's invention.

Why does it matter so much? Because it establishes, in one stroke, the temperament that governs the next ten years: he had no capacity for moderation. Whatever he undertook he undertook until he had burned himself hollow. He could not understand why helping the poor required decorum, exactly as he would later fail to understand why painting peasants required making them attractive. Being fired by the church was the hinge of his life: it closed off every remaining respectable way of earning a living, and it was at that bottom, owning nothing, that he picked up a pencil. In 1880, aged twenty-seven, he wrote to Theo that he was going to be an artist.

How this changes the story: "artist" was not his first choice; it was the last road left after all the others had dead-ended. That is a truer and far more useful account than the one where he was always destined to paint. A great deal of real commitment begins not in enthusiasm but in the exhaustion of alternatives.

The Theo cord: purity was purchased by somebody else

This is the book's second protagonist and the point most worth making explicit. Theo van Gogh, four years younger, managed a branch of the Goupil gallery and was one of the better-informed dealers in Paris. From 1880 until Vincent's death, Theo sent money nearly every month — a stipend that varied by period but broadly covered subsistence and materials — along with canvas, paint, books, and the effort of getting works into exhibitions. Across ten years, almost nothing came back.

The letters matter as much as the money. Vincent wrote Theo more than six hundred letters; with the rest of the correspondence the total approaches nine hundred, making it the most complete first-person record any major artist has left: complementary colour theory, Millet and Rembrandt, what he painted today, how much of the month's money is left, and unguarded admissions of illness and despair. That correspondence is the real parent text of Stone's novel, and the reason a book with invented dialogue still points, emotionally, in the right direction.

Naming the cord matters because it rewrites the economics of the "art above all" myth: Vincent could hold out against the market for ten years only because someone else was absorbing the part where you give in to it. Theo spent his days selling saleable pictures and his evenings writing to a brother who refused to make any. Those are not two facts but one. And the arrangement proved fatal in both directions: Vincent died in July 1890, and Theo died six months later, in January 1891.

A third person has to be added, because almost every romantic telling loses her: Theo's widow, Johanna van Gogh-Bonger. Left with a husband and a brother-in-law both dead, several hundred unsellable paintings and a crate of letters, she catalogued, translated and published the correspondence and pushed the work into exhibition after exhibition for the rest of her life. Genius is not automatically discovered. Van Gogh's posthumous fame was assembled, painting by painting, by a woman who spent decades doing it.

The Potato Eaters: what "refusing the market" concretely means

Saying "he didn't pander" is useless in the abstract; you need one picture. In Nuenen in 1885 Van Gogh painted what he considered his first real work: The Potato Eaters — five peasants around a small oil lamp eating potatoes, faces coarse, knuckles swollen and deformed, the whole canvas dark green, grey-brown and the colour of soil. There is scarcely an attractive square inch in it.

He stated the intention very clearly in his letters (paraphrased): he wanted it visible that these people, who dig the earth with those hands, are eating with the same hands what they dug; he wanted the picture to smell of bacon, smoke and potato steam. A painting of peasants that came out perfumed and tidy, he said, would be the bad painting. Peasants should be painted as peasants.

That is the concrete content of "refusal." To see how far against the current it ran, note what the market wanted: the 1880s salon taste ran to smoothly finished history painting, exotica, sweet landscapes and flattering portraits, and even the Impressionists were selling bright, pleasurable cities and day trips. Van Gogh submitted something so dark the faces are hard to read and so unhandsome it makes viewers uncomfortable. Sympathetic colleagues told him to soften it. He did not.

His fixed star was Jean-François Millet, the French painter of The Gleaners and The Sower, who painted nothing but peasants at work. Van Gogh treated Millet as his real teacher his whole life, copied him repeatedly, and said Millet painted not peasants but the soil itself. How it changes your eye: after this painting you stop reading the coarseness in Van Gogh as insufficient skill and start reading it as a position — to prettify the poor, in his view, is to lie about them. It holds all the way through: the postman Roulin at Arles, the old peasants, the weavers, and the utterly unsparing self-portraits. Not one of them is trying to be liked.

The colour revolution: from soil to sunlight, from seen to felt

If you keep one technical idea from this book, keep this. The two years from 1886 in Paris, living with Theo, are the great hinge: he collided with Impressionism (Monet, Pissarro) and Neo-Impressionism (Seurat's pointillism), met Gauguin and Toulouse-Lautrec, and fell hard for the Japanese woodblock prints then flooding Europe — flat brilliant colour, daring cropped compositions, clean contours.

Ten years, three jumps: Dutch earth tones, the Paris palette blowing open, then Arles, where colour becomes emotion.

In 1888 he went south to Arles, and the Provençal light pushed the whole thing to its limit. There he wrote one of the weightiest technical statements in modern painting (paraphrased): in The Night Café, I tried to express with red and green the terrible passions of humanity. Note what that sentence does. He is not reporting that the café was red and green. He is saying he chose red against green because the collision of those two makes a viewer uneasy.

This is a watershed in the history of painting. Impressionism asks "what light does my eye receive?" Van Gogh asks "what do I feel, and which colour will hit hardest?" Colour stops recording and starts asserting. That is why he is called a forerunner of Expressionism — the position that a work shows not the objective look of the world but the world after the artist's inner state has been pressed onto it. The swirling sky of The Starry Night is not a report that the night above Saint-Rémy was rotating; it is a report that it rotated in him as he looked.

The touch matured alongside the colour: impasto, paint squeezed almost straight from the tube and pushed with the brush, leaving visible ridges. The effect is to fix the act of painting permanently on the surface — you are not only looking at a wheatfield, you are looking at how one man's hand moved in front of it on a particular afternoon. It is also why reproductions fall so far short of the originals: what a print loses is exactly that thickness.

The Yellow House and the ear: a utopia that failed

In Arles in 1888 Van Gogh rented the Yellow House intending to found a Studio of the South — a community of painters living, working and pooling income together. It was the most outward-facing gesture of his life: he did not want solo fame, he wanted colleagues. To welcome Gauguin he painted the famous sunflowers, specifically as decoration for Gauguin's room.

Gauguin arrived in October 1888. They lasted about two months, quarrelling steadily — their positions were opposites, Gauguin arguing for painting from memory and imagination, Van Gogh insisting on working in front of the subject. On 23 December came the endlessly retold event: Van Gogh cut off part of his left ear (how much, and exactly how it happened, is still disputed), wrapped it and delivered it to a woman at a brothel. Gauguin left and never came back.

This is the most romanticised moment in the story and the one most in need of unpacking. Told as "genius maddened by love," it loses its context; put back, it looks like a collapsed collaboration compounded by a psychiatric crisis. The project into which he had poured every hope failed on the spot, and he did have a recurring illness whose diagnosis remains unsettled — temporal lobe epilepsy, bipolar disorder, porphyria and absinthe poisoning have all been proposed and none established. He then spent a year in the asylum at Saint-Rémy, where The Starry Night was painted, from his window plus imagination.

How it changes the way you read him: do not treat the ear as the source of the art. During attacks he could not paint at all — his letters state plainly how long he was unable to work afterwards. The paintings were made in the lucid intervals, at high speed and with enormous self-discipline. The illness was the enemy of the work, not its fuel.

Two thousand works in ten years: this is output, not inspiration

Put the numbers down and the romance changes shape. Van Gogh's entire career lasted about ten years (1880–1890) and produced roughly two thousand works, of which more than eight hundred are oil paintings — a large share of them crammed into the final two years, when at Arles and Saint-Rémy he often finished one or two canvases a day.

That volume is not explained by talent; it was drilled. He began barely able to draw and worked page by page through the standard training manuals of the day — the Bargue drawing course, a graded set of plates to be copied — drawing hands, and diggers, and plaster casts, over and over. He read colour theory systematically and wound coloured wool around small boards to test how complementaries behaved side by side. Here is the most counter-intuitive and most transferable lesson in the book: the man held up as the specimen of the mad genius actually pushed himself from zero to mastery in ten years by nearly plodding, unbroken daily repetition.

His own account (paraphrased) is not mystical either: rather than wait for inspiration, start working — inspiration arrives inside the working. And the more famous line: "If you hear a voice within you saying you cannot paint, then by all means paint, and that voice will be silenced." (paraphrased)

"I want to touch people": what he was actually trying to do

The last idea is what the book is finally transmitting. Van Gogh never said he wanted to be a great painter. He said something else (paraphrased): what am I in the eyes of most people — a nobody, an oddity. Very well: I want my work to show what there is in the heart of such a nobody.

That sentence accounts for every choice he made: why miners and peasants (they too are nobodies); why no flattery (what must be shown is what is in the heart, not what is on the face); why impasto and jarring colour (the effect he wanted was to be affected, not to be pleased). His success criterion was never "sold" but "did anyone feel something in front of this." By that criterion he never failed a single day.

In July 1890, at Auvers-sur-Oise north of Paris, he was shot and died two days later at thirty-seven, with Theo at the bedside. As Theo reported them, his last words were, in substance: "the sadness will last forever." The attribution cannot be fully verified, but it has served ever since as the story's signature.

The Argument in Outline

Compressed, the book traces a failure turning "not wanted" into "impossible to ignore" over ten years:

  • Sixteen to twenty-seven — continuous failure. Art dealer's clerk (dismissed), bookshop assistant, supply teacher, theology (could not pass the exams), evangelist (dismissed). By twenty-seven he is, in every social sense, finished.
  • Twenty-seven — he starts at the bottom. Not from discovering a gift but from running out of roads; and the months of living alongside the Borinage miners hand him the only subject he will ever want — people who work.
  • Five Dutch years — technique built from nothing, position staked out. The Potato Eaters is both the product and the manifesto: truth over prettiness.
  • Two Paris years — collision with modern painting; the palette detonates. Impressionism gives him light, the Japanese prints give him composition, Gauguin gives him the illusion of a colleague.
  • Two and a half years, Arles to Auvers — colour becomes emotion; output and illness climb together. The Yellow House fails, the ear, the asylum, The Starry Night, the wheatfields, and then the gunshot at Auvers.
  • Afterwards — Theo dead within six months; Johanna spending decades pushing the letters and the paintings into the world. Van Gogh is now among the most valuable and most recognised painters alive or dead — an outcome he had nothing to do with, completed for him by someone else.

What it establishes is one thing only: a person can carry a pursuit to its limit with no external feedback at all, provided somebody else absorbs reality on their behalf — and whether the trade was worth it, the book declines to decide for you.

Misreadings & Serious Objections

Misreading one, and the important one: treating the novel as evidence. Stone wrote biographical fiction. The dialogue is composed, events are compressed and reordered, and the early London infatuation with the landlady's daughter (called Ursula in the book; usually identified as Eugénie Loyer) is heavily dramatised and its factual basis long questioned. Any passage that reads with especially smooth dramatic timing deserves a query in the margin. For facts, go to the letters.

Misreading two: "he sold only one painting in his life." The claim refers to The Red Vineyard, bought by the Belgian painter Anna Boch for 400 francs in 1890. Strictly it is not accurate: other works changed hands through exchange, gift or small transactions, and Theo placed some. The precise statement is that he had almost no market, not that the lifetime count was mathematically one.

Misreading three: "he died completely unrecognised." Not so. In January 1890 the critic Albert Aurier published a long, ardent article on him in the Mercure de France; that same year his work hung at Les XX in Brussels, where The Red Vineyard sold. He died on the threshold of recognition rather than in total darkness — a detail that makes the story crueller and also truer.

Misreading four, the one most worth dismantling: "genius and suffering are inseparable," "the madness produced the art." This book and the film made from it are the main vectors of that myth. Lay out the material instead: his attacks stopped him working; his technique came from systematic training; his mature colour rests on a clear-headed study of colour theory (the complementary collisions were calculated, not hallucinated). The closer statement is that suffering did not produce his art; his art was forced out through the gaps between bouts of suffering. Binding the two together makes a better story at the cost of sanctifying pain while badly underestimating the near-industrial labour of that decade.

The open question: was it suicide? The standard account has him shooting himself in a wheatfield. In 2011 Steven Naifeh and Gregory White Smith proposed an alternative: that he may have been shot accidentally or in horseplay by local boys and chose not to say so. The thesis has supporters and forceful critics — researchers at the Van Gogh Museum have publicly challenged the strength of the evidence — and it remains unresolved. The honest formulation: suicide is still the mainstream view, but it is not beyond dispute.

A further criticism of the book itself: its portrait worked too well. Stone's ardent, pure, world-wronged Vincent has so thoroughly captured the popular imagination that it obscures other things equally true — that his relations with his family were long and badly strained and partly his own doing; that the period living with the pregnant prostitute Sien Hoornik (which the novel does include) was far messier and more painful than any novelised version; that he did real damage to people around him. Making a man into a saint and making him into a madman are the same simplification.

Ten Sentences

1. Van Gogh decided to draw at twenty-seven, after every previous occupation had failed — "artist" was not his first choice but the only road left when the others had dead-ended.

2. In the Borinage he gave his clothes, bed and money to the miners and slept on straw; the church dismissed him for a zeal unbecoming the office. He never did learn moderation, at anything.

3. He could hold out against the market for ten years because Theo sent money every month: a brother selling saleable pictures to support a brother who refused to make any. Those are one fact, not two.

4. The six hundred-plus letters to Theo are the most complete first-person record any major artist has left — the parent text of this novel, and more trustworthy than the novel.

5. The Potato Eaters is the manifesto: he wanted the canvas to smell of bacon, smoke and potato steam, and to show hands that dug the earth eating what they dug (paraphrased). Prettifying the poor, to him, was lying about them.

6. Paris blew the palette open and the Arles sun finished the job: "I tried to express with red and green the terrible passions of humanity" (paraphrased). Colour stopped recording what the eye saw and started stating what he felt — the doorway to Expressionism.

7. The ear was not romance: it was the on-the-spot collapse of the Yellow House community plus a psychiatric crisis still undiagnosed — and during attacks he could not paint. The illness was the enemy of the work, not its fuel.

8. Ten years, about two thousand works, eight hundred-odd oils, most crowded into the final two years — built from copying the Bargue plates and drawing hands and diggers over and over. Plodding work took him from zero, not revelation.

9. "If you hear a voice within you saying you cannot paint, then by all means paint, and that voice will be silenced" (paraphrased). His measure of success was never a sale but whether anyone stood in front of the picture and felt something.

10. He died on the threshold of recognition — a glowing review and a sale in the six months before — and Theo died six months after him; what actually carried him to the world was Theo's widow Johanna, spending decades editing the letters and placing the paintings. Genius is never discovered automatically.
