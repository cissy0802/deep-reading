What We Owe the Future

What We Owe the Future · William MacAskill · 2022

中文 →

In One Sentence

If humanity doesn't destroy itself this century, the people who come after us could outnumber everyone alive today thousands of times over. They haven't been born yet, but they are people just as we are, and what we choose now (whether we go extinct, which values we "lock in") may decide what kind of lives they get to live. So positively influencing the long-term future is one of the most important moral tasks of our time. That is longtermism.

Where It Sits

MacAskill is an Oxford moral philosopher. With Toby Ord he started the effective altruism movement, which uses evidence and careful reasoning to find the ways each unit of resources can do the most good. This book is the popular manifesto for longtermism. This page also folds in Ord's The Precipice (2020), which puts numbers on existential risk. The two books belong to the same framework.

The Core Argument

The whole book rests on three premises, which MacAskill compresses into one line (paraphrased): future people count; there could be a great many of them; and we can make their lives go better.

  • Future people count. Distance in time, like distance in space, doesn't reduce the weight of someone's suffering.
  • The future could be vast. As long as humanity survives, the number of future people will dwarf everyone who has lived so far, on any reasonable estimate.
  • We live at an unusually pivotal moment. Technology has given us, for the first time, the power to wipe out humanity (nuclear weapons, engineered pathogens, misaligned AI). It may also let us, for the first time, fix one set of values in place for millennia. So avoiding the end of civilization and improving its trajectory both carry staggering expected value.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Distance in time is not moral distance

What it is. This is longtermism's starting point and its hardest step to refute. MacAskill's example (paraphrased): if you break a glass bottle on a hiking trail, you should clean up the shards, and the harm is the same whether the child who cuts her foot walks by next week or a hundred years from now. Nobody would say the cut a century from now doesn't count.

The book opens with a thought experiment. Imagine living, in order of birth, the life of every human who has ever lived, a hundred-odd billion of them, from the earliest hunter-gatherers through farmers, slaves and factory workers to the present day. Then ask: if humanity lasts a long time, you are still only at the start of the film. Seen this way, the pain and joy of future people are no less real than what you're living through right now.

Why it matters. It challenges a habit economists take for granted, pure time preference: discounting future welfare simply because it lies in the future. Discount at 1% a year and a life five hundred years from now is worth less than a hundredth of a life today. As early as 1928 the mathematician and economist Frank Ramsey called such discounting "ethically indefensible," arising "merely from the weakness of the imagination." Keep one distinction clear: discounting because the future is uncertain is reasonable; discounting because it is late is not.

How it changes your view. It turns intergenerational justice from a slogan into arithmetic. You start noticing how often the "costs" in a policy debate are bills mailed to people who have no vote and no voice.

2. The scale of the future: humanity as a reckless teenager

What it is. Homo sapiens has been around for roughly 300,000 years, a typical mammalian species lasts about a million, the earth will stay habitable for hundreds of millions more, and if we leave the planet the horizon stretches further still. MacAskill estimates (paraphrased) that even if we last only as long as a typical mammal species and stay at today's population, future people will outnumber those alive today by about ten thousand to one.

His metaphor is that humanity is an imprudent teenager. Some of a teenager's decisions, like dropping out of school or racing a car, have consequences that last a lifetime, and teenagers often don't realize it. We are in humanity's adolescence: our power has suddenly grown, and our wisdom hasn't caught up.

Why it matters. Once you accept that distance in time is not moral distance, multiplying by this scale gives a strong conclusion: anything that makes humanity's long-term future even slightly better may carry more expected value than almost anything that affects only the present. This is what separates longtermism from ordinary environmentalism or "thinking of our grandchildren." It isn't thinking about grandchildren; it's thinking about a million years.

How it changes your view. It changes your sense of where we stand in history. We are not at the end of history but very likely near its beginning, and things that happen at the beginning tend to be the hardest to undo and the furthest-reaching.

3. Significance × persistence × contingency: how to value an effect on the future

What it is. Good intentions aren't enough to change the long-term future. You have to ask three questions, which MacAskill calls the SPC framework:

  • Significance: how much better does this make the world at any given moment?
  • Persistence: how long will the change last?
  • Contingency: would it have happened anyway without your effort? If it would arrive sooner or later regardless, you've only brought it forward a little.

The long-term value of a change is roughly the product of the three. Something hugely significant but bound to happen anyway, such as a technology that would inevitably be invented, has little long-term value. Something modest that will last a thousand years and hinges entirely on chance can be worth an astonishing amount.

Example: abolition was not inevitable. We tend to see the abolition of slavery as the natural fruit of economic progress. Drawing on historians such as Christopher Brown, MacAskill argues that it was largely the contingent product of a moral and cultural movement. The slave trade was still profitable, and British abolition was driven by a small band of Quakers and campaigners. The book gives special attention to Benjamin Lay, a hunchbacked eighteenth-century Quaker radical who once hid a bladder of red pokeberry juice inside a hollowed-out book and ran it through with a sword at a public meeting, spattering slaveholders with "blood." Almost no one in his day thought he had a point.

How it changes your view. It reminds you that moral views we now take for granted may have come within a hair of never appearing. Conversely, some of today's eccentric minorities may be the source of the next moral advance. That makes changing values a badly underrated long-term investment.

4. Value lock-in: while the glass is still molten

What it is. Value lock-in is when one set of values gets fixed in place and becomes hard to change for a very long time. MacAskill's metaphor is glassblowing (paraphrased): while glass is molten it can be blown into any shape, but once it cools it sets, and the only way to change it is to shatter it. Human culture is still relatively hot. Many value systems compete and things can still change, but some events could cool it quickly.

His historical example is China's Warring States era, the "Hundred Schools of Thought," when Confucians, Mohists, Daoists and Legalists competed. After the Han dynasty elevated Confucianism to sole orthodoxy, it remained the official ideology for roughly two thousand years. A school of thought that wins during one window can shape a civilization for an extremely long time.

The lock-in mechanism that worries him most is artificial general intelligence (AGI). Leaders die and ideas are diluted as generations turn over, which is why historically no set of values has stayed fixed forever. A regime or group in control of AGI could, in principle, have a set of goals carried out forever by systems that never tire and never die.

Why it matters. It raises the prospect that a future without extinction could still be terrible: humanity survives, but stays locked into bad or merely imperfect values for millions of years. Hence MacAskill's advice: until we're confident about what is right, keep the world "morally exploratory", with free speech, cultural diversity, competition among ideas and dispersed power, so that moral mistakes can still be corrected.

How it changes your view. It recasts tolerance and pluralism. They are not just good values; they are an error-correction mechanism. When you aren't sure what's right, keeping the ability to fix mistakes matters more than winning the current argument.

5. Existential risk: the gulf between 99% and 100%

What it is. An existential risk is one that would destroy humanity's long-term potential, through extinction or an unrecoverable collapse. The philosopher Derek Parfit left a famous comparison of three outcomes: (1) peace; (2) a nuclear war that kills 99% of people; (3) a nuclear war that kills 100%. Most people think the biggest gap is between (1) and (2). Parfit said the gap between (2) and (3) is far greater, because (3) kills not only everyone alive but also wipes out everyone who could ever have lived afterward.

In The Precipice, Ord puts numbers on these risks. These are his own subjective estimates for this century, which he stresses are rough judgments, not measurements:

Source of riskOrd's estimated chance of existential catastrophe this century
Unaligned artificial intelligence~1 in 10
Engineered pandemics~1 in 30
Nuclear war~1 in 1,000
Extreme climate change~1 in 1,000
All natural risks (asteroids, supervolcanoes, stellar explosions…)~1 in 10,000
Total (including other anthropogenic risks)~1 in 6

Toby Ord's subjective estimates in The Precipice (2020). The point is less the exact figures than the ratio: human-made risk dwarfs natural risk.

An analogy. Ord says we stand at the edge of a precipice. Humanity got through 300,000 years because natural risk is low, but since the mid-twentieth century we have created risks many times greater than nature's, with almost no matching safety culture.

MacAskill adds an analysis of collapse versus extinction. He thinks that even if civilization collapsed and nine-tenths of humanity died, we would probably recover. There is one catch: we have already burned through much of the easily reached coal and oil, and the first Industrial Revolution ran on exactly that shallow coal. So he counts rapid decarbonization, which leaves fossil fuels in the ground, as a kind of insurance: if civilization falls, our descendants still have a ladder to climb back up.

How it changes your view. It reorders the world's problems. The issues that get the most attention aren't necessarily the ones with the largest expected losses, and a low-probability catastrophe that can't be undone may deserve more investment than a high-probability one we can recover from.

6. Is it good to make happy people?

What it is. This is the most philosophical and most contested chapter, and it belongs to population ethics: when we compare worlds with different numbers of people, which is better?

Many people hold the intuition of neutrality: making an existing person happier is good, but bringing an extra happy person into existence is neither good nor bad. The slogan is "make people happy, not make happy people." MacAskill rejects this. His argument, roughly: if adding a person with a good life really were neutral, then human extinction, which means trillions of good future lives never happening, would be no great loss, and most people can't accept that.

The other side has a trump card, though: Parfit's Repugnant Conclusion. If you add up everyone's welfare (the total view), then an enormous population whose lives are each barely worth living comes out "better" than ten billion people living extremely good lives, because the total is larger. Almost everyone finds this absurd. MacAskill grants it is counterintuitive, but his position is that every theory of population ethics implies something counterintuitive, and the theories that avoid the Repugnant Conclusion pay a higher price (paraphrased). He also co-signed a 2021 paper arguing that avoiding the Repugnant Conclusion should not be a necessary condition for an acceptable theory of population ethics.

Why it matters. This chapter decides how much avoiding extinction really matters. If you accept the intuition of neutrality, extinction is bad only because it kills the people alive now. If you accept the total view, extinction is bad also because it cancels trillions of possible good lives.

How it changes your view. Even if you reject his conclusion, the chapter forces you to see that you already hold an implicit ethics about how many people should exist and what lives they should lead; you've just never set it out on the table.

7. Stagnation, and "robustly good" actions: what to do now

What it is. MacAskill also worries about a less dramatic risk: technological stagnation. Economic research finds that keeping technology advancing at the same pace takes ever more researchers; ideas are getting harder to find. Meanwhile global fertility is falling. If humanity stalled at a dangerous technological stage, with nuclear weapons and engineered pathogens but without adequate safeguards, it could stay stuck in that high-risk state for a long time.

So what can we do now? Given deep uncertainty, he favors robustly good actions: things that help in most plausible futures and are unlikely to backfire. For example:

  • advancing clean-energy technology, which cuts climate risk and leaves fossil fuels for our descendants;
  • guarding against engineered pandemics with far-UVC sterilization, early detection and faster vaccine platforms;
  • research on AI safety and governance;
  • keeping options open, continuing to learn, and steering careers or donations toward these neglected areas.

He stresses that an individual's biggest lever is usually not small daily habits but their choice of career, where their donations go, and taking part in politics and public debate. He even argues that, where circumstances allow, having children can on balance be good for the future, the opposite of the view that one should go childless for the planet's sake.

The Essential Skeleton

  1. Premise one (moral): future people matter as much as present people; distance in time carries no moral discount.
  2. Premise two (scale): if humanity continues, future people will vastly outnumber everyone past and present.
  3. Premise three (tractability): we live at an unusual moment and can affect the long-term future in two ways:
  4. 　a. ensure there is a future by reducing the risk of extinction and unrecoverable collapse (AI, engineered pathogens, nuclear war);
  5. 　b. make the future better by avoiding bad value lock-in and keeping the world morally exploratory and able to correct itself.
  6. The yardstick: use significance × persistence × contingency to pick what is worth doing.
  7. The philosophical support: an extra happy life is a good thing (reject the intuition of neutrality), so the loss from extinction is enormous.
  8. Conclusion: positively influencing the long-term future is one of the key moral priorities of our time, and under uncertainty we should favor robustly good actions.

In a sentence: the future is vast, this moment is pivotal, and for the first time the means of shaping that future are in our hands.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: longtermism means ignoring people alive today. MacAskill's claim is that the long-term future is "a key moral priority," which is weak longtermism, not the only priority. In an academic paper with the philosopher Hilary Greaves he has defended a stronger version, strong longtermism: in many decisions, the best option is the one that's best for the long-term future. The popular book deliberately holds back from that. Many of the actions he recommends (pandemic preparedness, AI safety, clean energy) also benefit people alive now.

Misreading 2: longtermism is a prediction about the future. It isn't a forecast. It is an ethical stance about making expected-value decisions under deep uncertainty.

Criticism 1: fanaticism and Pascal's mugging. If the future's value is astronomical, then shaving even a minuscule probability off extinction risk outweighs every present good in expected value. This has exactly the structure of Pascal's Wager: tiny probability × enormous payoff. Critics argue that this arithmetic leaves you open to being hijacked by any grand narrative. MacAskill replies that the risks he focuses on are not astronomically small, more like one in a hundred or even one in ten, so the argument needn't lean on fanaticism.

Criticism 2: cluelessness. We know next to nothing about causal chains a thousand years long, so the long-term effects of any action may cancel out or even backfire. What looks like a good deed today may have entirely unpredictable effects five hundred years on. Greaves herself is one of the most serious researchers on this problem.

Criticism 3: the Repugnant Conclusion and the total view. In his review "The New Moral Mathematics," the philosopher Kieran Setiya argues that counting an extra happy person as a good leads to conclusions many people can't accept. But without the total view, the badness of extinction, and with it the weight of longtermism, shrinks dramatically.

Criticism 4: SBF and FTX. Just three months after the book came out, in November 2022, the crypto exchange FTX collapsed, and its founder Sam Bankman-Fried was later convicted of fraud. He had been one of the biggest funders of effective altruism and longtermism, and MacAskill had advised the FTX Future Fund that Bankman-Fried bankrolled. Critics argue that the reasoning "for a vast distant good, today's rules can bend" is exactly longtermism's most dangerous side effect. MacAskill said publicly that he had been deceived and was furious, and reiterated that effective altruism has never endorsed breaking integrity for the "greater good."

Criticism 5: elitism and technocracy. Critics such as Émile P. Torres argue that longtermism lets a small circle of wealthy academics and tech figures decide what humanity's future should be. It may push present, concrete injustices (poverty, the legacies of race and colonialism) into second place. And talk of "preserving humanity's potential" smuggles in a vision of the future centered on technological expansion.

Crossovers

  • Neuroscience · critical periods: Hubel and Wiesel found that if a kitten has one eye sewn shut during its first weeks, its visual cortex permanently loses responsiveness to that eye, while the same deprivation in an adult cat for months has almost no effect. That's the biological version of molten glass cooling into shape. The difference: critical periods are designed by evolution, whereas a civilization's lock-in is accidental and designed by no one.
  • Evolution · the frozen accident: In 1968 Francis Crick proposed that the genetic code (which three-base codon maps to which amino acid) may be a "frozen accident." The original assignment may have been arbitrary, but once all life depended on it, any change became lethal, so it has persisted for billions of years. That is value lock-in at its most extreme: an early accident made permanent by later dependence. The difference: a genetic code isn't good or bad, but values are.
  • Psychology · your future self is a stranger: Brain-imaging studies by Hal Hershfield and colleagues found that thinking about yourself ten years on looks more like thinking about a stranger than about your present self, and people who feel that gap more strongly save less. If even our future selves feel like strangers, future others have an even harder time entering our moral view. The difference: your future self is still you, while longtermism asks you to take responsibility for actual strangers.
  • Astrobiology · the Great Filter: Robin Hanson argued in 1996 that because the universe is vast yet we see no alien civilizations (the Fermi paradox), at least one step on the road from dead matter to interstellar civilization must be extremely hard. If that step is behind us, good news; if it's ahead, most technological civilizations destroy themselves. That gives existential risk a cosmic-scale piece of indirect evidence. The difference: this is speculation, not proof.
  • Distributed systems · irreversible operations: In engineering, irreversible operations like deleting data or migrating a database schema get extra approvals, staged rollouts and soft deletes to keep a path to rollback, while reversible changes can be tried quickly. Longtermism's "keep options open, avoid lock-in" is the same principle. The difference: engineers usually know the target state, whereas humanity isn't yet sure what's right.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① Future people count; there could be a great many of them; and we can make their lives go better.

② Broken glass on a trail does the same harm whether it cuts a child next week or a hundred years from now.

③ Discounting the future for uncertainty is reasonable; discounting it merely for coming later is a weakness of the imagination.

④ Humanity is a reckless teenager: its power has suddenly grown, its wisdom lags behind, and some of its choices will last a lifetime.

⑤ A change's value to the future = significance × persistence × contingency; if it was bound to happen anyway, you only made it arrive a little sooner.

⑥ Moral views we now take for granted may have come within a hair of never appearing.

⑦ Culture is molten glass that can still be blown into many shapes; once it cools it sets, and AGI could cool it fast.

⑧ The gap between 99% dying and 100% dying is far greater than the gap between peace and 99% dying.

⑨ Until we know what's right, protect the ability to correct mistakes: a plural, exploratory world matters more than winning today's argument.

⑩ Beware the math of tiny probabilities times huge payoffs: longtermism's biggest risk is being hijacked by its own arithmetic.
