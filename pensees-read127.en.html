Pensées · Blaise Pascal · 1670 (posthumous)

In One Sentence

Man hangs between two infinities, the stars above and the dust below. He is the frailest thing in nature and the only thing in it that thinks, so his greatness and his wretchedness are both true at once, and any philosophy that sees only one of them is lying. Reason can lead us right up to this contradiction but cannot resolve it. Pascal's conclusion is that reason's last step is to admit that infinitely many things lie beyond it, and where reason gives out, we have to wager.

Where It Sits

Pascal was a seventeenth-century French mathematician and physicist. He built a mechanical calculator, ran experiments on the vacuum, and laid the foundations of probability theory with Fermat. After a religious experience in 1654 he threw in his lot with Jansenism, a rigorist Catholic movement that stressed original sin and grace and was locked in a feud with the Jesuits. The Pensées are the thousand-odd fragments he left toward an Apology for the Christian Religion he never finished. Friends published them after his death at thirty-nine. They are a summit of French prose and one of the distant ancestors of existentialism.

The Core Argument

  • Man is a paradox, both great and wretched. He is wretched because he is fragile, ignorant, self-deceiving and mortal. He is great because he knows he is wretched. A tree does not know it is wretched.
  • We hide from this truth through "diversion." Hunting, gambling, war, careers and society are not really about the hare or the promotion. They are about never being left alone to think about our condition.
  • Reason has limits, yet we still have to take a stand. Reason can neither prove nor disprove God, but you are already embarked, and not choosing is itself a choice. So you should run the numbers with reason, and also admit that we have a second way of knowing, which Pascal calls "the heart."

Put together: Pascal uses skepticism to dismantle human pride, then uses human greatness to dismantle skepticism, and leaves us at a place where all we can do is bet.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. The thinking reed: one root for greatness and misery

What it is. The most famous passage, paraphrased: "Man is only a reed, the weakest thing in nature, but he is a thinking reed. The whole universe need not arm itself to crush him; a vapor, a drop of water is enough to kill him. But if the universe were to crush him, man would still be nobler than what kills him, because he knows that he dies and he knows the advantage the universe has over him. The universe knows nothing of this." All our dignity, he goes on, lies in thought, so let us labor to think well. That is the principle of morality.

The point is not the platitude that humans think. The point is that greatness and wretchedness spring from the same source. Wretchedness itself proves greatness, Pascal argues, because only a deposed king grieves over his lost throne. Someone who was never a king does not mourn the lack of a crown. We suffer over being imperfect, mortal and ignorant, which means we carry a measuring stick that shows what we ought to have and lack.

An analogy. Picture a phone that could become aware its battery is at 3%. The awareness doesn't add any charge, but it gives the phone something no ordinary phone has: knowledge of its own condition. That knowledge doesn't save it, but it separates it from a brick.

Why it matters. From this Pascal draws a two-way warning that serves as the method of the whole book: "Man is neither angel nor beast, and the misfortune is that whoever would play the angel plays the beast" (paraphrased). He lands a punch on each side. The Stoics, who held that reason and will could bring us to imperturbable calm, saw only man's greatness and became proud. The skeptics and hedonists, with Montaigne as his chief example, saw only his misery and fell into despair or license. Each philosophy is half right and half wrong.

How it changes your view. You grow wary of any theory of human nature that tells only one story, whether about how rational and noble we are or about how selfish and pitiable we are. Both probably capture something real, and neither is complete. Pascalian maturity means holding both ends at once: don't let people see only their likeness to beasts and miss their greatness, and don't let them see only their greatness and miss their baseness.

2. The two infinities: our lost place in the cosmos

What it is. In the long fragment on the "disproportion of man," Pascal first makes you look up. The earth is a dot on the sun's orbit, the sun a dot among the stars, and the visible universe an imperceptible trace in nature's vast bosom. Then he makes you look down. Inside a mite's body there are legs, joints, veins, drops in its blood, and if you keep dividing, every level holds another whole world. Man is suspended between two abysses: a nothing compared with the infinite, an everything compared with nothing, "a mean between nothing and everything" (paraphrased).

This is more than grand description. It is an epistemological conclusion: because we stand in the middle, both ends, the beginning and the end of things, are hidden from us. Our senses work only within a narrow band. Too much noise deafens us, too much light dazzles us, and things too close or too far blur. Our knowledge works the same way and grasps only "some appearance of the middle of things." So we should neither hope for certain knowledge nor claim to know nothing at all.

A much shorter and even more famous line: "The eternal silence of these infinite spaces terrifies me." The medieval cosmos had been a bounded, layered cathedral full of meaning. The universe after Galileo was boundless, uniform and indifferent. Pascal was among the first to really feel that new universe. He took it in not as a scientific bulletin but as a condition of existence.

An analogy. A receiver tuned to one band of frequencies can't conclude that other bands don't exist, and it can't conclude that its band is the whole signal either.

Why it matters, and how it changes your view. It turns humility from a moral exhortation into a statement of fact about where humans stand as observers. Physics now spans about sixty orders of magnitude from the Planck scale to the observable universe, and we sit somewhere in the middle, which is a modern version of Pascal's picture. After this fragment you'll find it harder to say that science has more or less figured everything out, and just as hard to say that nothing can be known anyway.

3. Diversion (divertissement): why we can't sit alone in a room

What it is. The French word means "turning aside," steering attention away from something. Pascal's famous line: "All of humanity's unhappiness comes from a single thing: not knowing how to stay quietly in a room" (paraphrased).

His analysis of the mechanism is razor-sharp. Take a man who has just lost his only son and is buried in lawsuits. Why is he not grieving at this moment? Because he is utterly absorbed in watching which way the boar will run with the hounds after it. The hunter never wants the quarry itself. Hand him the hare and he'll turn it down. What he wants is the commotion of the chase, which keeps him from thinking about himself. Give a gambler each morning the money he might win that day, on condition that he not play, and you'll make him miserable. He wants the game that makes him forget himself, not the money. Kingship is the happiest station in the world, yet leave a king without company or occupation, with nothing to do but think about himself, and he becomes a wretched man. That is why kings are always surrounded by people whose job is to amuse them.

An analogy. Today one hardly needs an analogy. Scrolling short videos, checking messages, cramming the calendar full: all are high-definition divertissement. Pascal's insight is that boredom (ennui) is not a minor ailment of idleness. It is the bedrock we hit the moment we stop, namely our own finitude and death.

Why it matters. It flips a common assumption. We think we keep busy to reach a goal; Pascal says the goal is often the excuse for keeping busy. We think we are seeking rest, but once we get it we can't stand it and go looking for new excitement. "They imagine they are seeking repose, when in fact they are seeking only agitation" (paraphrased).

How it changes your view. The next time you reach for your phone in an elevator, in a queue, or before sleep, you can ask Pascal's question: what am I running from? This doesn't make all entertainment sinful, and Pascal himself grants that diversion is only human. It helps you tell apart enjoying something and using it to avoid yourself.

4. Pascal's Wager: the bet we can't avoid placing

What it is. The fragment is headed "Infinite—nothing." Pascal grants at the outset that if there is a God, he is infinitely incomprehensible, and reason can neither prove nor disprove his existence. "But you must wager." You are already embarked, he says, and not choosing is a choice. So do the accounting:

God existsGod does not exist
Bet "God" and live accordinglyInfinite gain (eternal happiness)A finite loss (some pleasures)
Bet "no God"Infinite lossA finite gain

The Wager as a decision matrix (schematic): so long as the probability that God exists is not zero, the expected value of the first row is infinite and swamps every finite gain or loss.

This is one of the earliest expected-value arguments in decision theory: add up probability times payoff and compare the options. Pascal's point is that even if the odds are slim, when one side of the bet holds infinite life and happiness and you are staking only finite goods, reason requires you to bet on God. "If you win, you win everything; if you lose, you lose nothing" (paraphrased).

The often-skipped second half is where the real mechanism lies. The interlocutor replies that he is made in such a way that he simply cannot believe. Pascal doesn't tell him to force it. He says the obstacle isn't a lack of evidence but the passions. Learn from those who were once bound as you are and have now staked everything, and do what they did at the start: take holy water, have masses said, act as though you believe, and "this will make you believe naturally and will make you stupid" (vous abêtira). "Stupid" here means docile like an animal: let the body and habit go first and draw the heart after them.

An analogy. It's like buying fire insurance. You don't think the house will certainly burn, but the losses on each side are wildly lopsided. The difference is that Pascal's policy asks you to change not just your wallet but your whole way of living.

Why it matters. The Wager matters in intellectual history in two ways. First, it recasts faith from a proof problem into a decision problem: you don't need certainty, only a comparison. Second, it concedes that beliefs are produced not only by argument but by behavior and habit. Modern psychology agrees on that second point; research on cognitive dissonance, for example, shows people adjusting their beliefs to fit what they have already done.

How it changes your view. Even if you reject its theology entirely, the Wager hands you a tool: on deeply uncertain questions where you must still act, don't ask only which outcome is more likely; ask what being wrong would cost in each direction. Pascal's shadow falls across debates about climate, pandemics and technological risk, and it is exactly here that later critics find the holes (see the criticism section).

5. The heart's reasons: the geometric mind, the intuitive mind, and the heart

What it is. The line quoted to exhaustion: "The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know at all" (Le cœur a ses raisons que la raison ne connaît point). It is usually taken as a slogan for romantic love, but in Pascal it is an epistemological claim.

He says we know truth not only by reason but by the heart. We know first principles such as space, time, motion and number directly by the heart. Reason can't prove them; it can only reason from them. Demanding that the heart prove first principles before accepting them would be as absurd as demanding that reason "feel" every theorem it derives before accepting it. So "the heart" is not emotion but an intuitive, holistic grasp that sees something directly, without derivation.

Alongside this runs a second distinction, between the geometric mind (esprit de géométrie) and the intuitive mind (esprit de finesse). The geometric mind starts from a few clear principles remote from ordinary life and deduces rigorously, step by step; its principles are hard to see, but once seen they don't mislead. The intuitive mind deals with principles in everyday life that are many, subtle and tangled. They are right in front of us but too numerous to list, so they must be taken in "at a glance." A mathematician who has only the geometric mind looks ridiculous in human affairs, and a person of finesse loses patience when forced to reason from definitions.

An analogy. The geometric mind writes a proof. The intuitive mind is the old doctor who walks into a ward, glances at a patient and knows something is wrong. Only afterwards can she list a few reasons, and never all of them.

Why it matters. This is not anti-rationalism but reason setting its own limits: "the last step of reason is to recognize that there are infinitely many things beyond it," and "two excesses: to exclude reason, to admit nothing but reason" (both paraphrased). Pascal was a first-rate mathematician, so when he says reason has limits he isn't saying it out of dislike. He says it because he knows exactly how far it can go.

How it changes your view. It gives you a checklist question: can this problem be broken into steps and derived, which suits the geometric mind, or is it tangled and in need of experienced, holistic judgment, which suits the intuitive mind? Many failures come from using the wrong tool, like measuring a relationship with KPIs or going on gut feel about a decision you could actually calculate.

6. Custom, imagination, and "truth on this side of the Pyrenees"

What it is. Pascal took over much of Montaigne's skepticism and used it to take apart the pride of human reason. Imagination is "the mistress of error and falsehood." Even the wisest judge will struggle not to laugh if a preacher has a hoarse voice, an odd face or a badly shaved chin, and his judgment will be bent. Judges wear red robes and doctors square caps precisely because a good part of their authority rests on appearances.

Justice is no different: "Truth on this side of the Pyrenees, error on the other." "A strange justice that a river bounds!" (both paraphrased). People obey the law not because it is just but because it is the law, and Pascal thinks it is better the people not see through this, since custom is the root of order. He adds that "custom is a second nature that destroys the first," and then asks whether what we call nature might itself be only a first custom.

Why it matters, and how it changes your view. This is really the same point as the Wager's second half: we are driven mostly by habit, body and imagination, and argument is only a small part of it. Pascal calls this side of us "the machine" (automate). Once you see it, you stop thinking you can persuade someone just by setting out the evidence. You also gain some distance from your own "obvious" beliefs. How many of them did you reason your way to, and how many are just clothes you've grown used to wearing?

7. The three orders: body, mind, charity

What it is. Pascal sorts value into three "orders" that do not communicate with each other. The order of bodies holds power, wealth and physical greatness: kings and the rich. The order of minds holds genius and learning, people like Archimedes. The order of charity holds holiness: the saints and Christ. The distance between them is "infinite." All bodies together are worth less than the least of minds, and all minds together are worth less than the smallest act of charity (paraphrased).

The crux is that the orders can't be measured against each other. Archimedes is great without a throne, and his brilliance needs no army. A king, however mighty, scores nothing in the order of minds. Tyranny, as Pascal defines it in another fragment, is wanting to obtain by one order what belongs to another: using power to make people think you clever, or using cleverness to demand that they fear you.

An analogy. It's like length, weight and color. You can't ask whether three meters is more than five kilograms.

How it changes your view. It works as a detector for category errors. Wealth can't buy respect, a degree can't buy love, and a job title doesn't prove an idea right. Whenever you bristle at the thought that someone should be obeyed just because they're rich, you are invoking Pascal's three orders.

The Essential Skeleton

Fragmentary as it is, the book has a clear argument behind it. Pascal himself left an outline sorting the fragments into bundles, and the line runs roughly like this:

  1. Describe the human condition before mentioning God. Man is a contradiction: great and wretched, longing for happiness and never getting it, craving truth and never grasping it (the two infinities, imagination, custom).
  2. How do we handle the contradiction? We flee it. Diversion lets us live without thinking. But that proves we already know the truth is too painful to look at.
  3. Philosophy's two solutions both fail. The Stoics grasp only the greatness and fall into pride. The skeptics grasp only the misery and fall into despair.
  4. Only one explanation fits both sides at once: man was created great and then fell (original sin). Greatness is the trace of what we were, wretchedness our present state. For Pascal this is not a proof but the hypothesis that best explains the data.
  5. God is a "hidden God" (Deus absconditus). He gives enough light to those who seek him and enough darkness to those who don't. So the evidence will never be strong enough to compel belief, nor weak enough to make belief absurd.
  6. So reason can't settle it, and you have to bet. The Wager is addressed to someone who has been persuaded, can't prove it, and still can't believe. The rest is left to habit and the heart.

In a sentence: it first makes you admit you are a riddle, then tells you that a riddle needs an answer that explains it, not more distraction, even if the only way to approach that answer is to wager.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "the heart has its reasons" means feeling trumps reason. It doesn't. Pascal's heart is the intuitive grasp of first principles, on a level with the axioms of geometry. He opposes admitting only reason, and he opposes excluding reason just as firmly.

Misreading 2: the Wager is a proof that God exists. It isn't. It presupposes that reason can't prove anything here. It is an argument about how to act, and it is only one step, aimed at a particular reader: the skeptic who has been persuaded and still can't believe.

Misreading 3: the Pensées is a finished book. It is a set of papers left unfinished at his death. The order of the fragments was set by editors, and editions differ widely. Brunschvicg (1897) rearranged them by theme, while Lafuma (1951) followed Pascal's own bundles as closely as possible, and the two numberings don't match. That is why citations often read "L418 / B233." Much of the "structure" you read was supplied by later hands.

Criticism 1: the many-gods objection. Diderot put it in one line (paraphrased): an imam could reason in exactly the same way. If there are countless possible gods, each threatening unbelievers with infinite punishment, the Wager can't tell you which one to back, and backing the wrong one may be as bad as not betting at all.

Criticism 2: infinite payoffs break decision theory. If any non-zero probability yields infinite expected value, then believing with a 1% chance is just as infinitely good as believing wholeheartedly, and the Wager can't tell them apart. This is the philosopher Alan Hájek's "mixed strategies" objection. A more general version is known as Pascal's mugging. A stranger says: give me five dollars or I'll use magic to make a trillion people suffer. If you calculate strictly by expected value, you should pay. This suggests that the very arithmetic of astronomical payoff times tiny probability may be broken. The objection is still in heavy use today in debates over longtermism and AI risk.

Criticism 3: belief can't be willed, and training yourself into belief through habit is morally suspect. W. K. Clifford's maxim: "It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence." William James's "The Will to Believe" partly defends Pascal: when a choice is live, forced and momentous, refusing to choose is itself a choice.

Criticism 4: the "sublime misanthrope." Voltaire devoted the last of his Letters on the English (Lettres philosophiques) to answering Pascal. Man is not so wretched, he argued; diversion is not a disease but our nature, and we are made to act, not to sit in a room brooding over our misery (paraphrased). Nietzsche both revered and pitied Pascal, calling him "the most instructive victim of Christianity": a mind that could have been great, taught by Christianity to despise itself (paraphrased). The force of this criticism is that Pascal paints the human condition so dark in order to make us crave a rescue, so the diagnosis and the cure are not wholly independent.

Criticism 5: the severity of Jansenism. His view of man rests on an Augustinian account of original sin and grace, in which humans outside grace are almost nothing. Readers who don't share that premise will find the "greatness and wretchedness" brilliantly drawn but the explanation by "the Fall" arriving too quickly.

Pascal Beyond This Book

Pascal the mathematician is the Wager's ancestor. In 1654 he and Fermat solved, by correspondence, the "problem of points": if a game of chance is interrupted midway, how should the stakes be divided? Their answer was not by the rounds already won but by each player's chances from here on. That is the birth of expected value and the starting point of probability theory, and "Pascal's triangle" was his tool for counting the combinations. On the night of 23 November that same year he had a religious ecstasy and wrote the Memorial: "Fire. God of Abraham, God of Isaac, God of Jacob, not of the philosophers and scholars… Certainty, certainty, feeling, joy, peace" (paraphrased). He sewed the paper into the lining of his coat, and a servant found it only after his death. In the same year he invented the mathematics of uncertainty and lived through a certainty that needed no calculation. The Wager sits exactly where the two meet.

The Provincial Letters (1656–57) show his other side. In eighteen anonymous letters he satirized Jesuit casuistry, a technique of breaking moral rules down case by case that kept carving out more exceptions for sinners. He took particular aim at probabilism: if a single respected theologian held an act permissible, you could do it, even when the opposing view was better argued. Pascal mocked it as turning morality into loophole-hunting. The letters made him a model of French prose, and they include the line "I have made this letter longer only because I have not had the time to make it shorter" (paraphrased). Read them alongside the Pensées and a tension appears: he mocks those who use probability as a moral loophole, yet uses probability to argue for faith. The difference is that they used probability to let themselves off, while he used it to force himself to take the question seriously.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · "shock me rather than leave me alone": In a 2014 Science paper, Timothy Wilson and colleagues asked people to sit alone with their thoughts for 6–15 minutes. Many preferred to give themselves a mild electric shock, about two-thirds of men and a quarter of women. It's almost a lab version of not knowing how to stay quietly in a room. The difference: the study measured discomfort and gives no support to Pascal's claim that what we flee is death.
  • Buddhism · papañca (mental proliferation): Early Buddhist texts describe the mind automatically spinning one thought into layer upon layer of concepts and getting dragged along without being able to stop. Pascal's diversion has the same structure: a mind that won't stay empty for a moment. The difference is the way out. Buddhism asks you to stop and look straight at the restlessness, while Pascal thinks that once you see it, you still need God to fill the gap.
  • Neuroscience · the somatic marker hypothesis: Antonio Damasio studied patients with prefrontal damage whose reasoning was intact but who couldn't even settle on an appointment date, because they had lost the body's signals that "pre-price" options. This gives a secular version of the heart's reasons: without that underived, holistic valuation, reason itself seizes up. The difference: Pascal's heart can also know God, while Damasio's concerns survival and choice.
  • Complexity science · sensitive dependence: Pascal wrote that if Cleopatra's nose had been shorter, the whole face of the earth would have changed (paraphrased). That's the historian's version of the butterfly effect: in systems with positive feedback, tiny initial differences are amplified into vastly different outcomes. The difference: Lorenz was describing unpredictability in deterministic equations, while Pascal was mocking the contingency and vanity of human history.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① Man is only a reed, the frailest thing in nature, but he is a thinking reed.

② The universe can crush a man, yet he is nobler than the universe, because he knows he dies and the universe knows nothing.

③ Man's greatness lies in knowing he is wretched; a tree does not know it is wretched.

④ Man is neither angel nor beast, and the misfortune is that whoever would play the angel plays the beast.

⑤ Caught between two infinities, we are nothing compared with the infinite and everything compared with nothing.

⑥ The eternal silence of these infinite spaces terrifies me.

⑦ All our unhappiness comes from not knowing how to sit quietly in a room. The hunter wants the chase that spares him from thinking about himself, not the hare.

⑧ You are already embarked, and not choosing is a choice: if you win, you win everything; if you lose, you lose nothing.

⑨ The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know at all, and reason's last step is to admit that infinitely many things lie beyond it.

⑩ Body, mind and charity are orders infinitely apart, and tyranny is wanting to obtain by one what belongs to another.

Deep Read · You now have the through-line of the Pensées: the thinking reed and the paradox of greatness and misery, the two infinities, diversion, the Wager, the heart and reason, custom and imagination, the three orders, plus the many-gods objection, Pascal's mugging, and the critiques of Voltaire and Nietzsche.
