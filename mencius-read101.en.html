Mencius

Mengzi · Meng Ke · c. 4th century BCE (compiled by Mencius and his disciples)

中文 →

In one sentence

Mencius argues one thing, and spends an entire book arguing it: everything you need in order to be good is already in you — not a rule poured in from outside, but a sprout that pushes up on its own; if it never became a tree, the problem is not a missing seed but an axe, or your own refusal to water it. Grant that, and politics is rewritten too. A ruler is also a human being and also has that flinch of pity, so good government requires no separate science of statecraft — only that he extend to the people the feeling he already has for those near him. And a ruler who cannot extend it is no longer a ruler: killing him is not regicide.

Where it sits

Meng Ke (c. 372–289 BCE) came from the small state of Zou during the Warring States period. He never studied under Confucius or his immediate circle and said so plainly — he learned at second hand, from the later Confucian school, traditionally traced to the lineage of Confucius's grandson Zisi. He spent his life travelling between the courts of Qi, Liang (Wei), Teng and Song, met a great many rulers, and was never actually employed by any of them; in the end he withdrew and put the book together with his students. For a thousand years the text ranked as merely one philosopher among many. Then Han Yu placed him in the daotong, the orthodox line of transmission, and in the Song dynasty Zhu Xi bound the Mencius together with the Analects, the Great Learning and the Doctrine of the Mean as the Four Books — the core curriculum of the civil service examinations for the next seven centuries. Mencius became the "Second Sage," ranked immediately after Confucius. If the Analects gave China its default script for being a person, the Mencius supplied the argument for why that script is the right one.

The core claims

Seven books, three commitments:

  • Human nature is good — but "good" is a sprout, not a fruit. Everyone is born with the "four sprouts," four moral beginnings that society did not install. They are as small as a flame just catching or a spring just breaking ground, and without cultivation they go out or dry up.
  • Political legitimacy rests on the people, not on heaven's warrant or on force. "Those who submit to force do not submit in their hearts." Benevolent government is not a posture of kindness but a concrete programme that begins by securing everyone a stable livelihood.
  • Personal courage can be accumulated. Doing the right small thing day after day builds up haoran zhi qi, a flood-like moral energy, and with it an unmovable mind: wealth cannot corrupt you, poverty cannot shift you, force cannot bend you.

The core ideas, one at a time

The four sprouts: a thought experiment that strips out every motive

Mencius's most famous move is a thought experiment — an imagined case designed to remove confounding factors one by one and see what is left. "Anyone who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well will feel alarm and compassion." The force is in what comes next: he eliminates every self-interested motive in turn. Not in order to get in with the child's parents. Not to win a reputation among neighbours and friends. Not out of distaste for the sound of the child's cry. Subtract all the calculation, and the flinch that remains was there from birth.

From that flinch he counts out four innate dispositions, the "four duan." Duan means a beginning, a sprouting tip — and that single word is the hinge of the whole doctrine:

The sproutWhat it looks like day to dayThe virtue it grows into
CompassionYour chest tightens when you see someone sufferRen — genuine care for others
Shame and aversionYour face burns after something underhanded; meanness disgusts youYi — the sense of what is fitting
DeferenceYou instinctively let someone else take the better seatLi — ritual order and proportion
Right and wrongYou can tell something is off without being taughtZhi — moral discernment

Innate sprouts on the left, cultivated trees on the right. All of Mencius's ethics is the arrow running left to right.

He then adds the sentence that carries the whole system: "Whoever has these four sprouts in himself and knows to extend and fill them out — it is like a fire beginning to catch, a spring beginning to break through." That clears up two misreadings at once. Innate goodness does not mean people are born good — a flame that small is put out by a breath. But neither is it empty consolation: you do not have to manufacture a moral self from nothing, because the material is already there. What it changes is the aim of moral education. It stops being sculpture and becomes gardening. You are not carving a statue out of a block of wood; you are tending something that is already growing — which means the only available actions are weeding, watering, and not yanking on it.

Rushing water and the willow: what Mencius and Gaozi were really fighting about

The sharpest exchange in the book is with Gaozi, a contemporary thinker who held that human nature is in itself neither good nor bad, and whose words survive only inside the Mencius. Gaozi opens well: "Human nature is like rushing water. Open a channel east and it flows east; open one west and it flows west. Human nature is no more divided into good and bad than water is divided into east and west." It is an elegant image, and its point is that morality is a direction imposed by circumstance, not a property of the water.

Mencius's counter is better. "Water indeed does not distinguish east from west — but does it not distinguish up from down? The goodness of human nature is like water running downhill." He grants that water can be sent uphill — strike it and it will splash over your forehead, force it along a channel and it will climb a mountain — but that is shi, external pressure, not the water's own tendency. People do evil in the same way: pushed there by circumstance, with their underlying inclination unchanged.

The second round is clearer still. Gaozi says human nature is like the willow and morality like cups and bowls: you bend nature into shape. Mencius goes straight for the joint: are you following the willow's nature when you make cups from it, or mutilating it? If mutilating, then on your account practising benevolence and rightness means maiming human nature — and who would follow you?

What is actually at stake is not whether people are nice. It is a contest between two metaphors: is morality carving, or is it growth? Mencius chose growth, and set the key for two thousand years of Confucian thought. Xunzi later chose carving — "warped wood must be steamed and pressed against a frame before it is straight" — and built a rival tradition centred on ritual and law. You still use one of these two metaphors whenever you face a child, a junior colleague, or yourself: are you straightening a warped board, or tending a tree? The choice quietly determines everything you do next.

The trees of Ox Mountain: if nature is good, why is the world full of bad people

This is the question the doctrine must answer, and Mencius answers it with the loveliest passage in the book. "The trees of Ox Mountain were once beautiful. But it lay on the edge of a great city, and axes came to it day after day — could it stay beautiful?" Whatever survived the axes was then grazed off by cattle and sheep, until the mountain stood bare. "People see it stripped and think it never had timber in it. But is that the mountain's nature?"

Transferred to a person, the observation becomes fine-grained. Something in us regrows in the quiet of the night — he calls it the night air and the air of dawn, that clarity a person has at first light before the day's business touches them. Even then, he says, what little remains of the likes and dislikes we share with other people is "barely anything." And then the day's conduct fetters and destroys it again, round after round, until not even the night air can hold, and a person comes to look scarcely different from an animal.

Why the mechanism matters: it converts evil from a question of essence into a question of attrition. Badness is not something extra you were issued; it is something that has been cut away from you. Out of this comes his most practical single line: "The way of learning is nothing else: it is to go and get back your strayed heart." Notice how counterintuitive that is. Self-cultivation is not acquisition but recovery. You do not have to read every classic before you qualify as a decent person. What you need right now is to stop and look at the thing that daily logging has nearly finished off.

Flood-like qi and accumulated rightness: courage is a balance you build up

Asked what he was good at, Mencius answered: "I am good at nourishing my flood-like qi." Qi resists translation; here it is roughly the moral energy that lets a person hold their ground. He describes it as "supremely great, supremely firm; nourish it with uprightness and do it no harm, and it fills the space between heaven and earth."

The operative sentence is the next one: "This qi is produced by accumulated rightness; it cannot be seized by an occasional righteous act." Accumulated rightness means exactly what it says — you did not fudge it on this small thing, or that one, and the balance slowly thickens. The alternative is a raid: one splendid public gesture, grabbed from outside. And the moment you do something you cannot square with yourself, "the qi starves." This is the most usable causal claim in the book: whether you dare speak up in the room that matters depends less on eloquence or temperament than on whether you have been cutting corners lately. Hence the famous line: "If on searching myself I find I am upright, then though they be ten thousand, I go."

Immediately afterwards he installs a speed limit, using a story most readers know without knowing where it came from. A man of Song, impatient that his rice shoots were growing slowly, went down the rows pulling each one taller. He came home exhausted and announced he had helped the shoots grow; his son ran out to look, and every shoot had withered. Mencius's conclusion: keep the work constantly in mind, but do not fix a schedule and hound it — do not forget it, and do not help it grow. The proverb comes from a passage about self-cultivation, not about farming. What it blocks is moral impatience: set out to become a better person by the end of the week and you will mostly kill the shoots.

The great man and "heaven's rank": the standing nobody issued you

Someone once praised the great diplomat-strategists Gongsun Yan and Zhang Yi to Mencius — men who moved whole states by persuasion, whose anger made feudal lords tremble and whose quiet made the world go still. Were they not great men? Mencius's reply is withering: that is "the way of a concubine" — reading the master's face and calling obedience a skill, no different from a bride instructed to be reverent and never cross her husband. The real great man, he says, "dwells in the wide house of the world, stands in its correct position, walks its great road… Wealth and honour cannot corrupt him, poverty and obscurity cannot shift him, force and threat cannot bend him."

Underwriting that steadiness is a distinction between two kinds of rank. Heaven's rank is benevolence, rightness, loyalty, good faith, an untiring delight in the good — the nobility nature confers. Human rank is being a duke or a minister — the nobility other people confer. The ancients cultivated the first and the second followed; his contemporaries, he complains, use the first as a lever to get the second and then throw it away. He puts it even more directly: "Everyone wants to be honoured. And everyone has within himself something honourable — they simply never think of it." He names the catch too: what Zhao Meng ennobles, Zhao Meng can debase — Zhao Meng being a powerful minister of Jin, a man whose favour made careers. Anything another person can give you, that person can take back; only what you grew yourself is beyond recall. This became the template for the Chinese scholar's self-image for two millennia, and it remains the oldest available antidote to hanging your worth on a title, a rating, or somebody's good opinion.

Benevolent government and extending kindness: politics is not a separate logic

King Xuan of Qi once saw an ox being led to be sacrificed, saw it trembling with fear, could not bear it, and ordered a sheep substituted. His people mocked him for stinginess. Mencius seized on the incident: "That heart is enough to make you a true king." Then he pressed: if you could not go through with it for an ox you had never met, how do you go through with it for the people in your care? "Treat the elders in your own family as elders should be treated, and extend it to the elders of other families; treat your own young as the young should be treated, and extend it to the young of others." That is extending kindness: goodness does not ask you to conjure love for strangers out of nothing, but to move the feeling you already have for those near you outward, ring by ring. "Extend your kindness and it is enough to protect all within the four seas; fail to extend it and you cannot protect your own wife and children."

He also draws a distinction that has lost none of its edge: "It is not that you cannot; it is that you will not." Tucking Mount Tai under your arm and leaping the northern sea — that is genuinely impossible. Breaking off a branch for an elder and claiming you cannot — that is refusal wearing the mask of incapacity. A great many things described as "not feasible right now" are simply not wanted.

One level up sits his contrast between the true king and the hegemon, the most consequential pairing in Chinese political thought:

The hegemon — force borrowing the name of benevolenceThe true king — virtue practising benevolence
Runs onPower; benevolence is the signageCharacter; the benevolence is real
RequiresA large stateA small one will do
How people submitNot in their hearts — they are merely outmatchedGladly, from the centre of the heart
DurabilityDissolves the moment the power ebbsLasts as long as the allegiance does

The distinction transplants cleanly into organisations: compliance squeezed out by metrics and genuine buy-in have very different costs and very different half-lives.

Stable livelihood, stable heart: the most underrated Mencius is the institutional one

Reading him as a moralist who only talks about the inner life is the commonest mistake. His benevolent government has hard economic content, and it starts with a line too cold for an idealist: "To have a stable heart without a stable livelihood — only the scholar can manage that. As for the common people, without a stable livelihood they will not have a stable heart." A stable livelihood means predictable means of support; a stable heart means steady moral conduct. Then the knife: when people consequently fall into crime and you punish them, that is "netting the people" — trapping your own subjects like game.

So his proposals come with numbers. Plant mulberries on the five-mu homestead and those over fifty can wear silk; keep the livestock breeding in season and those over seventy can eat meat; do not conscript the eight-member household away from its hundred mu at planting and harvest, and it will not go hungry. Add "reduce punishments, lighten taxes" and "take from the people according to a fixed limit." He even designs a land system, the well-field: a square of land divided like the character for "well" into nine plots, eight farmed privately by eight households and the central one worked jointly, its yield serving as the tax — "public work finished, then they may attend to their own." It ties a ceiling on taxation, cooperative labour and neighbourly mutual aid into a single arrangement — "going out and coming in they keep each other company, they stand watch for one another, they support one another in illness." Whether the well-field ever historically existed remains disputed; most historians read it as an idealised construction. But the direction of the argument is unmistakable: morality is not a slogan urging the poor to tighten their belts. First give people a living that does not require them to do wrong. Food comes before virtue.

The people are the heaviest: the hardest limit on power in Chinese thought

"The people are the most important; the altars of the land and grain come next; the ruler is the lightest." That is already pointed, but the sharper passage follows. King Xuan asks: Tang banished Jie, King Wu struck down Zhou — subjects killing their sovereigns. Is that permitted? Mencius answers: "He who violates benevolence is called a thief; he who violates rightness is called a mutilator; a mutilating thief is called a mere fellow. I have heard of the execution of the fellow Zhou. I have not heard of any regicide."

The power of the move is that it is definitional. He does not argue that you may kill a king; he argues that Zhou had stopped being one. What makes a ruler a ruler is the practice of benevolence and rightness; lose those and the title lapses on its own. Pair it with his other principle — if a ruler treats his ministers as dirt and weeds, they will treat him as an enemy — and you have a fully conditional, two-way account of political obligation.

How dangerous was this? The founding Ming emperor Zhu Yuanzhang read these passages, flew into a rage, had Mencius's tablet removed from the Confucian temple for a time, and later ordered the text cut down into an abridged Mencius for examination use, with some eighty-odd passages deleted — most of them exactly these: the people outweighing the ruler, the execution of the "mere fellow," ministers regarding a ruler as an enemy. A book canonised by the state and censored by the same state: the paradox is itself the measure of what is in it.

The heart's office is to think: set the greater part first

One more idea, usually skipped, which turned out to be the foundation of later Neo-Confucianism. A student asks why, given the same humanity, some become great and others small. Mencius answers: "The ears and eyes do not think, and are therefore screened off by things… The office of the heart-mind is to think. Think and you get it; fail to think and you do not. This is what heaven gave us. Set up the greater part first, and the lesser part cannot take it away." The greater part is the heart-mind; the lesser part is the appetites of the senses.

What matters is that Mencius is not an ascetic. He says openly that palates share common preferences; he never asks anyone to mortify the flesh. What he objects to is being led by the senses, letting the lesser part sit in the greater part's chair. "Nourish the lesser part and you become a small man; nourish the greater and you become a great one" — not "a person with desires is small," but "a person who is only ever towed by them." Finally he runs the line all the way up: "He who fully develops his heart knows his nature; knowing his nature, he knows heaven." The trace of goodness inside a person is not a social convention but a fact about the cosmos. That single sentence is the bedrock of the whole Neo-Confucian edifice: look far enough inward and you arrive at the order of the universe.

The argument, distilled

Taken piecemeal the seven books look scattered. There is really one line: heart, then self, then the world.

  • Starting point: a thought experiment (the child at the well) establishes that goodness is innate, and breaks it into four countable sprouts.
  • Holding the ground: three sets of images repel three objections — rushing water and the willow, against Gaozi (morality is growth, not carving); the trees of Ox Mountain, against the evidence of the world (evil is attrition, not essence); the man of Song pulling at his shoots, against yourself (growth cannot be hurried).
  • Onto the person: extend and fill out the sprouts, accumulate rightness until the flood-like qi forms, gain an unmovable mind, become a great man — while the substance of cultivation is only "getting back the strayed heart," recovery rather than acquisition.
  • Out to the world: extend the pity you already feel for your own family, or for a trembling ox, and you have benevolent government — which comes with hard specifics (stable livelihood, light taxes, the well-field, not disrupting the farming seasons), a strategic choice (true king versus hegemon), and a floor (the people outweigh the ruler; a tyrant is a mere fellow).
  • Back up to heaven: fully develop the heart, know your nature, know heaven. The goodness inside you has a metaphysical warrant, not merely a social use.

So the thing Mencius circles endlessly is a single claim: benevolence is not an unreachable standard for sages, it is something you already have. "Everyone can become a Yao or a Shun." Which means that when you fall short, it is not that you cannot — it is that you will not.

Misreadings and real criticisms

  • Misreading 1: innate goodness means people are born good. He said sprout, not fruit, and said plainly that unextended they are "not even enough to serve your parents." Ox Mountain can be logged bare. Reading him as an optimist discards precisely his urgency.
  • Misreading 2: he only cares about the inner life. The opposite: he argues about acreage, tax rates, planting seasons and criminal penalties, and is among the most materially attentive of the early philosophers. But do not overcorrect and read him as an economist — the historicity of the well-field system is still doubted, and most historians treat it as an idealised construction.
  • Misreading 3: "the people are the most important" is democracy. This is anachronism. What Mencius wants is a benevolent ruler who decides on the people's behalf. There is no franchise, no channel of participation, only the extreme exit of executing a tyrant — usually carried out, in his own examples, by another aristocrat. It is the most powerful indigenous resource for limiting rulers, but it is not popular sovereignty.
  • Xunzi's rebuttal, the one to know. Xunzi held that human nature is bad — but the two men are not using the word the same way. Xunzi's "nature" means innate appetite ("born with a liking for gain"); Mencius's means the moral beginnings that distinguish us from animals, so half of this thousand-year quarrel is people talking past each other. Xunzi's genuinely damaging blow is logical: if nature is already good, what are sage-kings and rituals for? Mencius has to answer with extension and environment — and that answer weakens the thesis. If goodness needs that many external conditions to emerge, how far is it really from "malleable"?
  • The soft spot in the method: analogy is not proof. This is the fairest and most damaging criticism. Water runs downhill because of gravity. What makes people run toward good? Nothing connects the two necessarily; the persuasion is rhetorical. His reply to Gaozi on whether rightness is internal or external — the business about the whiteness of a white horse and the whiteness of a white man — has long been thought to shift the terms mid-argument. Mencius himself conceded, "Do I love disputation? I have no choice." He is formidable at cornering an opponent. Momentum is not the same as demonstration.
  • Two faces of "better no Book of Documents than to believe all of it." Of the "Completion of the War" chapter, he said he accepted only two or three strips of it, on the grounds that when the supremely benevolent attacks the supremely cruel, how could blood have flowed until it floated the pestles? The line is quoted as a model of critical spirit, but be honest about the reasoning: he rejects the record because it offends his moral principles, not because of any evidence. Judging history by doctrine opened a door that later scholars fought over for centuries.
  • The standard modern criticism: a doctrine of innate goodness is bad for institutional design. Believe that hearts are reliable and rulers can be reformed by moral suasion, and you will invest your hopes in a sage on the throne rather than in hard checks on power. This is a recurring modern Chinese reflection on the Confucian theory of governance. In fairness, Mencius went as far as his age permitted — a tyrant may be executed, ruler and minister bind each other conditionally — but he did not, and could not, supply an institutional answer.
  • The most concrete criticism comes from his own life: nobody hired him. King Hui of Liang's opening words were, "Venerable sir, you have come a thousand miles — surely you bring something that will profit my state?" Mencius replied: why must you speak of profit? There is benevolence and rightness, and that is all. In an age where states survived by farming and fighting and swallowing their neighbours, his programme was too slow. That does not make him wrong. It is a reminder that being right about the long run frequently has no seat at the table in the short one.

Ten sentences

1. "Anyone who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well will feel alarm and compassion" — subtract getting in with the parents, subtract reputation, subtract distaste for the cry, and the flinch that remains was born in you.

2. The four sprouts are shoots, not fruit: "like a fire beginning to catch, a spring beginning to break through." Unextended, the fire goes out and the spring dries up.

3. Morality is gardening, not sculpture. Gaozi said nature is willow to be bent into bowls; Mencius said that is mutilation. You are not straightening warped wood — you are tending something already growing.

4. "The trees of Ox Mountain were once beautiful"; axes made it bare. Evil is not something added to you. It is what has been cut away.

5. "The way of learning is nothing else: it is to go and get back your strayed heart." Cultivation is recovery, not acquisition.

6. Flood-like qi "is produced by accumulated rightness; it cannot be seized by an occasional righteous act" — courage is a balance built from small honest days, and one bad conscience drains it. Hence: "though they be ten thousand, I go." But do not be the man of Song pulling at his shoots: do not forget it, and do not help it grow.

7. "Wealth and honour cannot corrupt him, poverty cannot shift him, force cannot bend him" — while the great persuaders who read their masters' faces earn four words from Mencius: the way of a concubine.

8. You hold a rank nobody issued you: "everyone has within himself something honourable — they simply never think of it" — because what Zhao Meng ennobles, Zhao Meng can debase. What another can give, another can take back.

9. Politics is not a separate logic: extend the care you already give your own elders and children, and that is benevolent government. But "without a stable livelihood the common people will not have a stable heart" — food comes before virtue.

10. "The people are the most important; the ruler is the lightest." A sovereign who violates benevolence and rightness is a "mere fellow": "I have heard of the execution of the fellow Zhou; I have not heard of any regicide." In the end he proves one thing only — everyone can become a Yao or a Shun, so when you fall short, it is not that you cannot, it is that you will not.
