History of the Peloponnesian War

Thucydides · late fifth century BCE

中文 →

In one sentence

An Athenian general who lost a city and was exiled for it spent the next two decades recording, year by year, how his own state went from the most brilliant thing in Greece to a ruin — and while recording it he did something nobody had done before: he deleted the gods, fate, omens and moral bookkeeping from the list of causes, and left only human beings — what they fear, what they calculate, and what they turn into under pressure. The result is both a war narrative and the first attempt to make politics a reusable body of knowledge. Its coldest conclusion: justice exists only between parties of roughly equal strength; everywhere else there is only strong and weak.

Where it sits

Thucydides (c. 460 – c. 400 BCE), an Athenian, was elected strategos (general) in 424 and exiled by his own city for twenty years after failing to hold the northern town of Amphipolis. The exile became the book's enabling condition: he writes that precisely because he was no longer in Athens he could reach both sides of the war, the Peloponnesians not least (paraphrased). This is the shared starting point of Western historiography and of political science: virtually every textbook of what is now called realism in international politics opens with him.

The central claims

  • The real cause of a war and the causes the belligerents state out loud are two different things. What the parties argue about is a list of specific grievances; what actually drives them to war is a structural fact — Athens was growing, and Sparta was afraid. Separating those two layers is, in itself, the birth of political analysis.
  • States are driven by three things: fear, honour, and interest. Not good and evil. And because those three are constants of human nature, history repeats — not particular events, but the same reaction to the same kind of situation.
  • The logic of power turns around and eats the powerful. The more expertly Athens acts on "the strong do what they can," the faster it destroys itself. Thucydides never says so. He simply places the massacre at Melos and the catastrophe in Sicily one after the other.
  • Character is not fixed; it is what circumstances permit. Plague, civil war and prolonged fighting turn one population into another — and the meanings of words rot along with them. This is the deepest layer of the book and the least often quoted.

The core concepts, one at a time

"A possession for all time" — a new kind of writing, at the price of showing the gods the door

To see what he did, look at what came before. Herodotus, a generation older, wrote the Persian Wars gloriously: oracles come true, dreams warn, a tyrant's hubris draws divine punishment. That is meaningful history — things happen this way because the universe keeps a moral ledger.

Thucydides lays out his method in a single paragraph, Book 1 chapter 22, and it is the foundation of everything after. He says, in substance: as for the facts, I did not trust the first account I happened to hear, nor my own impressions, but checked as far as possible — even though eyewitnesses to the same event constantly contradict one another; as for the speeches, I could not recall the exact words, so I have written what the situation called for, while staying as close as I could to the general sense of what was actually said. Then comes the famous self-positioning: the absence of myth-like material (to mē muthōdes) will, he fears, make it less charming to hear; but he wrote it not as a prize essay to win applause once (agōnisma), but as a possession for all time (ktēma es aiei).

"A possession for all time" is not an author's boast; it is a functional claim. What he is writing is not what happened but something that will still work later. Work by what mechanism? By the next clause — as long as human nature (to anthrōpinon, "the human element") stays the same, similar things will recur in similar ways. The intended reader is therefore some future person facing a comparable situation, not the Athenian audience of the day.

The price is paid in the same move. Strike oracles, portents and divine retribution out of the causal chain and history loses its moral guarantee — there is no longer a backstop rule that the wicked must lose. It makes his narrative cold to the point of inhumanity, and it also makes it, for the first time, checkable and transferable. That flat register you find in any serious conflict assessment today — capabilities, intentions, constraints, and not a word about who deserves to win — starts here. Fittingly, the first complete English translation was made by Thomas Hobbes (1628), his first published book; the political world of Leviathan, with no god and no good or evil, only fear and self-preservation, has an unmistakable pedigree.

Pretext and true cause: the cut between aitiai and prophasis

The Greeks argued about who started the war, and they argued about specifics: whether Athens should have intervened in the sea fight between Corcyra and Corinth, whether besieging Potidaea broke the treaty, whether the trade embargo against Megara was an act of hostility. Thucydides files all of that under one phrase — aitiai kai diaphorai, the charges and the disputes: the reasons the parties put on the table.

Then, in Book 1 chapter 23, he writes the most quoted judgement in the book (paraphrased): "the truest cause (alēthestatē prophasis), and the one least spoken of publicly, was that the Athenians were growing great and inspiring fear in the Spartans, and so compelled them to war."

The cut separates two levels. Aitiai are the fuses — the concrete items each side hurls at the other in public. Prophasis here names the deeper condition that never reaches the table and yet does the actual work: a shift in the balance of strength. (In Greek, prophasis ordinarily leans toward "the reason given"; by attaching "truest" to it, Thucydides is saying that the real reason is precisely the one not given.)

Why does the distinction matter? If the cause is a set of specific grievances, settling them prevents the war; if the cause is structural, every grievance is merely a pretext picked up off the ground — settle this one and the next one grows by itself. Move it to unrelated ground and it still works: a couple fighting about the bins, two departments at war over one budget line. What can be articulated is aitiai; what cannot be said out loud — who is gaining ground in this relationship and who feels threatened by it — is the prophasis. Aim at the wrong level and you enter the loop where each settled pretext breeds two more.

So why did Athens grow strong enough to frighten Sparta? For this he inserts a fifty-year digression (scholars call it the pentecontaetia) on how, after the Persian Wars, Athens turned a voluntary anti-Persian alliance — the Delian League — into an empire step by step: the treasury moved from Delos to Athens, contributions hardened into tribute, leaving became rebellion. He records Pericles' own words for it: our rule is by now like a tyranny; it may have been wrong to take it, but it is no longer safe to let it go (paraphrased). An empire is not a choice you can stop making; it is a vehicle whose brakes it has already removed itself.

Fear, honour, interest: a theory of motive with no good or evil in it

On the eve of the war, Athenian envoys stand up in Sparta and explain in public where their empire came from. Their account, in substance: we did not seize this position by violence; we were pushed into it — first by fear, then for honour, and finally for interest. The three Greek words: deos (fear), timē (honour, standing), ōphelia (advantage).

What each layer actually is. Fear is the base: in an environment with no arbiter, where nobody can promise you won't be swallowed next year, expansion is a form of self-preservation — you take one more strait not from greed but because you cannot sleep while someone else holds it. Honour is the layer moderns most underrate and that proves most stubborn: states, like people, need their weight acknowledged, and a great power cannot absorb being publicly slighted by a small one even when the slight costs it nothing. Interest is the easy one: tribute, grain routes, markets.

The point of the triad is not the three words but what is missing from them: not one syllable about justice, piety or decency. The envoys' full argument runs: any city in our position would do what we do, so reproaching us is pointless; we are in fact more restrained than most strong powers, and you should be grateful. Inside that is realism's central assertion: a state's behaviour is determined mainly by its position and its strength, not by its character. Swap in another city at Athens' desk and the script barely changes.

How it changes the way you look at things: once you hold this frame, you stop asking whether the other side is wicked and start asking what it fears, what standing it is defending, and what account it is settling. That is analytically powerful — it lets you predict an opponent you dislike. It also carries a danger worth stating plainly: Thucydides puts this doctrine in the mouths of Athenian envoys, not in his own voice. Recording the self-defence of the strong is not the same as signing it.

The Melian Dialogue: justice exists only between equals in power

In 416 BCE Athens sends a fleet to Melos — a neutral island, a Spartan colony, which had never attacked Athens. Here Thucydides breaks his own form: instead of speeches he writes an exchange, the only dialogue in the book, and by common consent the passage he polished hardest.

The Melians open with right: we have done you no wrong, attacking neutrals is unjust, the gods will not side with you. The Athenian reply is stripped of every ornament (paraphrased): we will not waste time on fine words. The standard of justice holds only between parties equally able to compel each other; what actually happens is always this — the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must.

Each round then closes off another exit:

  • "Then let us stay neutral." No — your neutrality would read, to our subject states, as proof of weakness: cities that hate us prove our strength; independent neighbours prove only that we dare not act. (The coldest mechanism in the book: an empire must be cruel because it rules on the credibility of its threats, and sparing one party spends some of that credibility.)
  • "We still have hope." Hope is a luxury — only those with something in reserve can afford to gamble on it; for those with nothing, staking hope is staking everything.
  • "The gods favour the pious." The answer spreads the whole worldview out: we expect no less of the gods than you do, because what we do is nothing but the law that men acknowledge and the gods themselves follow — those who can rule, rule. We did not make this law; we found it in force and act by it, and we believe you would do the same in our place.
  • "The Spartans will come for us." The Spartans never take risks for others; they do what is safe for themselves. (Confirmed later in the text: the Spartans did not come.)

The outcome is recorded in a few lines of near-bureaucratic prose: the city fell, every adult male was killed, the women and children sold, Athenian colonists sent out. Thucydides offers not one word of comment.

It has lasted twenty-four centuries because it makes the internal structure of "might is right" explicit: it is not that the strong think justice does not exist, but that the condition under which the concept holds — both parties able to compel the other — is absent here. That is far more precise than "the weak are prey," and far harder to refute. It also explains in passing why there is international law between great powers and mostly posture between a great power and a small one.

But the real stroke comes in the next book. Right after Melos, he begins the Sicilian expedition. The Athens that has just announced that the strong do what they can turns around and buries its best fleet and army in Sicily. He never writes the word "retribution." He simply puts the two in that order. It is the most effective silence in the book.

Civil war at Corcyra: when words start to lie

In 427 BCE the Athenian ally Corcyra collapsed into civil war — the Greeks called it stasis, faction and mutual slaughter inside a single city, the worst disaster in the Greek political imagination. Democrats and oligarchs purged each other; men were killed in temples, fathers killed sons. After the killing, Thucydides inserts the heaviest analysis in the book (Book 3, chapters 82–83), and its subject is no longer Corcyra but human beings.

The thesis first (paraphrased): war, by stripping away the easy supply of daily life, becomes a teacher that instructs by violence (biaios didaskalos) — note both halves of the metaphor: a teacher changes the student, and this teacher's method is violence, so violence is what the student learns. Nothing in human nature was created; it was merely permitted.

Then the famous inventory of corrupted words (paraphrased): reckless daring was called loyal courage; prudent delay was called the excuse of a coward; moderation was called a cover for unmanliness; being able to see every side of a question meant being unfit to act on any. Also: the man who succeeded in a plot was clever, the man who detected one cleverer; readiness to avenge was more respected than readiness to survive; and even the oaths between allies held only so long as neither side had a better option.

What makes this extraordinary is that it names an observable early indicator of social collapse: not the amount of violence, but the drift of words. When "moderate" starts to mean "traitor," when "restraint" starts to mean "weakness," when "people on our side" becomes the only moral unit — the violence has not begun, but its linguistic infrastructure is already laid. Once the words are swapped out, the middle position no longer exists in the language, and a position that does not exist in the language is one nobody can stand on.

So in any argument that is heating up, the first thing to watch is not who is right but whether the key words are quietly changing meaning. Online pile-ons, office factions, any political polarization — hold this 2,400-year-old list against them and the items tend to match one by one. He adds a diagnosis (paraphrased): these things will happen again, because human nature is the same. Not fatalism — a warning that this reaction is the default setting and has to be resisted deliberately.

The Funeral Oration and the plague: the ideal and its dissolution, side by side

Book 2 contains an arrangement nobody forgets. First comes Pericles' Funeral Oration (epitaphios), one of the most famous political speeches in the Western record. He praises no individual dead; he praises the city worth dying for: our constitution puts power in the hands of the many; poverty bars no one of ability; we live privately as we please and publicly under law; we love beauty without extravagance and wisdom without softness; Athens is the school of Greece (paraphrased). That passage defined what later ages would mean by "citizen" and "open society," and has been quoted at funerals, on monuments and in constitutional preambles ever since.

And then the very next chapter is the plague.

In 430 BCE a disease of unknown origin arrived by ship in a besieged Athens crowded with refugees from the countryside. Thucydides had it himself, and his account is the earliest systematic description of symptoms in medical literature: it began with violent heat in the head and inflamed eyes, bleeding in throat and tongue, foul breath; then coughing, chest pain, retching; the skin reddened and broke out in small blisters; sufferers could not bear the lightest clothing and wanted only to throw themselves into cold water; most died on the seventh to ninth day, and survivors often lost fingers, toes, or their memory. He explicitly refuses to give a cause, leaving physicians and laymen alike to speculate about where it came from (paraphrased) — he would rather leave a blank than fill it with a story.

Heavier than the symptoms is the second half: the dissolution of moral order. Athenians began consuming the present; funeral rites lost their forms and bodies were flung onto pyres others had lit; fear of the gods failed, because the pious and the impious died alike, and fear of the law failed, because nobody expected to live long enough to stand trial.

Read the two chapters together and you have his real method of argument. He does not write "ideals are fragile." He sets the city's most perfect self-portrait a dozen pages before its dissolution and lets you draw the conclusion. And it is not only a literary device; it is a substantive claim. The virtues in that speech — lawfulness, public spirit, a sense of honour — are not Athenian nature but the product of particular conditions: safety, order, a predictable future. Remove the conditions and the virtues go with them. Civilization is not a quality; it is a state. Pericles himself, incidentally, died of the same disease not long after delivering the speech.

Sicily: how caution manufactures a catastrophe

In 415 BCE Athens votes to attack Syracuse in Sicily — an opponent of comparable size, overseas, barely connected to the main war. It is the climax of the book and its most instructive failure.

The decisive detail is almost absurdly small. The veteran Nicias opposed the expedition, so in the assembly he tried a tactic: exaggerate the troops and money required, and frighten the Athenians off. The Athenians heard him out, decided that in that case they would raise exactly that much — and voted the expedition through at his own inflated numbers. A limited adventure was upgraded into a national stake by the man most determined to stop it. Talking up the risk to deter a collective that has already grown excited is a way of raising its bet for it.

Every step after that adds to the pile. Alcibiades, the expedition's chief advocate, was recalled to stand trial over a religious scandal after sailing, defected to Sparta instead, and handed Athens' operational intentions to the enemy. Sparta sent the general Gylippus to Syracuse. The besieger became the besieged. The last blow is nearly black comedy: the army finally decided to withdraw, a lunar eclipse fell that night, and Nicias, a believer in divination, delayed the retreat by "thrice nine days" — twenty-seven of them — on the seers' advice. The author who had scrubbed oracles out of causation for seven books here records superstition killing an army outright.

The end: the fleet destroyed inside the harbour, the land army broken at the river Assinarus, soldiers drinking water fouled with blood out of thirst; Nicias and Demosthenes executed, the survivors thrown into the Syracusan quarries as slaves. His summary (paraphrased): this was the greatest action of the war and for the losers the most calamitous — fleet, army, everything destroyed; many went out and few came home.

Unpacked, the mechanism repeats daily in modern organizations:

  • Decision detached from information. Most of those voting had no idea how large Sicily was or how many cities it held; Thucydides notes the ignorance explicitly.
  • Opposing by inflating the cost ends up endorsing the project (see above).
  • The objective quietly expands during execution: from "support an ally" to "conquer Sicily," with no moment of explicit re-decision.
  • Domestic score-settling wrecks the front: the cost of recalling Alcibiades was paid by the expedition.
  • The moment to cut losses is postponed for an irrational reason: the withdrawal was decided and then delayed twenty-seven days, when the window was a few.

He gives the deeper diagnosis elsewhere, assessing Pericles (paraphrased): Pericles led the people by his standing rather than being led by them; those who came after competed for popular favour and so handed public affairs over to the crowd's passing moods. Sicily is that sentence coming due. Note what he is criticizing: not democracy, but democracy after its leaders begin bidding for affection. And he records the other half too — even after losing everything staked in Sicily, Athens held out for nearly another decade, which, he says, shows how deep its reserves had been (paraphrased).

The distilled skeleton

What this thick book circles, again and again, is one line: it wants to prove that politics can be studied as a mechanism — and its method of proof is not argument but arrangement.

Book 1 lays the rules: the method, the cut (pretext versus true cause), and the fifty-year digression explaining where Athenian power came from — a checkable way of writing, a tool for layering causes, a structural origin. After that he largely stops arguing and simply orders the material so each juxtaposition speaks for itself:

  • Funeral Oration → plague: the city's most perfect self-portrait, then its dissolution. Conclusion: virtue is a function of conditions.
  • A rebellion debated → Corcyra: first an assembly that can still reverse its own death sentence the next morning, then a civil war entirely out of control, with the inventory of rotted words attached. Conclusion: remove the constraints and the floor of human nature shows.
  • Melian Dialogue → Sicily: the nakedest statement of power, then the destruction of those who made it. Conclusion: the logic of power is not wrong, but whoever follows it to the end runs into his own limit.

So what does it establish? Not "might is right" — that is what Athenian envoys say at Melos, not what the author says. It establishes this: states are driven by fear, honour and interest; those motives are constants of human nature, so behaviour recurs when situations resemble each other; and the more expertly a state acts on that logic alone, the faster it delivers itself into catastrophe. Established by what? By facts checked at first hand; by writing each side's strongest case as a speech in opposition rather than adjudicating for the reader; and by the order in which things are placed. In the end the book's persuasive force comes from the fact that its author never once steps forward to say "you see?"

Misreadings and honest criticism

  • The "Thucydides Trap" is not Thucydides. The phrase is a framework proposed by the political scientist Graham Allison around 2015 for discussing US–China relations (later the book Destined for War): a rising power and an established one are prone to war through structural fear. It is genuinely distilled from Book 1.23 — but Thucydides himself never says such a war is unavoidable; he devotes an entire book to specific diplomatic failures, misjudgements and roads not taken. Critics, classicists among them, have also argued that Allison's tally of sixteen historical cases, twelve of them ending in war, is hard to sustain on case selection and coding. Compressing a book about contingency into a single law is exactly what the book itself is on guard against.
  • "He is objective and takes no side" is the biggest misreading of all. He wrote the speeches — by his own statement he wrote "what the situation called for," which makes every speech simultaneously evidence and composition, and scholars have argued for two centuries about what that sentence precisely licenses. His selection is heavily skewed: almost nothing on economic life, almost nothing on women, very little on Sparta's internal affairs (he could not get the information), and his preference for Pericles is not concealed. He is also a participant — he fought, was exiled, had the plague — and some of his figures and chronology sit awkwardly beside other sources and the archaeology. F. M. Cornford went further in Thucydides Mythistoricus (1907): the narrative skeleton is a Greek tragedy — transgression, blindness, ruin — with "human nature" installed where the gods used to be. His greatness is not that he is perfectly accurate but that he was the first to make accuracy the standard, and to state his method and its limits in public.
  • "He certified realism" is also a simplification. The line through Hobbes, Hans Morgenthau and Kenneth Waltz does treat him as the founder. But another reading — Richard Ned Lebow's The Tragic Vision of Politics, for instance — points out that the book's structure is precisely that the more realist Athens becomes, the worse it ends: the logic voiced at Melos gets its invoice from Sicily four years later. Whether he is stating a law or exhibiting a law destroying itself depends on whether you read one passage or the whole work.
  • "Athens lost because it was a democracy" moves too fast. What he criticizes is leaders competing for favour after Pericles, and an assembly swinging between fear and hope — not majority rule as such; he even praises the constitution of the Five Thousand in 411 as well run (paraphrased). And Athens held out for nearly a decade after losing everything it had staked in Sicily, which is not much of an argument that the system was doomed.
  • The pathogen of the Athenian plague is still undetermined. Smallpox, typhus, plague, measles and viral haemorrhagic fevers have all been proposed. In 2006 a Greek team tested dental pulp from a mass grave in the Kerameikos cemetery in Athens and reported Salmonella enterica Typhi (typhoid fever); other researchers immediately challenged the finding on methodological and contamination grounds, and it has not become consensus. He declined to name a cause, and 2,400 years later the blank is still there.
  • The book is unfinished. The text breaks off in the middle of the events of 411 BCE — in the middle of a sentence. The last seven years of the war, including Athens' surrender in 404, come to us through Xenophon's Hellenica. The structure is also uneven: some years are narrated almost day by day, others in a few lines, and Book 5 contains long stretches that read like transcribed treaties — evidence, for some scholars, of repeated revision never harmonized.

Ten sentences

① He deleted the gods — not out of impiety, but because once "fate" is allowed into causation, history stops being reusable knowledge.

② The book's first cut is a distinction of levels: what the parties argue about is pretext (aitiai); what drives the war is the thing not said out loud (prophasis) — Athens growing, Sparta afraid.

③ Which means: settling articulable grievances gets you nowhere, because the unsayable thing keeps manufacturing new ones.

④ States are moved by fear, honour and interest, with no good or evil in the list — but remember that this comes from Athenian envoys, not from the author's signature.

⑤ "The strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must" means something precise: justice requires that both sides be able to compel each other, and that condition is absent here.

⑥ An empire must be cruel because it rules on the credibility of its threats — sparing one neutral is, in the eyes of every subject state, credibility spent. The coldest mechanism in the book.

⑦ The earliest signal of social collapse is not violence but the drift of words: recklessness renamed courage, prudence renamed cowardice, moderation renamed unmanliness.

⑧ The Funeral Oration sits immediately before the plague, and that is his argument: civilization is not a quality but a state; withdraw the conditions and the virtues withdraw too.

⑨ Pericles himself admits it: our rule is by now like a tyranny, wrong perhaps to have taken, but no longer safe to release — an empire is not a choice you can stop making; it is a vehicle that has removed its own brakes.

⑩ Nicias inflated the forces required in order to stop the expedition, and the assembly approved it at his numbers; later he delayed the retreat twenty-seven days over an eclipse. The most cautious man in the room is often the one who makes the disaster large.
