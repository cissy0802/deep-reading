Reality+

Reality+: Virtual Worlds and the Problems of Philosophy · David Chalmers · 2022

中文 →

In One Sentence

You assume the opposite of "virtual" is "real." This book spends four hundred pages saying no. The things in a virtual world are real things, the events there really happen, and a life led there can be a genuinely good life — and if it turns out that we ourselves are running inside some enormous computer, that would not mean you have been deceived. The table in front of you would still be a real table. It would just be built out of bits rather than quarks. Making that case rigorously, question by question, is the whole of what this book does.

Coordinates

David Chalmers (1966– ) is an Australian philosopher at NYU and one of the most influential philosophers of mind alive. In the mid-1990s he named the hard problem of consciousness, which redrew the map of an entire field (see "Chalmers Beyond This Book" below); he is also co-author, with Andy Clark, of the famous 1998 paper on the extended mind. Reality+ (2022) is what happens when he runs the old problems of mind and metaphysics through digital technology. Its seed was an essay he wrote in 2003 for the official Matrix website, "The Matrix as Metaphysics." He has a name for the method: technophilosophy — using technology as a laboratory for thought experiments in one hand, and philosophy to see clearly what the technology actually delivers in the other. The book is long, in seven parts, and hangs the entire introductory-philosophy syllabus on this one hook.

The Central Claims

Three claims, and they form a chain:

  • Virtual realism: virtual reality is genuine reality. Virtual worlds are not illusions, not fictions, not second-class substitutes. Virtual objects are real digital objects, and what happens in a virtual world really happens.
  • The simulation hypothesis is not a sceptical hypothesis but a metaphysical one. The whole tradition from Descartes onward assumes that if a demon is fooling me, my beliefs about the world are all false. Chalmers says that step is simply wrong: even inside a simulation, nearly everything you believe about tables, trees and friends is still true. You would only have been wrong about what the world is made of underneath.
  • A virtual life can be a meaningful life. Equating VR with Nozick's experience machine is a mistake: the machine is a pre-scripted solo performance, while a virtual world contains real actions, real consequences and real other people.

The Core Concepts, One by One

1. The problem of the external world: from the cave to the vat

You have to see what the book is dismantling, or everything after it is just a slogan.

Philosophy has a question that has refused to go away for two and a half millennia: how do you know the world in front of you is really there? Chalmers runs through the classic scenes. Plato's cave, where prisoners see only shadows on a wall and take them for the things themselves. Zhuangzi's butterfly dream: waking, he cannot say whether he is a man who dreamt he was a butterfly or a butterfly now dreaming it is a man. Descartes' evil demon — suppose an all-powerful spirit is feeding a seamless external world directly into my mind, in which case every belief I hold about things outside me is false. And its modern versions: Hilary Putnam's brain in a vat, wired to a computer that supplies all its input, and The Matrix.

All of these have the same shape, and all are used to drive the same conclusion: you have no way, from the inside, to tell the real world from a perfect fake, so you don't know which one you're in — and if you're in the fake, almost everything you believe is false. Notice that is two steps, and all the destructive power lives in the second. Chalmers accepts the first (you really can't tell) and puts his knife straight through the second. That cut is the hinge of the book.

2. A metaphysical hypothesis, not a sceptical one

The simulation hypothesis says: this universe, your body and brain included, is a program running on some computer, and you are one of its processes. The reflex response is, "So I'm living inside a con?"

Chalmers' central claim, in paraphrase: the simulation hypothesis is not a sceptical hypothesis (your beliefs are false) but a metaphysical one (this is what the world is made of). That distinction is the single best thing to carry out of the book. Take a concrete belief — "there is a table in front of me." Inside a simulation, is it true or false? The sceptic says false: there's no wood there, only data moving through circuits. Chalmers says true: there really is a table there, and it is made of bits.

His analogy is powerful. The history of physics is a history of discovering that things are made of something other than we thought — and not once did any of those discoveries make tables unreal. Once a table was solid continuous stuff; then it was atoms, which turn out to be mostly empty space; then quarks and electrons, which are better described as excitations of quantum fields and are not little balls at all. Every time, the X in "a table is made of X" got replaced, and "there is a table" stayed true. So swap the fields for bits. Why should that substitution, and no other, turn the table into a fake?

He puts it another way: the simulation hypothesis is a creation story. It says the universe began when someone started a program — which is the same kind of statement as "God made the world in seven days" or "it started with a Big Bang." Those are claims about where a world came from, not claims that your experience is a swindle. Creation stories don't make the created world fake.

3. Structuralism: why a table made of bits is still a table

That cut needs a handle or it's just assertion. The handle is structuralism about physical concepts — the hardest technical component in the book, and the one most readers skip.

The idea: physics tells us about structure — what stands in what relation to what, interacting by which laws — and does not tell us what any of it is intrinsically. Open a physics textbook. Mass is defined as the quantity that plays a certain role in F=ma; charge as the property that interacts according to Coulomb's law. Follow the definitions down and every term is fixed by its position in the structure; none is fixed by what stuff it is made of. This isn't Chalmers' invention — Russell argued in The Analysis of Matter (1927) that physics gives us the structure of the external world and not its intrinsic nature.

The inference then runs easily. If "table" and "electron" pick out whatever it is that plays that causal role, then in a sufficiently fine-grained, stable simulation, where the organisation of bits plays exactly the electron role, the table role, the role of your hand — then those bits are electrons, tables and your hand. You were not wrong about the table. You were wrong about its implementation, and the implementation was never part of what you believed. Physics has a slogan on this side too: John Wheeler's "it from bit", the conjecture that physical reality may be informational all the way down. If reality could be made of information anyway, then "we're in a simulation" and "we're in an informational universe" are nearly the same sentence — one just adds a programmer.

4. What counts as real: five criteria and virtual digitalism

"Virtual things are real" sounds like word-play until you say what "real" means. So Chalmers does something clarifying: he pulls apart the several things ordinary language bundles into that one word, and tests virtual objects against each.

A sense of "real"What it demandsDo virtual objects pass?
ExistenceIt's there; it isn't nothingYes. The data structure really is on the server
Causal powersIt has effects; you can interact with itYes. A virtual wall really stops you; virtual money really buys things
Mind-independenceIt doesn't depend on what any one mind thinksYes. Log out and the virtual city is still there
Non-illusorinessIt is as it appears; it isn't fooling youYes — provided you know you're in VR. Being kept in the dark is another matter
GenuinenessIt's a real X, not a counterfeit XDepends which X. A virtual cat is a genuine digital object, but not a genuine biological cat
Chalmers' five criteria for reality. The last row is the crucial one: a virtual cat isn't a counterfeit, it's a different kind of thing.

That fifth row is the subtle one and the most misread. In the contrast between a real diamond and a fake, the fake is fake because it is passing itself off as a diamond. A virtual cat passes itself off as nothing; it is a virtual cat, and a perfectly genuine digital object. "Virtual" is not a synonym for "fake"; it names a kind — the way "inflatable" isn't a synonym for fake. A bouncy castle is not a counterfeit castle. It's another sort of castle.

Hence his position, virtual digitalism: virtual objects are digital objects — real data structures and processes with determinate properties and causal powers, which several people can see and manipulate together. The rival view is virtual fictionalism: the sword in the game is like the sword in a novel, something we merely pretend exists, so "I picked up the sword" is strictly false and true only within the fiction. (This is defended seriously in the literature — by McDonnell and Wildman among others; the argument is live.) Chalmers' reply is blunt: fictional objects have no causal powers, and virtual objects do. Sherlock Holmes cannot take money out of your bank account; a virtual item can sell for a real price and a virtual transaction can really bankrupt someone. Whatever has consequences is not a fiction.

5. The simulation argument: how likely is it that we're sims?

Everything so far was about what follows if we're simulated. This is the other question: are we?

The starting point is Nick Bostrom's simulation argument (2003), which doesn't assert that we are simulated but forces a trilemma: at least one of these is true — (1) almost all civilisations go extinct before acquiring the power to run large numbers of high-fidelity simulated civilisations; (2) almost all civilisations that acquire it have no interest in using it; (3) we are almost certainly living in a simulation.

The bones are simple. If (1) and (2) both fail, then simulated minds like ours vastly outnumber original ones — a mature civilisation can run thousands of civilisations, while the original runs once. If sims are the overwhelming majority and you can't tell from the inside which you are, the odds say bet on sim. (Reasoning that treats you as a typical sample from all observers of your kind is called anthropic reasoning, and it is contested; the criticism section settles that account.)

Chalmers accepts the shape of the argument and offers a rough figure of his own: perhaps a one-in-four chance that we are simulated — a number he explicitly presents as a way of saying "this is not dismissible," not as a calculation. He also distinguishes kinds of simulation, because the consequences differ: global vs local (the whole universe simulated, or only your immediate surroundings rendered), pure vs impure (your mind is itself part of the program, or you have a biological brain wired in, as in The Matrix). Note that only in a pure simulation must the computer be generating the consciousness of the people in it — which is the load-bearing assumption hidden under the whole argument.

6. Are simulated people conscious? The hidden pillar

This looks like a side-branch and is in fact a supporting wall. The simulation argument needs simulated people to be conscious. If the "people" running on the computer are hollow — no inner experience — then minds like ours were never created inside simulations, the numerical majority evaporates, and the argument fails on the spot.

Here there's a nice tension. Chalmers is the most prominent anti-reductionist about consciousness alive: he holds that consciousness cannot be reduced to physical processes — that is the hard problem. So why should a silicon sim be conscious?

His answer is an argument from his earlier work, organizational invariance: consciousness depends on a system's fine-grained functional organisation — the pattern of causal interaction among its parts — and not on the material that implements it. The support is the fading qualia thought experiment. Replace the neurons in your brain one at a time with silicon chips of identical function, so that at every step the external behaviour and the internal causal structure are unchanged. What happens to your experience? Saying it blinks out when one particular neuron is swapped is absurd — which one, and why that one? Saying it fades gradually is worse, because it yields a middle state in which your experience has dimmed almost to nothing while your brain goes on scrupulously reporting "I see a vivid red" — you would have lost all ability to judge your own experience, which makes no sense. The only coherent option left: consciousness tracks functional organisation, not material.

So the position is a combination punch: consciousness is irreducible (the hard problem stands) plus consciousness follows organisation (so silicon can have it). That is what licenses him to treat sims as people and virtual worlds as real worlds — without it, the whole value half of the book collapses — and it is where he takes the heaviest fire.

7. Simulation theology

A by-product he writes lightly, though it isn't a joke. If we are in a simulation, the simulator meets nearly every classical criterion for a god relative to our world: they created it; within it they are omnipotent (they can change any line of code) and omniscient (they can read any state); and they stand outside its space and time. The point is not that religion has been vindicated, but that "there is a creator" can be a thoroughly naturalistic hypothesis — the simulator might be a graduate student in the next universe over. The old theological difficulties come along too, notably the problem of evil: if a creator is all-powerful and all-good, why the suffering? The simulation-flavoured answer is uncomfortable but practical: the simulator may be neither all-good nor especially interested in us. They may just be running an experiment.

8. Can a virtual life be a good life? The experience machine

The last link is the personal one. Even if virtual worlds are real, would spending your life in one be a wasted life?

The standard objection is Robert Nozick's experience machine (1974). Suppose a machine could give you any experience you wanted — writing a great novel, being deeply loved, standing on a summit — and you would forget you were plugged in, so it would feel entirely real. Would you plug in for the rest of your life? Nozick bets you wouldn't, and concludes: we care about more than experience. We care about actually doing things, actually becoming someone, actually being in contact with the world.

Chalmers' reply is to separate two things that get conflated:

Nozick's experience machineA virtual world
What happensNothing happens; only a stream of piped-in experienceEvents really occur at the digital level and leave consequences
Your agencyNone. The script is written; you are watchingReal. Your choices actually change the state of the world
Other peopleNo one is there — only scenery in human shapeReal people at the other end; real relationships
Do you know?No — and that is precisely what makes it disturbingYes. You know you are in VR. Nobody is deceiving you
Why "VR equals the experience machine" is a misdiagnosis: Nozick's target was a deceptive, passive, solitary performance — and a virtual world has none of those three features.

So Nozick's intuition is tracking deception, passivity and solitude, not non-physicality. Someone who really accomplishes things in a virtual world, really builds relationships there, and really knows where they are, is outside the reach of his objection. Chalmers concludes that a virtual life can in principle be as meaningful as a physical one; today it falls short, but that is a matter of resolution, haptics and bodily presence — a shortfall of engineering, not of principle.

He doesn't overplay it. The book concedes two hard problems. First, virtual worlds have owners — the "god" of your world is a corporation that can rewrite its physics, see everything you do, and expel you from the universe. Second, the body — today's VR covers sight and sound, while hunger, fatigue, touch and illness go on happening to the physical body, and those are not the trimmings of a life.

The Argument in Outline

Strip away the detail and the book is a single chain:

  • 1 From Plato to Descartes to The Matrix, scepticism takes two steps: you can't tell a real world from a perfect fake (granted); therefore in the fake all your beliefs are false (never actually established).
  • 2 The second step is wrong. Physical concepts pick out whatever plays the relevant causal role (structuralism), and in a simulation something plays those roles completely — so there really is a table there; it's made of bits.
  • 3 The simulation hypothesis therefore changes category, from sceptical to metaphysical: it belongs with "everything is made of quarks" and "it started with a Big Bang," not with "you are being conned."
  • 4 Turn the same ruler on VR: by existence, causal powers, mind-independence, non-illusoriness and genuineness, virtual objects are real digital objects — not fictions, since fictions have no causal powers.
  • 5 Are we in one? The trilemma forces an uncomfortable probability; his rough figure is around one in four. But that step quietly requires that simulated people be conscious — for which he has organizational invariance and the fading-qualia argument. That both saves the simulation argument and secures the reality of other people inside virtual worlds.
  • 6 So the value question lands: the experience-machine objection is about deception, passivity and solitude, not about being non-physical. A virtual life can in principle be a good life — and the real remaining question isn't "is it real?" but "who owns this world?"

Put differently, what the book establishes is this: "real" was never one line. It was several different lines that we had carelessly tied into one. Untie them and virtual worlds pass most of the tests — while the things genuinely worth fearing (deception, loss of agency, being owned) turn out to have nothing to do with virtuality. They apply just as well in the physical world.

Misreadings & Honest Criticism

Three common misreadings

  • "Chalmers says we're living in a simulation." He doesn't. He says it's a possibility not to be waved away (his rough figure: about one in four) and — this is the actual point — that it wouldn't be nearly as bad as you think. The thesis is "this is not a catastrophe," not "this is a fact."
  • "If virtual is real, then everything's real and fakery doesn't matter." The opposite. The fourth criterion, non-illusoriness, draws the line sharply: a virtual world you know you're in is not deception; one you're kept in the dark about is. Deepfakes are vicious precisely because they impersonate what they are not — and a virtual cat impersonates nothing.
  • "It's a metaverse advertorial." The book did land at the peak of the 2021–22 metaverse boom, but its arguments don't depend on those products, and it names the most dangerous feature of virtual worlds outright: yours has an owner who can rewrite its physics, read all your data, and throw you out. The fairer complaint is that he treats that lightly (below).

The strongest objections

  • Newman's problem: structuralism may prove too much. The most technical and most damaging hit. In 1928 the mathematician M. H. A. Newman objected to Russell's structuralism: if a description of the world says only "there exist things standing in such-and-such a structure," then given enough elements almost any collection can be arranged to satisfy it — so the claim is nearly empty. Applied here: if a table just is whatever plays the table role, "real" may have been thinned out to the point of saying almost nothing. The price of winning the argument that there are real tables in the simulation may be that the word "real" is no longer worth much. Chalmers knows the objection and tries to answer it (restricting to causal structure, using consciousness as a non-structural anchor), but nobody thinks the matter is settled.
  • The consciousness step is the soft spot in the whole building. The simulation argument needs conscious sims, and so does the claim that other people in virtual worlds are people. That rests on a functionalist premise, and the opposition is serious. John Searle's Chinese Room imagines someone with no Chinese sealed in a room, manipulating incoming Chinese characters by a rulebook; from outside the answers are fluent, and inside no understanding occurs at all. On that basis he argues that syntax is not sufficient for semantics or consciousness, which require the specific causal powers of biological brains. If that camp is right, there are no minds in a simulation: the numerical majority disappears, and the "other people" in virtual worlds are shells. Chalmers has his reply, but the book is staked on the most contested ground in philosophy of mind.
  • The anthropic reasoning is shaky. The argument asks you to treat yourself as a random sample from all observers of your kind (the self-sampling assumption). That style of reasoning is already in poor repute elsewhere: the same move generates the notorious Doomsday Argument, which infers from your typical position in the sequence of all humans that humanity is nearly finished — a conclusion most people find absurd without being able to say where it fails. How to draw the reference class, and why to assume random sampling, remain unsettled; the specific numbers built on top (25%, 99%, anything) should not be taken seriously.
  • The embodiment objection. From the embodied-cognition tradition (see Read 73, The Embodied Mind), cognition and value alike grow out of a body that gets hungry, gets tired and dies, and out of its history of action. Can a world that is audiovisually convincing but weightless, costless and reloadable grow the same kind of meaning? Climb a virtual mountain with no hypothermia and no fall available, and something is missing — Chalmers will say that's an engineering gap (risk can be designed in), and his opponents will say it's a matter of principle (a risk you can undo is not a risk).
  • Thin on political economy, and broad rather than deep. The book grants that virtual worlds have owners, but spends little on platform monopoly, surveillance advertising and addictive design. When your universe is operated by a listed company, "can a virtual life be meaningful?" stops being only a metaphysical question and becomes a question about power. Events helped the criticism along: after publication, metaverse investment cooled fast and the technology stayed far behind the ambition of the arguments. And hanging a full philosophy syllabus on one hook buys enormous readability at the cost of giving each specialist problem only introductory depth.

Chalmers Beyond This Book

Read only this book and you'd take Chalmers for a philosopher of VR. In fact the book is an outgrowth of a much larger project with one thing at its centre: consciousness.

The hard problem of consciousness. The distinction he drew in the mid-1990s, and his most important contribution. The easy problems are how the brain integrates information, discriminates stimuli, controls behaviour, reports its own states — hard work, but we know what an answer would look like (a mechanism). There is exactly one hard problem: why is any of that processing accompanied by experience at all? Why is there something it is like, on the inside, to process red light, rather than the whole thing running in the dark? The force of the framing is that this is not one more unsolved scientific question: it points out that no amount of mechanism will, on its own, deliver the existence of experience.

Philosophical zombies. The thought experiment behind the hard problem: imagine a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of you whose behaviour, speech and neural activity are identical — it says "of course I'm conscious" — but who has no experience whatever. If such a being is conceivable without contradiction, then consciousness is not a logical consequence of the physical facts, and physicalism is incomplete. From this he moves to naturalistic dualism: consciousness is a fundamental feature of the world, as basic as mass or charge, requiring laws of its own. (Opponents concentrate their fire on the step from conceivable to possible.) Following the same line, he takes panpsychism seriously without endorsing it — the view that consciousness, in some minimal form, is present at the fundamental physical level — on grounds of elimination: if consciousness can neither be reduced nor conjured out of complexity, perhaps it was there from the start.

The extended mind (1998, with Andy Clark). His second most influential paper, and directly relevant here. Otto has Alzheimer's and writes everything he needs to remember in a notebook he carries; Inga keeps it in her head. When they decide to visit the museum, Inga consults memory, Otto consults the notebook, and both set off. If the notebook plays exactly the causal role biological memory plays — always available, trusted, routinely used — on what grounds do we say Inga has a belief and Otto doesn't? The conclusion: the boundary of the mind isn't the skull; it's wherever the functional coupling ends. Put that beside Reality+ and his real position comes into view: minds can extend into technology and reality can be made of information — so entering a virtual world isn't a flight from the real. It's one more step in something minds have always done.

Ten Sentences

1. "Virtual reality is genuine reality." (paraphrased) — not consolation, but a claim with five criteria you can check one by one.

2. The simulation hypothesis is not a sceptical hypothesis but a metaphysical one: it says what the world is made of and where it came from, not that you're being conned. This is the sentence to keep.

3. We have rewritten what tables are made of several times — solid stuff, atoms, quarks, quantum fields — and not one of those rewrites made tables fake. Bits won't either.

4. Physics gives us structure, not material: an electron is whatever plays the electron role — so bits playing that role in a simulation are electrons.

5. "Virtual" is not a synonym for "fake"; it names a kind. A virtual cat is a real digital cat. It is simply not a cat in the biological sense.

6. One test separates the fictional from the virtual: Sherlock Holmes cannot take money out of your account, and a virtual item can. Whatever has consequences is not a fiction.

7. Under the argument that we're probably simulated sits a pillar nobody looks at: the simulated people have to be genuinely conscious. Remove it and the building falls.

8. Replace neurons one at a time with functionally identical chips and consciousness neither blinks out at some particular one nor quietly dims while you insist you see vivid red — so consciousness follows functional organisation, not material.

9. Nozick's experience machine chills us because it is deceptive, passive and empty of other people — not because it is non-physical. A virtual world you know you're in, act in, and share with real people escapes all three.

10. So the real question about a virtual life was never "is it real?" but "who owns this world?" — when your universe has a proprietor who can rewrite its physics, read everything you do, and expel you from it, that is the thing to worry about.
