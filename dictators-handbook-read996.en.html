The Dictator's Handbook

The Dictator's Handbook: Why Bad Behavior Is Almost Always Good Politics · Bruce Bueno de Mesquita & Alastair Smith · 2011

中文 →

In One Sentence

No leader rules alone; every one of them depends on a group of backers to stay in power, and the smaller that group, the more a leader can feed them and ignore everyone else. A dictator's brutality, graft and indifference to disaster victims usually aren't signs of unusual wickedness; within his power structure, they are simply the surest way to keep the job.

Where It Sits

Both authors are political scientists at New York University. The book is the popular version of The Logic of Political Survival (2003), an academic monograph they wrote with Randolph Siverson and James Morrow, recasting "selectorate theory," backed by game theory and cross-national data, as a coldly practical manual for rulers.

Core Arguments

  • The first goal of politics is not governing but surviving. President, king, general or CEO, the first job is to hold on to power; policy is a means to that end.
  • Every leader depends on a small set of indispensable people; regimes differ only in how big that set is. Democracy and dictatorship are not different in kind but two ends of a single variable: does the leader have to keep a few hundred people happy, or tens of millions?
  • With a small coalition, leaders buy loyalty with private rewards; with a large one, they have to deliver public goods. Good policy doesn't come from good people but from structures that force leaders to look after the many.

Put together: to predict what a leader will do, ignore the character and the slogans and count how many people he needs to stay in office.

The Key Concepts, Unpacked

1. Three circles: interchangeables, influentials, essentials

What it is. The authors divide the people in any polity, whether a country, a city, a company or a sports federation, into three nested groups:

  • Interchangeables, the "nominal selectorate": everyone with a formal say in choosing the leader. In a democracy, all voters; in a company, all shareholders.
  • Influentials, the "real selectorate": the people who actually decide. In Saudi Arabia, senior royals; in the Soviet Union, the Party's Central Committee; in a company, the board and a few big shareholders.
  • Essentials, the "winning coalition": the smallest group whose support the leader cannot lose without being removed. In a US presidential race, roughly the voting majorities in enough states to win the Electoral College; in a military junta, perhaps a dozen generals and the commanders of a few elite units.

Schematic: the three circles. Every decision a leader makes is first about keeping the innermost circle on side.

Analogy / example. Picture a listed company. Hundreds of thousands of retail shareholders are interchangeables: they can all vote, and almost none do. The board and a few institutional investors are influentials. The handful of board votes that can form a majority on whether the CEO stays are the essentials. What a CEO fears isn't retail investors grumbling; it's those few directors being lured away.

Why it matters. The circles replace the fuzzy democracy-versus-dictatorship binary with quantities you can compare. What really distinguishes regimes is the size of the inner circle relative to the outer ones, not whether there are elections or a constitution: everyone voted in the Soviet Union, yet the essentials were a tiny clique.

How it changes the way you see. Faced with any organization, ask three questions: who is entitled to choose the leader, who actually chooses, and whose defection would bring the leader down? Answer those and your predictions will usually beat anything in its charter or mission statement.

2. Coalition size decides everything: private goods or public goods

What it is. Leaders have limited resources and can spend them two ways. Private goods go only to supporters: concessions, kickbacks, cushy sinecures, immunity from prosecution. Public goods benefit everyone: rule of law, roads, schools, free speech, public health.

The key is the arithmetic: the smaller the inner circle, the cheaper it is to buy them with private goods. Ten generals can each be given a gold mine. If you depend on twenty million voters, you can't pay them all, so the efficient move is to spend on things everyone benefits from and deliver results the majority likes. Hence small-coalition regimes breed corruption and large-coalition regimes breed good policy. Democratic leaders aren't nobler; the structure pushes them into doing good.

Analogy / example. The authors' most striking case is King Leopold II of Belgium. At home he was a constitutional monarch answerable to parliament and voters, and he built railways and public works, a fairly enlightened ruler. But in the Congo Free State, his personal possession from 1885 to 1908, he answered to no local person at all, and in pursuit of rubber and ivory he ran a regime of forced labor, severed hands and hostage-taking that killed vast numbers of people (estimates vary widely, but the scale runs to millions). One man, two systems, two kinds of behavior. The lesson: don't ask whether he was good or evil; ask what structure he sat in.

Why it matters. It overturns the intuition that good leadership flows from good character. If institutions determine behavior, swapping in a better person often achieves nothing: put the newcomer in the same chair, dependent on the same small circle, and he soon behaves the same way.

How it changes the way you see. When an organization is riddled with graft and resources flow to a few, stop cursing the leader's greed and look at how small his inner circle is. What actually changes behavior is enlarging the group he has to please.

3. The loyalty norm: why a dictator's cronies rarely defect

What it is. Why don't the essentials just switch to a more generous leader? The answer is the size of the selectorate relative to the coalition. The bigger the outer pool and the smaller the inner circle, the more each essential knows two things: he could be replaced at any moment by someone from that vast pool, and if a new leader takes over, he will build his own circle, which probably won't include you. Loyalty to the incumbent is born of fear of replacement. The authors call this the "loyalty norm."

It explains a seeming paradox: why so many authoritarian regimes love universal elections. Every Soviet citizen had the vote, yet the outcome was never in doubt. Mass suffrage served not to give people a choice but to make the outer pool as big as possible, so that every senior official knew a long line of people was waiting to take his place. That is the book's second rule: keep the interchangeables as numerous as possible.

Analogy / example. Think of a pro sports team. If the bench holds dozens of players as good as you, you do what the coach says; if you're the only one who can play your position, you can negotiate. The dictator's art is to make every crony feel the bench is endless.

Why it matters. It explains why small-coalition regimes are often remarkably stable, with rulers lasting decades, while democratic leaders fall regularly: in a democracy the essentials lose little by switching, so their loyalty to any one leader is low, and a policy failure is enough to drop him.

How it changes the way you see. When you wonder how a terrible leader stays standing, check whether the people around him have anywhere else to go. Those with no exit are the most loyal, not from devotion but from calculation.

4. The five rules of ruling

What it is. The authors distill the logic into five pieces of "advice" to leaders; this is the "handbook" of the title:

  • Rule 1: Keep your winning coalition as small as possible. The fewer people you must please, the less each costs and the more you keep.
  • Rule 2: Keep your nominal selectorate as large as possible. Make every essential aware that plenty of others could replace him.
  • Rule 3: Control the flow of revenue. Whoever controls where the money comes from and where it goes decides who eats.
  • Rule 4: Pay your key supporters just enough to keep them loyal. Too little and they defect; too much and they can afford to set up on their own.
  • Rule 5: Don't take money out of your supporters' pockets to make the people's lives better. Money spent on the public is money the essentials see as taken from their plates.

Analogy / example. The authors take pains to show the rules aren't just for dictatorships. In Bell, a small city in California, city manager Robert Rizzo used a special election with extremely low turnout, only a few hundred votes, to turn Bell into a "charter city," escaping state limits on officials' pay. He set his own salary at roughly $787,000 a year plus lavish benefits and paid the council members who backed him handsomely, while residents' tax burden grew. After the scandal broke in 2010 he went to prison. A small American city ran the full small-coalition playbook. Companies, too: when HP's CEO Carly Fiorina pushed the Compaq merger in 2002, Walter Hewlett, son of a founder, fought her in a proxy battle; she won narrowly by securing key institutional votes, yet the board later lost faith in her and she was ousted in 2005.

Why it matters. It redescribes "bad behavior" as rational choice: in a small-coalition structure, corruption is not a malfunction of the system but how it runs. An anti-corruption drive that leaves the structure intact just changes who collects.

How it changes the way you see. Hold the five rules up against any organization, including your company or your club: how big is the inner circle, who controls the money, how many could replace the insiders? Much of what we call office politics turns out to be these rules in miniature.

5. Where the money comes from: the resource curse and aid that buys policy

What it is. A leader's money comes either from taxing the people or from sources that don't need the people: oil, minerals, foreign aid. The difference is huge. If you live off taxes, you need people working, motivated and productive, so you must grant some freedom and some public goods. If the money gushes out of the ground, your income no longer depends on their productivity; you just need enough guards around the oil fields.

That is the authors' account of the resource curse, the tendency of resource-rich countries to be poorer, more corrupt and more autocratic. Resources aren't poisonous in themselves; they free leaders from needing their people.

Foreign aid works the same way. The authors' claim is blunt: aid mainly buys policy rather than helping the poor. Donor leaders, usually democrats, want the recipient government's cooperation on foreign and security policy, cooperation often unpopular with the recipient's own people. If the recipient is a small-coalition regime, its leader can pass a share of the aid to his inner circle and sell the policy without worry. The book's examples include the large US aid flows to Egypt after the Camp David Accords and aid to Pakistan during the war on terror.

Analogy / example. When Cyclone Nargis devastated Myanmar in 2008, killing well over a hundred thousand people, the junta restricted foreign relief workers for weeks. In the authors' framework this is no mystery: the victims were not in the coalition, and letting foreigners in could have weakened the junta's grip on the situation and the money. For a small-coalition leader, disaster relief is not a duty but a risk.

Why it matters. It throws cold water on well-meaning intervention: aid channeled through a small-coalition government is likely to entrench that government rather than improve lives.

How it changes the way you see. When judging a government or an organization, first ask where its money mainly comes from. Whoever doesn't need your productivity won't care about your welfare.

6. The most dangerous moments: early days, deathbeds, and empty coffers

What it is. Leaders aren't equally safe at all times. The authors point to several moments of special vulnerability:

  • Just after taking power. The new leader doesn't yet know who is reliable, and the essentials don't know if they'll be kept on, so coups and purges cluster in the first years. Many new autocrats start by purging the very people who helped them seize power, since those people know best how it's done.
  • When gravely ill or dying. Essentials are loyal because they expect future rewards; once the leader looks to have little time left, that future vanishes and they start betting on the successor. That's why autocrats' health is often a state secret.
  • When the money runs out. Revolutions, the authors argue, tend to break out not when people are most miserable but when the leader can no longer pay the army and the inner circle: an economic crisis, aid cut off, a collapse in commodity prices. What often decides a revolution is whether soldiers ordered to fire on the crowd actually fire.

Analogy / example. Picture a firm that keeps its key staff with big bonuses. As long as there's cash, nobody leaves. The moment cash flow dries up, or the boss announces retirement with no successor named, the key people start job-hunting and the firm can unravel within weeks.

Why it matters. It shows that a regime's stability depends not on its popularity but on the inner circle's expectations of future rewards, which gives a clear starting point for questions like "why did that regime suddenly collapse?"

How it changes the way you see. To judge whether an organization is stable, don't look at surface support; look at whether the essentials still believe in tomorrow's paycheck.

The Spine of the Argument

1. Premise: leaders want above all to keep power, and nobody can hold it alone.

2. Structure: every polity has three circles, interchangeables, influentials and essentials, and the leader must keep satisfying the essentials.

3. Mechanism: when the coalition is small, private goods are cheap and graft and repression become optimal; when it is large, private goods are too expensive and public goods and good policy become optimal. The ratio of the outer pool to the inner circle sets the level of loyalty.

4. Implications: from this follow tax levels, the resource curse, how aid gets used, how disasters get handled, when coups and revolutions happen, and why autocrats tend to last longer.

5. Evidence: the academic version tests these claims on cross-national data; the popular book demonstrates them with cases from history and current affairs: Leopold II's two faces, Rizzo in Bell, the HP boardroom, the Myanmar cyclone.

6. Prescription: to get leaders to do good, don't search for good people; enlarge the group they must please: at the national level, real electoral competition and political participation; in aid, tying help to the recipient expanding its coalition; in companies, shareholders who genuinely oversee management.

What the book establishes is one claim: the difference between good and bad politics is not moral but structural, and in a small coalition, bad behavior is almost always good politics.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: it's a how-to for aspiring tyrants. The handbook voice is satirical rhetoric, like Machiavelli's The Prince (Read 42): it writes from the ruler's chair so readers can see the rules. The authors' actual prescriptions are about constraining leaders.

Misreading 2: democratic leaders are the good guys. The authors insist democrats follow the same logic with a larger coalition; they too shrink the number they really need to please through gerrymandering, courting pivotal voter blocs and handing special favors to interest groups. The virtue of democracy is not kinder leaders but leaders who find it harder to survive by buying off a few.

Misreading 3: elections mean a large coalition. The Soviet Union had elections; many authoritarian states do. What matters is whether elections can actually remove the leader and whether the essentials really need majority support.

Criticism 1: measurement and statistics. In the academic work, coalition size is a rough index built from a few regime-database variables, not a direct count of people. In a 2008 article in the American Political Science Review, political scientists Kevin Clarke and Randall Stone argued that the original statistical setup was flawed: once democracy is properly controlled for, many effects of coalition size shrink sharply, suggesting "democracy" itself is doing the work. The authors replied, but the dispute was never fully settled.

Criticism 2: oversimplified motives. Treating every leader as a survival-maximizing calculator sidelines ideology, religious faith, nationalism, personal conviction and legitimacy. Some leaders do take risks for beliefs, and some insiders defect out of principle. The leaner the theory, the more it seems to explain, and the harder it is to falsify.

Criticism 3: optimistic prescriptions. Tying aid to coalition expansion sounds good but is hard to enforce: recipients can stage cosmetic electoral reforms, and donors usually care more about strategic interests. And the transition to a larger coalition can itself be highly unstable.

Criticism 4: the risk of cynicism. If everyone comes to believe politics is nothing but bribery, it may erode investment in public spirit and institutional trust, which are exactly what make large coalitions work.

Crossovers

  • Primatology · de Waal's Chimpanzee Politics: At Arnhem Zoo in the Netherlands, Frans de Waal saw that alpha males held their position through alliances, grooming and peacekeeping, and that the physically strongest male was often toppled by rivals who teamed up. The likeness: power rests on a coalition that must be constantly maintained. The difference: chimp coalitions are two or three animals, with no vast pool of interchangeables, no taxes and no public goods.
  • Evolutionary biology · Ewald on the evolution of virulence: Biologist Paul Ewald proposed that a pathogen's virulence depends on whether its transmission needs a healthy host. A cold spreads by people walking around sneezing, so it stays mild; malaria rides mosquitoes and doesn't need its host mobile, so it can be brutal. That is the resource curse's structure: a parasite (ruler) no longer depending on the host's (people's) activity has no reason to go easy on it. The difference: pathogens have no intent, and Ewald's hypothesis remains debated.
  • Distributed systems · Raft's majority and terms: In the Raft consensus algorithm, a leader node must win votes from a majority of the cluster and keeps its role by sending regular heartbeats; once it can't reach a majority, others start a new election. The likeness: leadership hinges on continual confirmation from a minimal winning coalition. The difference: nodes demand no private payoffs, and the algorithm's whole point is that any majority yields an equally good leader.
  • Eastern thought · Han Fei's "two handles": Han Fei (main list Read 130, Han Feizi) taught that a ruler controls his ministers through just two handles, punishment and reward, which must never be handed to ministers. That mirrors the rule about controlling revenue. The difference: Han Fei wanted a rich state and a strong army and treated the ruler's survival and the state's strength as one thing; Bueno de Mesquita argues the two often conflict.

The Book in Ten Lines

1. No one rules alone; every leader depends on a group whose defection would bring him down.

2. The first goal of politics is survival; policy is just a tool for it.

3. Three circles: those entitled to choose, those who actually choose, and those you can't lose.

4. A small circle is bought with private rewards; a large one can only be pleased with public goods.

5. Good policy comes not from good people but from structures that force leaders to serve the many.

6. The same Leopold II built railways in Belgium and severed hands in the Congo: the problem was the system, not the man.

7. The bigger the outer pool and the smaller the inner circle, the more loyal the cronies, because they know they can be replaced.

8. A ruler whose money isn't earned by his people won't care about his people: that is the resource curse.

9. Aid often serves mainly to buy policy, not to help the poor.

10. In a small coalition, bad behavior is almost always good politics; to change the behavior, enlarge the group that must be pleased.
