ReOrg

ReOrg: How to Get It Right · Stephen Heidari-Robinson & Suzanne Heywood · 2016; folds in their HBR article of the same year, "Getting Reorgs Right"

中文 →

In One Sentence

Most reorganizations fail not because the new org chart is wrong but because the goals were never pinned down, only the "lines and boxes" changed while the way work actually gets done stayed the same, and things were done in the wrong order; the authors break a reorg into five steps — work out what it has to pay back, see clearly what works and what doesn't today, compare several options, connect the processes, decision rights and people, then launch, listen and correct course — and insist that a reorg is an investment with a return to account for, not a new way of drawing the chart.

Where It Sits

Both authors led organization-design work at McKinsey; Heidari-Robinson also served as energy and environment adviser to the British prime minister, and Heywood went on to senior board roles in industry. The book is a rare, strictly step-by-step manual on the narrow subject of how to run a reorg, built on McKinsey surveys of thousands of executives who had been through one.

Core Claims

Claim one: reorgs have a poor track record, and they fail in predictable ways. According to the McKinsey research the authors cite, more than 80 percent of reorgs fail to deliver the value they were meant to in the time planned, and about 10 percent do real damage to the company; only about one in six fully hits its targets on schedule. The causes cluster tightly: unclear objectives, missing key actions (above all on processes and people), and the wrong sequence.

Claim two: the org chart is only a small part of a reorg. Leaders tend to equate reorganizing with redrawing the lines and boxes — who reports to whom, how units are carved up. But whether the new organization works depends on how work flows, who holds which decisions, who sits in the key roles, and whether employees have understood "what does this mean for me." Change the structure without changing how it runs, and you've swapped the bodywork but kept the old engine.

Claim three: a reorg can be run as a disciplined project. It isn't a gamble on a leader's instinct but five steps in order; each produces something definite, and skipping any of them costs double later.

Key Concepts, One by One

1. The real track record: why reorgs are so common and fail so often

What it is. A reorganization (reorg) is a deliberate, sizable change to an organization's structure — how units are divided, who reports to whom, how many management layers there are, what roles are responsible for. Reorgs are everywhere: a new CEO arrives, strategy shifts, a merger closes, results disappoint. Yet the numbers the authors cite are stark: more than 80 percent miss their intended value on schedule, about 10 percent leave the company worse off, and only about one in six fully succeeds. They also cite research suggesting that reorgs can cause more stress and anxiety than layoffs, with a noticeable drop in productivity in around 60 percent of cases (paraphrased).

The mechanism: three classic ways to die. The authors sort failures into three kinds (paraphrased): (1) objectives aren't clear enough — slogans like "break down silos" or "get closer to the customer" can't be tested, so no one knows whether they've been met; (2) key actions are missed — the chart is drawn and victory declared, with no rework of processes and no serious choice of people; (3) the order is wrong — the structure is announced before anyone has worked out why, people are appointed before anyone has defined what the roles must do.

An analogy. A reorg is like major surgery: the operation itself weakens the patient for a while (productivity dips, morale wobbles), so it's worth doing only when the benefit clearly outweighs the trauma — and most failed operations go wrong not in the cutting but in a fuzzy diagnosis and missing aftercare.

How it changes your view. The next time you hear "we're reorganizing," your first question is no longer what the new chart looks like but: what problem is this meant to solve, how will we know it's solved, and have the costs been counted? On base rates, a reorg fails by default; to succeed, you have to do the things the failures skipped.

2. The reorg's P&L: first work out what it has to pay back (Step 1)

What it is. The first step is to "construct the reorg's profit and loss." That means treating the reorg like an investment and writing down the value it must create as measurable targets — cost saved, revenue gained, time to market shortened, specific risks reduced — and writing the costs in too: advisers and severance, months of lower productivity, the risk of losing key people.

Why do it this way. First, it forces honesty: if the leadership can't say what this reorg buys, perhaps there shouldn't be one — many problems are solved by fixing a process, changing a person or reworking incentives, with no need to touch structure. Second, it aligns the top team before work starts: five executives carrying five different ideas of what the reorg is for is the root of most of the infighting later. Third, it gives Step 5 a yardstick for judging success and deciding when to correct course.

An example. "We will build a customer-centric organization" is a slogan. "Cut the time from a key account's request to a proposal from six weeks to two, while lowering account-coverage cost by 15 percent" is a P&L target. The first can be read any way you like; the second tells you directly which processes and roles need to change.

How it changes your view. You start seeing structure as a means, not an end: no org chart is good or bad in itself, only useful or useless against a measurable goal. In later HBR writing the authors also warn of the opposite error: many reorgs aim too low, merely reshuffling what exists instead of using the moment to chase real value.

3. Understanding today's strengths and weaknesses: don't break what still works (Step 2)

What it is. Step two is to "understand current weaknesses and strengths." Before designing anything, use data and interviews to establish where the current organization actually gets stuck and where it in fact runs well. The strengths must be inventoried too — new leaders often condemn the old organization wholesale and end up cutting away things that are inconspicuous but vital: an informal cross-unit network, a veteran who quietly holds up a whole product line.

A standard tool: spans and layers. "Span" is how many people a manager directly leads; "layers" is how many levels of management separate the top from the front line. Spans too narrow (a manager with two or three reports) and layers too many, and information decays on the way up and down, decisions slow and management costs swell; spans too wide, and managers can't keep up. Analyses like this turn "it feels bureaucratic around here" into numbers you can compare.

An analogy. A doctor doesn't operate because a patient says "I feel awful all over"; they run scans and blood tests to find which organ is failing and which are healthy. Step two is the organization's checkup.

How it changes your view. You grow wary of the urge to tear it all down: the point of a reorg is to fix what's broken, not to prove your predecessor got everything wrong. A reorg plan that can't name a single strength of the current organization probably hasn't understood it yet.

4. Choosing among several options: don't let the boss's favorite chart win by default (Step 3)

What it is. Step three is to "choose from multiple options." A common pattern, the authors observe, is that the leader already has a picture in mind — one used at a previous company, or a "best practice" from some consulting report — and the reorg merely implements it. Their demand: put several genuinely different structures on the table (by product, by region, by customer segment, by function, or some matrix), then score each against design criteria drawn from the first two steps.

The mechanism: design criteria. The criteria come from the Step 1 targets and the Step 2 diagnosis — say, "major customers must deal with a single point of contact," "R&D's economies of scale must not be weakened," "no more than five management layers." Every option trades some goals against others: organizing by product sharpens product focus but may send three teams to call on the same customer; organizing by customer does the reverse. There is no perfect structure, only the one whose costs fall least on what matters most right now.

Why it matters. With only one option, the discussion becomes "is this a good plan," easily pushed through by authority or inertia; with several side by side, it becomes "how much B are we willing to give up for A," and the trade-offs finally get put on the table.

How it changes your view. It turns org design from a contest of taste into a contest of trade-offs: every structure makes some things easier and others harder; what matters is knowing what you're paying for what.

5. Plumbing and wiring: what actually makes an organization run (Step 4)

What it is. The authors call step four "get the plumbing and wiring right." It's a renovation metaphor: lines and boxes are a house's floor plan — here the living room, there the bedroom — but whether the house is livable depends on whether the pipes and wires inside the walls are connected. For an organization, the plumbing and wiring are processes (how work passes from one person's hands to the next), decision rights (who decides, who must be consulted, who just needs to be told), role responsibilities, and the specific people in each key seat.

Why it's the hardest step and the one most often skipped. A few executives can draw an org chart in a meeting room; connecting the plumbing means going into every team and redefining processes and roles one by one — heavy, fiddly, unglamorous work. So many reorgs declare victory once the structure is announced. The result: people have moved seats but still work the old way, two logics collide, and everyone concludes that "nothing changed, it just got messier."

Choosing people happens here too. The authors favor filling key roles top-down, layer by layer: settle the top tier first, then have those leaders help choose the next. The longer appointments drag on, the longer people are left hanging — and the more likely the most sought-after talent leaves first.

How it changes your view. You realize the org chart is the least important diagram: an organization's real shape is written in its processes and decision rights, not its reporting lines. Whether a reorg is "done" depends on whether the way work is done has really changed.

6. The human half: "What does this mean for me?"

What it is. A thread runs through the whole book: for leaders a reorg is a strategic question; for employees it is first a personal one — is my job safe, who's my boss, will I still do what I do now? Until that question is answered, employees can't hear a word about the exciting new vision. This is also the main source of the productivity loss: people guess, ask around, wait and see, and spend more time on their résumés than on their work.

Two communication blunders. In HBR the authors named two common mistakes: "wait and see" — saying nothing until the plan is final, so rumor fills the vacuum — and "ivory-tower idealism" — talking only about the grand vision and never about what happens to each person. The right approach is to say early and often what has been decided and what hasn't, and to answer "what does this mean for me" as fast as possible.

How much participation. The authors don't want everyone designing the reorg, either: too few involved, and the plan loses touch with front-line reality and nobody buys in; too many, and the process drags, confidentiality leaks, and everyone lobbies for their own turf. A small team does the core design, but diagnosis and detailed design should draw heavily on what the front line knows.

How it changes your view. You come to see uncertainty itself as a major cost of a reorg: the longer it runs and the more people are left in limbo, the more the organization pays. Speed and clarity are themselves a way of looking after people.

7. Launch, learn, course-correct: a reorg is a process, not a release (Step 5)

What it is. Step five is to "launch, learn, and course-correct." The day the new organization goes live is not the finish line but the start of a trial run: track progress against the Step 1 P&L, keep gathering feedback from the front line, and when a process breaks or decision rights clash, fix it promptly. No design comes out right on paper the first time; what matters is having a way to come back and repair it.

Why it matters. Many leaders announce the new structure and move their attention elsewhere, leaving a half-finished product for those below to figure out. The opposite failure is just as common: at the first resistance, tear it up and start again, until the organization staggers from one reorg to the next and employees learn that "it'll all change again in two years anyway" and commit to nothing. Course correction means targeted repair, not another reorg.

How it changes your view. You come to treat an organization as a system that needs ongoing tuning rather than a blueprint fixed once drawn: a good reorg ends with a feedback loop built in.

The Skeleton of the Argument

What the book establishes is simple: reorgs fail so often not because organizations are too complex for anyone to get right, but because most reorgs systematically skip the same few things. Its evidence comes from surveys of large numbers of executives who'd been through reorgs: failures cluster in unclear goals, neglected processes and people, and the wrong order. So the remedy is targeted: the Step 1 P&L blocks vague goals; the diagnosis and comparison of Steps 2 and 3 block drawing charts on instinct; the plumbing and wiring of Step 4 block changing only lines and boxes; the feedback loop of Step 5 blocks "announced means done"; and communication and people choices throughout block the loss of hearts and minds. In a sentence: treat a reorg as an investment that must earn its return, be built in sequence, and be inspected and repaired — not as a new org chart.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: "Follow the five steps and the reorg will succeed." The method lowers the odds of botching the job yourself; it doesn't guarantee the strategy is right. If the Step 1 judgment of value is wrong — say the market doesn't actually need that capability — flawless execution just does the wrong thing well.

Misreading two: "A reorg means layoffs or cost cutting." In the book, a reorg's goal can be growth, speed, risk control or new capability; cost is only one possibility. Equating reorgs with layoffs only feeds employee panic.

Misreading three: "Structure doesn't matter; only people and culture do." The emphasis on plumbing and wiring doesn't make structure irrelevant; structure determines which collaborations happen by default and which take extra effort. What the authors oppose is changing only the structure.

Criticism one: the source and testability of the data. The key figures come mainly from McKinsey executive surveys rather than independent tracking of company performance; whether a reorg "delivered value" is self-reported, open to memory bias and survivor bias. Numbers like 80 percent or one in six are best read as directional warnings, not precise failure rates.

Criticism two: a top-down, consultant's-eye view. This is a book for the executives and advisers who launch reorgs: employees appear mainly as people to be communicated with and retained, not as participants with interests and judgment of their own. For those in the middle, being reorganized, it offers limited direct help.

Criticism three: a linear process against messy reality. The five steps form a neat sequence, but real reorgs often happen under pressure — a merger deadline, a crisis, a new CEO's first hundred days — with incomplete information and politics in play, leaving little time for careful diagnosis and comparison. The book also says relatively little about the reorg as a contest for power: whose turf shrinks, who uses the moment to expand, dynamics that often decide the outcome more than process design does.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · Conway's law: Melvin Conway observed in 1968 that organizations which design systems end up producing systems that mirror their own communication structures; software practice later developed the "inverse Conway maneuver," redrawing team boundaries first and expecting the architecture to follow. Alike: both hold that structure shapes output. Unlike: Conway looks at communication paths, while this book warns that changing reporting lines isn't enough — without new processes and decision rights, communication won't change either.
  • Psychology · Uncertainty hurts more than bad news: In a 2016 Nature Communications study, de Berker and colleagues found that people showed stronger stress responses when they couldn't tell whether a shock was coming than when they knew it was. That is the neural case for answering "what does this mean for me" as fast as possible: people can bear bad news; what they can't bear is limbo. Unlike: the experiment's uncertainty was a quantifiable probability, while a reorg's is tangled up with trust and fairness.
  • Distributed systems · Online schema migration: To restructure a large table that is serving live traffic, engineers often use "expand–contract": run old and new structures side by side, switch over gradually, keep a rollback ready. Alike: an organization too must be rebuilt while it runs, and Step 5's course correction is the rollback-and-repair mechanism. Unlike: data can be copied exactly, but relationships and tacit understanding can't be dual-written.
  • Evolution · Molting: To grow, an arthropod must shed its old exoskeleton, and until the new shell hardens it is at its most vulnerable. Alike: during a reorg an organization is also in its soft-shell phase — productivity dips, talent is easily lost — so the shorter the better. Unlike: molting is triggered by hormones on a fixed program, while a reorg is a choice that must be justified — no shedding the shell for reasons other than growth.
