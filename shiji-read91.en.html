Records of the Grand Historian

Shiji · Sima Qian · completed c. 91 BCE

中文 →

In One Sentence

A man who had been castrated as a punishment — who by the standards of his own age no longer counted as fully human — bought his existence back with a three-thousand-year history. He invented a way of writing the past whose basic unit is the person rather than the year, gave emperors, assassins, merchants, jesters, hard-line magistrates and outlaw knights seats in the same book, and asked in it the sharpest question Chinese historiography has ever asked: if the good starve and the cruel die in bed, does "the way of Heaven" mean anything at all?

Where It Sits

Sima Qian (c. 145 – c. 86 BCE) was Grand Astrologer at the Han court, inheriting both the post and an unfinished history from his father Sima Tan. In 99 BCE he spoke up for Li Ling, a general who had surrendered to the Xiongnu; Emperor Wu took it as an insult, and Sima Qian was imprisoned and castrated — the most degrading penalty in the code, one that placed a man permanently outside respectable society. He could have taken the honourable exit of suicide. He chose to stay alive and finish the book. The result runs to 130 chapters and some 526,000 characters, from the legendary Yellow Emperor down to his own emperor's reign. Calling it "China's first comprehensive history in annals-biography form" undersells it twice over: it is the template that all twenty-four later dynastic histories copied, and it is the fountainhead of Chinese narrative literature. Lu Xun's famous verdict — "the historian's swan song, an unrhymed Li Sao" (the Li Sao being the great lament of the exiled poet Qu Yuan) — says exactly this: the book occupies the summit of two disciplines at once.

The Central Claims

Sima Qian stated his own programme in twelve characters, in a letter to his friend Ren An: to examine the boundary between Heaven and man, to trace the pattern of change from antiquity to the present, and to make of it the argument of one school. Unpacked:

  • Heaven and man: is history decreed or made? What he offers is not an answer but an unconcealed doubt. In the biography of Bo Yi he asks outright: if Heaven really "sides always with the good," why did the two most upright men of their age starve to death? That question dragged Chinese history-writing from vindicating the cosmic order to interrogating it.
  • Change has a traceable mechanism. History is not a stack of dates but the long evolution of institutions, tempers and interests — from feudal fiefs to centralised commanderies, from the frugal early Han to Emperor Wu's endless wars and fiscal extraction. He wants the line that explains why it came to this.
  • A history is an authored work, not an archive. "The argument of one school" was a phrase reserved for philosophers: it claimed that this book had a position of its own, fit to stand beside the Confucians and the Daoists. It is the declaration of independence of Chinese historiography: the historian is not a copyist but a thinker.

The Core Concepts, One by One

The annals-biography form: changing the unit of history from the year to the person

Before the Shiji, Chinese histories were annalistic — the Spring and Autumn Annals and the Zuo Commentary march year by year. Annals keep the chronology clean but shred every life into fragments scattered across decades; you can never assemble the answer to "who was this person?" Sima Qian rotated the axis: people are the warp, events the weft.

He split 130 chapters into five kinds, each with a job:

FormChaptersWhat it carries
Basic Annals12The spine: year by year, whoever actually commanded the empire
Tables10Chronological grids that flatten parallel timelines into something searchable
Treatises8Rites, music, pitch-pipes, calendar, astronomy, state sacrifices, waterworks, finance — thematic cross-sections
Hereditary Houses30Lineages that persist across generations
Arrayed Traditions70Individual lives, plus "collective biographies" grouping a whole type of person

Four lenses on one past: the spine, the grid, the institutional cross-section, the close-up.

What makes this structure remarkable is that it satisfies two opposed demands at once — narrative and retrieval. The Tables work like a database index, flattening the interlocking chaos of the Warring States into a grid you can look things up in. The Treatises run horizontally, tracking irrigation or currency across dynasties. The Annals give you the spine; the Traditions give you people. You get a full human fate without losing the whole. For two millennia after, from the Book of Han to the History of Ming, every official dynastic history used this frame — one book set the format in which a civilisation would remember itself.

Mutual illumination: a person's truth, deliberately scattered

The annals-biography form has a built-in defect: the same event recurs in several people's chapters. Sima Qian turned the defect into an instrument. The different faces of one man are placed on purpose in different chapters, and only reading across them gives you the whole. Later scholars named the technique hujian, "mutual illumination" — a term of their coining, not his.

The showcase is Xiang Yu and Liu Bang, the two men who fought over the empire after the Qin collapsed. In Xiang Yu's own Annals he is nearly a god: he smashes the Qin army after burning his boats and breaking his cooking pots, he is surrounded at Gaixia to the sound of his enemies singing his homeland's songs, he refuses at the last river to cross back to safety. Sima Qian writes his heroism to the hilt, and a reader nearly forgets he lost. Xiang Yu's other face — the mass burial of two hundred thousand surrendered Qin troops, the sacked cities, the envoy boiled alive — you meet in other men's chapters. Liu Bang runs the other way: statesman and founder in his own Annals; elsewhere, the man who shoved his own children off the cart to lighten it while fleeing, and who answered Xiang Yu's threat to boil his captive father with "then send me a cup of the soup."

This is a way of not lying that also leaves room to live. In an age when writing the founding emperor's squalor plainly could cost your head, distributing the truth is the only method that preserves both the facts and the author. But the point is not merely evasion. It embeds a view of history: each face a man shows in each relationship is real, and there is no single chapter that can say him once and for all. You have to assemble the fragments yourself, and the assembling is the judgement. It is also why the Shiji survives rereading: any single chapter is literature; only the cross-reading is history.

"Examining the boundary between Heaven and man": the most famous doubt in Chinese historiography

The boundary between Heaven and man means the seam between cosmic order and human agency — the central problem of Han thought. The dominant view, associated with the Confucian scholar Dong Zhongshu, held that Heaven has intentions, signals approval and displeasure through omens, and rewards virtue. History, on this account, is moral: the good flourish, the wicked fall. Sima Qian laid that doctrine alongside three thousand years of evidence he had gathered himself, and it broke.

In the biography of Bo Yi he writes (condensed): "The way of Heaven has no favourites; it sides always with the good. Were Bo Yi and Shu Qi not good men? They heaped up virtue, kept themselves clean — and starved." Bo Yi and Shu Qi were brothers who refused to eat the grain of a dynasty they judged illegitimate and died of hunger on a mountain; they were the moral exemplars of their culture. Against them he sets the bandit Zhi, who butchered his way through a long life and died peacefully of old age. Then the line: "I am deeply perplexed. This thing called the way of Heaven — is it there, or is it not?"

The weight of that sentence comes from who wrote it. Here is a man who had personally verified that virtue is not repaid: he told the truth once and paid for it with lifelong humiliation. He did not turn his injury into a complaint. He distilled it into a general question about the order of the world, and then honestly reported that he could not answer it. That is what "the argument of one school" is worth: he will neither make excuses for Heaven nor stop asking. For a reader today the passage offers an adult posture toward injustice — don't deny it, don't sedate yourself with "everything happens for a reason," and don't pretend the question is closed.

Writing out of indignation: converting suffering into work

The letter to Ren An is the key to the whole book. Ren An, facing execution, had written asking Sima Qian to intercede; Sima Qian replied explaining why he could not, and why he had not killed himself when disgraced. The letter contains the sentence every Chinese schoolchild still learns: "Death comes to all men; but one death may weigh as much as Mount Tai, another as little as a goose feather." He stayed alive, he says, because to die obscurely with the work unwritten would be the featherweight death.

Then he lists his company. King Wen, imprisoned, expounded the Book of Changes; Confucius, driven into hardship, composed the Spring and Autumn Annals; Qu Yuan, exiled, wrote the Li Sao; Zuo Qiuming, blind, produced the Discourses of the States; Sun Bin, his kneecaps cut away, set down his military treatise; Lü Buwei, banished to Shu, left his compendium; Han Fei, a prisoner in Qin, wrote his essays on persuasion and indignation. "The three hundred poems of the Book of Odes were for the most part written by worthies venting what was pent up in them." This is the doctrine later called writing out of indignation: great work does not grow in favourable weather; it is the road a person cuts for themselves after reality has bricked up every other exit.

This is not a beautification of suffering. The mechanism is concrete. The ordinary channel for making a life count — office, achievement, posterity — was permanently closed to him after the punishment. He needed some other unit in which a life could be measured as not-wasted, and the work itself became the proof that he existed. He says he will "hide it in a famous mountain and pass it to the one who will understand it": even if no contemporary can read it, even if reading it is dangerous, keep it alive until such a reader appears. Chinese literati invoked this logic for two thousand years afterwards. A failed man writing a book is the exit a civilisation reserved for its losers to keep their dignity.

Breaking his own categories: who gets into the Annals, who gets a House

The classification scheme of the Shiji appears to follow rank and in practice keeps breaking it — and every breach is a verdict.

  • Xiang Yu gets Basic Annals. He was never emperor and he lost. But for those years after the Qin fell, he was the man who actually issued orders to the realm. Sima Qian's criterion is plainly effective power, not title.
  • Empress Lü gets Basic Annals. During her son Emperor Hui's nominal reign, the ruler was his mother. So Sima Qian gave her the Annals and not him — a woman entered the imperial sequence as a de facto sovereign.
  • Chen She gets a Hereditary House. Chen She was a conscripted labourer whose rebellion collapsed within months and who left no line to inherit anything. But he lit the fire that burned the Qin. Placing him among hereditary aristocrats amounts to saying: it was this farm boy who turned the table over first.
  • Confucius gets a Hereditary House. A commoner all his life, with no claim whatsoever to the category. Sima Qian's reason is that his influence descended through generations — treating a transmission of ideas as equivalent to the continuity of a fief.

Read the four together and his real ruler is visible: how much a person moved the causal chain of history, not where they stood in the ritual register. That was an aggressive position to take. Confucius's Spring and Autumn Annals had made its judgements through the "rectification of names" — the right title implies the right praise or blame. Sima Qian appears to inherit the method while quietly rewriting its content with the act of classification itself. It is also the thing that kept making later readers uneasy: who authorised you to re-seat the world? His answer was on the record — he was making the argument of one school.

"The Grand Historian says": the invention of the historian's first person

Nearly every chapter closes (sometimes opens) with a passage marked The Grand Historian says. It looks like a formatting habit; it is in fact a durable technology. It separates the facts from the historian's judgement into two visible layers. The body narrates; the verdict is concentrated in one signed paragraph. The reader always knows where the events stop and Sima Qian starts.

What is in those paragraphs is often startlingly frank. On Xiang Yu he concedes in the same breath that the man destroyed the Qin in three years, something without precedent in recent antiquity — and then refuses him any mercy: to the end Xiang Yu kept saying "Heaven is destroying me, it is no fault of my generalship," and Sima Qian's response is flat — "Was he not deluded!" You are still wet-eyed from the death scene when the author taps you on the shoulder to say that this man never once understood what he had done wrong. On the general Li Guang, undecorated and ill-used all his life, he passes no verdict at all; he simply quotes a proverb: "The peach and the plum do not speak, yet a path is worn beneath them" — real substance needs no advertising; people find their way to it. The form was adopted wholesale by every later official history and by Sima Guang's great chronicle, becoming the standard closing move of Chinese historical writing.

The merchants and the outlaw knights: everyone who is not a king or a minister

The most subversive stretch of the Shiji is its collective biographies, which group a type of person into a single chapter: assassins, outlaw knights, merchants, jesters, diviners, cruel magistrates, conscientious officials. It is the first time — and for a long time the last — that a Chinese history turned its lens from the court to the society.

The chapter on merchants and money-making is the astonishing one. In it Sima Qian records the earliest systematic economic observation in the tradition: "The whole world comes bustling after profit; the whole world goes streaming away after profit." He quotes the older statesman Guanzi — "when the granaries are full, people learn ritual and restraint; when clothing and food suffice, they learn honour and shame" — morality has material preconditions; people eat before they are refined. The sharpest passage is his ranking of economic policy: best is to go along with people's pursuit of gain and let it run; next, to channel it; next, to instruct; next, to regulate; and worst of all is for the state to go into business and compete with its own people. He wrote that under an emperor who had just nationalised salt and iron and was reaching into private wealth by every available lever. Read it beside his Treatise on state finance, which documents that extraction step by step, and the criticism stops being deniable.

The chapter on outlaw knights defends a justice that operates outside the state. He defines these men by conduct: their word is good, their action follows through, a promise made is a promise kept, and they will spend their lives going to the rescue of someone in trouble. To officialdom they were organised crime. Sima Qian takes the question seriously: when the state's justice fails, is this private code of obligation and trust not also an order? He draws no conclusion, but raising the question was offence enough. The historian Ban Gu later attacked him for exactly these two chapters — for "demoting the retiring scholar and promoting the swaggering adventurer" and for "exalting wealth and power while treating poverty as shameful." Seen from here, that is precisely what is most modern in the book: it concedes that a society runs several value systems at once, and the court's is only one of them.

The craft: two sentences, two men

Shiji still reads well two thousand years on because Sima Qian mastered an extremely economical form of characterisation: pass no comment; just find the one line that exposes what a person is made of.

The First Emperor of Qin passes on procession, and both Xiang Yu and Liu Bang are somewhere in the crowd. Xiang Yu says: "That man — I could take his place." Liu Bang says: "So that is what a man should amount to." One wants to seize; the other wants to become. One collides head-on; the other envies first and schemes after. Everything the two of them later do already has its source in those two sentences.

Then Gaixia. Surrounded, hearing his own homeland's songs rising from the enemy camp and understanding what that means, Xiang Yu drinks and sings: "My strength could uproot mountains, my spirit overshadowed the age — but the times are against me and my horse will not run. My horse will not run; what then can I do? Yu, my Yu, what is to become of you?" A man who could uproot mountains is left with a single remaining problem: what happens to the woman he loves. For the assassin Jing Ke, setting out on a mission he will not survive, one line does all the work: "The wind cries, the river runs cold; once gone, the brave man does not return." Compressing an enormous fate into one private moment became a basic skill of Chinese narrative after him. But keep one thing in view: these scenes and speeches were reconstructed from hearsay, old documents and family memory. They are the shape of what happened, not a recording of it.

The Argument, Distilled

Reduced to a single line, the book proves one thing: history can be understood by human beings, and need not be handed over to Heaven.

Step one, he built the instrument. Three thousand years will not fit in a book without structure, so: Annals for the spine, Tables for coordinates, Treatises for institutional cross-sections, Houses for continuity, Traditions for individuals. The instrument is itself a thesis — the basic unit of history is a human choice, not an omen or a date.

Step two, he laid out the evidence and deliberately declined to filter it. Emperors came in; so did assassins, merchants, jesters and torturers. Winners came in, but Xiang Yu, Chen She and Li Guang — all losers — are written more movingly than the victors. His categories keep breaking because he is holding only one ruler: who actually moved things.

Step three, with the evidence assembled, he checked it against the going explanation — that Heaven rewards the good — and it did not fit. Bo Yi starved; the bandit died in bed; the best general of the age was never ennobled; he himself had been mutilated for telling the truth. So he did the bravest thing in the book: he declined to paper over the contradiction, wrote it down, and admitted he had no answer.

Step four, unable to locate causation in Heaven, he relocated it entirely into human affairs: the succession of institutions, the loosening and tightening of state finance, the drift of customs and morale, the consequences of individual character and choice — Xiang Yu blaming Heaven to his last breath. The cosmic end of the question he leaves hanging and unresolved; the human end he writes out in full detail. That is what "examining the boundary between Heaven and man" looks like in practice.

Finally he signs it, calls it the argument of one school, hides it in a famous mountain, and waits for a reader. So the conclusion has two layers: history is human causation, not a divine ledger — and the writing of history is itself a way for a man destroyed by his circumstances to recover his weight.

Misreadings & Serious Criticisms

  • Misreading one: treating the Shiji as verbatim record. Ban Gu relayed an earlier verdict that it is "a true record" because "its prose is straight, its facts checked, it neither invents praise nor conceals evil." That describes an attitude, not a guarantee that every speech has a source. The banquet at Hongmen with its sword dance and its bursting-in bodyguard, and the whole tale of the orphan of Zhao, are reconstructions; the orphan story in particular contradicts the earlier Zuo Commentary and has been disputed for centuries. The workable reading: trust the structure and the arc; treat scenes and dialogue as evidence-based literary restoration.
  • Reliability is layered, not uniform. The opening chapter on the Five Emperors is an editing of legend and cannot be taken as history; the further down you read, the harder the ground. One striking corroboration: after oracle bones were excavated, the scholar Wang Guowei matched the Shang royal genealogy in the inscriptions against the Shiji's list of Shang kings and found a close fit — the old sources Sima Qian worked from were far more reliable than early twentieth-century sceptics assumed. That vindicates the Shang genealogy specifically; it does not retroactively make the Five Emperors historical.
  • The text is damaged and partly not his. The Book of Han records that ten chapters existed only as titles; later hands, notably Chu Shaosun in the late Western Han, supplied replacements. Passages in today's text marked "Master Chu says" are his. The Shiji you read is not wholly Sima Qian's.
  • Ban Gu's attack deserves a hearing. He charged that Sima Qian's judgements "deviate considerably from the sages": he put the Daoists ahead of the Confucian classics when discussing first principles, promoted adventurers over retiring scholars, and admired wealth while disdaining poverty. Factually the charge is largely correct; only the valuation is reversed. What Ban Gu saw as deviation is what we now read as pluralism. It is also a useful reminder that the book is not neutral — it has a strong personal slant.
  • The "slander" charge. In the Eastern Han, the minister Wang Yun called the Shiji a "book of slander" against Emperor Wu. The label is political abuse, but the phenomenon it points at is real: the treatises on state finance and on the imperial sacrifices, and the chapter on cruel magistrates, present the reign's fiscal extraction, its credulity about immortality cults, and its legal brutality in dense array. Note how it was done: a contemporary historian writing under the eyes of the man in power, and doing it by declining to pronounce and simply arranging the facts.
  • The form has costs. Slicing history into people is strong on fate and weak on structure: events repeat across chapters and the reader has to do the stitching, while long-run variables that attach to no individual — population, climate, prices — have nowhere to live (the Treatises only partly compensate). This is the opposite of what modern structural historians, the Annales school above all, went looking for: precisely a history whose unit is not the great man. The Shiji's greatest strength and its worst limitation are the same thing: it believes in people.
  • Don't romanticise "writing out of indignation." It does not claim suffering is good for you, still less that you should go seek it. Sima Qian never made peace with his humiliation — he says his bowels twist nine times a day at the memory. The claim is narrower: once the damage is done and the exits are shut, a person can still choose to convert it into something that lasts. It is a survivor's technique, not a justification of the wound.

Ten Sentences

1. Examine the boundary between Heaven and man; trace the pattern of change from antiquity to now; make of it the argument of one school. Twelve characters, and that is the entire programme of the book.

2. He changed the unit of history from the year to the person — Annals, Tables, Treatises, Houses, Traditions — so that you get a complete human fate without losing the whole. For two thousand years afterwards, China wrote its official history to this format twenty-four more times.

3. A person's truth is distributed: Xiang Yu's heroism fills his own chapter while his atrocities sit in other men's; Liu Bang's statecraft is in his Annals and his squalor elsewhere. Never lie, but scatter the truth — the craft of telling it in a dangerous age.

4. "Were Bo Yi and Shu Qi not good men? They heaped up virtue and starved… I am deeply perplexed: this thing called the way of Heaven — is it there, or is it not?" The bravest moment in Chinese historiography is a historian admitting he cannot answer.

5. "Death comes to all men; one death may weigh as much as Mount Tai, another as little as a goose feather." He refused suicide because dying obscurely with the work unwritten was the featherweight death.

6. King Wen imprisoned expounded the Changes; Confucius in hardship made the Annals; Qu Yuan exiled wrote the Li Sao — work is often the road a person cuts after every other exit has been bricked up. Not a hymn to suffering; a survivor's technique.

7. Xiang Yu never reigned yet got Annals; Empress Lü was a woman and got Annals; Chen She was a conscript and got a House; Confucius was a commoner and got one too. He held one ruler only: who actually moved things — never who ranked where.

8. "The whole world comes bustling after profit, and goes streaming away after profit." Best policy is to let that run, then channel, then instruct, then regulate — and the worst government of all is the one that goes into business against its own people.

9. "I could take his place" (Xiang Yu) against "So that is what a man should amount to" (Liu Bang). The best characterisation is not a verdict; it is finding the line that gives a person away, and then shutting up.

10. Having written Xiang Yu's death as high tragedy, he turns and punctures it: the man died still saying "Heaven is destroying me, it is no fault of my generalship" — "Was he not deluded!" Pitying someone and indulging their self-deception are two different things.
