The Myth of Sisyphus

Le Mythe de Sisyphe · Albert Camus · 1942

中文 →

In one sentence

You get up, catch the tram, work four hours, eat, work four hours, eat, sleep, Monday Tuesday Wednesday and on — until one day the word why surfaces on its own. What you notice next, Camus says, is that you have spent your whole life demanding an explanation from the world, and the world has never once answered; that gap between the demand and the silence is what he calls the absurd. And the only honest way to live with it is neither suicide (which deletes the one asking) nor faith (which fills the silence with an invented answer), but holding the gap open with your eyes wide open — and it turns out that in exactly that posture a person recovers freedom, intensity, and even happiness.

Where it sits

Camus (1913–1960) was born poor in French Algeria: his father was killed at the Marne when he was one, his mother cleaned houses and was illiterate and half deaf, he grew up in a working-class district of Algiers, and tuberculosis at seventeen ended both his goalkeeping and his path into academic philosophy. The Myth of Sisyphus appeared in 1942 in occupied Paris alongside the novel The Stranger and the play Caligula — what he called his cycle of the absurd, one idea in three forms. (The first edition even had to drop the chapter on Kafka, who was Jewish, to get past German censorship; it was restored after the war.) It is not a system: in the preface Camus says he is offering only the description, in a pure state, of a sickness of the spirit (paraphrased). He spent his life refusing the label "existentialist" — and precisely because the book is sharp, readable, and unpompous, it became the most widely read philosophical essay of the twentieth century.

The core claims

  • The absurd is not a property of the world, nor a defect in you, but the fault line where the two meet: a human being who insists on meaning, unity, an explanation — and a world that keeps silent. Remove either term and the absurd vanishes.
  • Which means every "solution" to the absurd is a cheat. Suicide deletes the questioner; the religious or metaphysical leap deletes the silence. Both are escapes: one flees by dying, the other by hoping.
  • The one honest posture is not to solve it but to refuse to look away — lucidity, revolt, scorn, holding the gap open. And that posture is its own payment: "One must imagine Sisyphus happy."

The core concepts, one at a time

The absurd: it is not in you and it is not in the world

In everyday speech "absurd" is an adjective — what a ridiculous situation. Camus turns it into a relation: it is a between, not a thing. His defining sentence: the absurd is born of the confrontation between the human need and the unreasonable silence of the world. Note both halves. The "need" is what a human being cannot stop wanting — meaning, unity, one thread that ties everything together. The "silence" is simply what the world is: it does not answer, not out of malice, but because it is not the kind of thing that answers.

An analogy: a key that will not fit a lock. The absurdity is not in the key and not in the lock; it lives only in the will not fit. File down the key (suicide), or pretend the lock has opened (faith), and the absurd disappears — but that is not solving the problem, it is tearing up the question. From this Camus draws the strictest rule in the book: the absurd requires three things at once — the world, the human demand, and the lucidity that sees they do not match. Drop any one of them and you are out of the game.

Schematic: the absurd is in neither box but in the mismatch between them — delete either side and it is gone, but so is the question.

The absurd walls: when it comes for you

Camus does not open with an argument; he opens with a feeling. The absurd is bumped into first and understood afterwards. He lists the walls you walk into.

Weariness. "Rising, tram, four hours of work, meal, tram, four hours of work, meal, sleep, and Monday Tuesday Wednesday Thursday Friday and Saturday in the same rhythm" — and then one day the why arises. His observation here is exact: weariness comes at the end of a mechanical life, but it is also what starts consciousness moving. It is both an ending and a beginning: from there you either fall back into numbness or begin to wake up.

The strangeness of the world. For a moment a stone, a landscape, refuses to recognise you; what Camus calls the primitive hostility of the world rises back up through the millennia of scenery we have painted over it. We normally project our own feeling onto nature — beauty, nostalgia. Strip the projection and the world shows the face that has nothing to do with us.

The inhumanity of other people. His image is still lethal: a man is talking on the telephone behind a glass partition; you cannot hear him, you only see his meaningless dumb show — and you find yourself wondering why he is alive. Cut the current of familiarity and any person instantly looks mechanical and unjustified.

Time and death. We live on tomorrow — the promotion, the children grown, retirement — yet what we are longing for is the very thing that eats us: he belongs to time, and by that horror he recognises his worst enemy (paraphrased). And death can never be experienced: of another's death you see only the event, and at your own you are not present. So everything said about "afterwards" is not knowledge.

Two kinds of suicide: the physical and the philosophical

The book's first line is famous: "There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide." Why? Because you can be wrong about how many dimensions the world has and still have lunch, whereas whether life is worth living is the one question you must answer with an act — you did not kill yourself today, so you have already answered. What Camus wants to know is whether a person who has seen the absurd can go on living without lying to himself.

He maps two escape routes. The first is physical suicide. It looks like the most honest response — no meaning, so end it. Camus rules it out: suicide is consent, an abolition of the absurd at one of its terms. The absurd needs someone lucid to carry it, and the dead carry nothing; living, therefore, is the only way of keeping the absurd alive.

The second is "philosophical suicide" — the leap. This is the best and most-skipped chapter, in which Camus goes after thinkers who saw the absurd before he did. Kierkegaard, for whom faith demands a suspension of reason and a jump into God. Shestov, the Russian thinker for whom the very place reason breaks down is where God appears, so the absurd itself becomes a door. Jaspers, the German existential philosopher for whom the "limit situations" that defeat thought put us in touch with an unsayable transcendence. Husserl, founder of phenomenology, whom Camus reads as sliding from "back to the things themselves" into timeless essences — reinstalling reason in the world by the back entrance. His charge against all of them is not "you are wrong" but "you cheated": you set out from the absurd and, halfway across, either deified it or quietly abolished it. Any move that carries you from the world gives no answer to therefore the answer is elsewhere — God, Spirit, the laws of history — is the same manoeuvre: hope traded for lucidity.

The rule he sets himself is disarmingly plain: I want to know whether I can live with what I know, and with that alone.

Revolt: not a solution, just keeping your eyes open

"Revolt" here is easily misheard as barricades. In this book it is metaphysical: going on asking though you know no answer is coming, going on wanting to live though you know you will not. Camus's formulation: the absurd is a constant confrontation between a man and his own obscurity, a demand, an insistence on an impossible transparency (paraphrased).

His analogy is a condemned man. Someone who knows he is to be executed has three options: hope for a pardon (the leap), hang himself in the cell tonight (suicide), or look the executioner in the face and not turn away. The third changes nothing whatever, and it is the only one that has not bowed to the sentence. That refusal to avert the eyes, Camus says, is the whole of human dignity — and it is what gives life its value.

Why is this not self-deception, a consolation prize dressed up as heroism? Because it assumes no reward. Revolt buys no afterlife, no meaning, not even success. It guarantees exactly one thing: you have not lied to yourself. From which comes a counter-intuitive result: abolish hope and you stop being hope's hostage. That is also the line between the absurd and despair — the despairing person still privately holds that there ought to have been meaning and there isn't; the revolted person has dropped the "ought" as well, and is left with what there is.

Absurd freedom: buying today back from tomorrow

Camus is explicit that he is not touching the two-thousand-year quarrel about free will — whether the will is metaphysically free is a question that only has bite inside a theological frame (how does freedom square with omnipotence and omniscience). With God out of the picture, the question lapses. He is after a different freedom: the freedom a human heart can actually experience inside one finite life.

The mechanism is elegant: it is tomorrow that enslaves us. The ordinary person believes himself free while spending every day in service of an aim — the promotion three years out, the house after retirement, the life that starts "once things settle down." That aim is a rope, and every hour of today has been mortgaged against it. The moment you take seriously that death cuts every long plan in half, the rope snaps. Not into "we all die, so why bother," but into: you stop living for an imagined future, and today belongs to you for the first time.

His summary image is sharp: the everyday man is a slave of tomorrow; the absurd man has simply stopped waiting. Absurdity, revolt and freedom are three faces of one movement: because you see there is no answer (absurd), you stop fleeing (revolt), and so you stop mortgaging the present to an imagined future (freedom).

The ethic of quantity, and three absurd men

Here comes the most attacked and most quoted-out-of-context step in the book. With no God and no eternal scoreboard, what standard is left? Camus answers: not living best but living most — not a ranking by quality (that would need an absolute standard to rank by) but quantity: more experience, more lucid hours, more intensity in the present.

He immediately plugs the obvious hole. "Everything is permitted" — Dostoevsky's proposition — is in Camus's hands not a shout of liberation but a bitter acknowledgement of a fact (paraphrased). It does not mean nothing is forbidden; it means no God will be auditing the books at the end. You remain answerable for what you do — only to other people rather than to heaven.

Then three portraits of "the absurd man," which Camus expressly says are not moral models but three ways of living all the way out without counting on eternity. Don Juan is not a rake; his secret is that he does not believe a single true love is waiting for him, so he gives each affair everything and ends each without lying. He is accused of shallowness, but depth was never what he was after — quantity and clarity were. The actor has made a profession of the absurd: he lives an entire life in three hours, takes his bow, and keeps nothing; his glory is earned by the body in the moment and handed straight back — he is a professional of transience. The conqueror chooses action over contemplation and history over eternity, knowing every victory will be erased, and still insists on getting things done on the human side of the line. What the three share is one thing: none of them buys anything on credit from "later."

Absurd creation: describe without explaining, build to be erased

Camus puts art at the summit, not out of aestheticism but for a logical reason: creation is the one act that can perform the absurd faithfully. A genuinely absurd work must describe and not explain — it refuses to supply the world with the reason the world lacks. He is scathing about the novel that sets out to prove a thesis: it commits philosophical suicide on paper. A good absurd work puts the phenomena down, lets the absurd happen in the reader, and draws no conclusion (his own Stranger is written exactly this way: Meursault never explains himself).

He adds a harsher condition: the creator has to accept that the work will be wiped out. "Ephemeral creation" — sculpting in wet sand with the tide coming. Why do it, then? Because creating is living twice: once through the days, and once again in giving them shape. That repetition is itself the payment; it does not need immortality to be redeemed.

In the same chapter he reads Dostoevsky's Kirilov, the engineer in Demons: if God does not exist then man is God, and to prove that freedom to everyone he must kill himself. Camus admires how far the reasoning is pushed and then names it as another leap — Kirilov uses death to install a new absolute (the man-god), while the absurd man needs no absolute at all. His verdict on Kafka is parallel: Kafka takes the absurd to its limit and then, at the end, grants hope — an inexplicable grace. Magnificent, Camus says, but that is a different road from his.

Sisyphus: the part that matters is the walk down

The myth is four lines long: Sisyphus offended the gods and was condemned to roll a boulder to the top of a hill, from which it rolls back down, forever. The Greeks meant it as the worst possible punishment — futile and hopeless labour was held to be the most dreadful sentence there is. Camus first restores why he was sentenced: he had put Death in chains, tricked the lord of the underworld into letting him go back up, and then refused to return because he could not give up the sun, the sea, and warm stones. It was his scorn of the gods, his hatred of death and his passion for life that earned him the penalty. In other words, his crime was wanting too badly to live.

Then the book's finest turn: Camus says the part that interests him is not the pushing but the walk back down after the stone has rolled. That interval is unsupervised and taskless — "that hour is the hour of consciousness." If Sisyphus walks down empty and numb, he is a beast of burden and there is no tragedy at all; the tragedy exists only in the moments when he sees his situation clearly — and it is that same clarity that lets him win.

Win how? By scorn: there is no fate that cannot be surmounted by scorn. The gods meant to crush him with meaninglessness — but once he sees it plainly and stops expecting a reprieve, the punishment loses its grip. You cannot threaten with hopelessness a man who has given up hope. Then comes the decisive move: "the rock is his thing." Once the fate is claimed, it stops being a sentence and becomes property. Camus reinforces this with another figure — Sophocles' Oedipus, blinded and stripped of everything, who nevertheless concludes that all is well (paraphrased): the line takes fate out of the hands of the gods and makes it a human matter, to be settled among men.

Hence the ending printed on a thousand covers: "The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy." Note what kind of happiness this is — not motivational uplift, but happiness in the sense of his other line, that happiness and the absurd are two sons of the same earth: only after you fully concede that there is no meaning can you have a joy that does not depend on one. Camus supplies his own ballast: there is no sun without shadow, and it is essential to know the night.

The argument in one line of sight

The four parts are a single straight line: ask whether life is worth living → show that every escape is dishonest → give the one posture that holds → act it out through three figures and a myth.

  • An absurd reasoning: start from suicide as the real question, define the absurd (human demand × the world's silence), seal off both exits (physical suicide, the philosophical leap), and derive three consequences — revolt, freedom, passion.
  • The absurd man: Don Juan (love by quantity), the actor (several lives in an evening), the conqueror (acting on this side of eternity) — not models, but three ways of buying nothing on credit.
  • Absurd creation: art as the most complete absurd act, on condition that it describes without explaining and accepts its own erasure; Kirilov and Kafka are shown smuggling in hope.
  • Sisyphus: the whole argument compressed into one image — lucidity plus scorn plus ownership, and the punishment stops working.

What does it actually establish? Strictly, one negative result: you cannot get from "life has no meaning" to "therefore end it." The rest — that revolt is worth it, that happiness is available — is not deduced but placed in front of you to be recognised. That is the book's weakness and the reason it persuades more than arguments do.

Misreadings and honest criticisms

  • The big misreading: "find your rock and learn to love your grind." The line is now used to gild overwork, and it is exactly backwards. Camus's Sisyphus is not reconciled with the gods; he despises them. His happiness comes from seeing through and refusing every consolation, not from accepting his lot and working hard. Using it to talk someone into enduring a situation they could change turns a book about revolt into a book about submission.
  • The second misreading: absurd = nihilism = anything goes. Camus himself wrote that "everything is permitted" is a bitter acknowledgement, not a cheer (paraphrased). But there is a fair version of this criticism: The Myth of Sisyphus supplies no ethics at all. It handles "should I live" and never touches "may I kill." Camus conceded the hole; it took him nine years and The Rebel to fill it.
  • Is "one must imagine Sisyphus happy" Camus's own leap? This is the sharpest objection. He forbids everyone else to jump from "no answer" to "therefore hope," then jumps from "no answer" to "therefore scorn, therefore happiness." Logically, indifference, irony or quiet detachment are equally consistent. Thomas Nagel's 1971 essay "The Absurd" presses exactly here: if from the eternal standpoint nothing matters, then it does not matter that nothing matters — so the fitting response is irony rather than Camus's heroism. Nagel's point is that Camus stages as high drama something that ought to be worn lightly.
  • The treatment of suicide is thinner than it looks. Getting from "the absurd requires both terms" to "so do not kill yourself" is an aesthetic judgement rather than an argument — why must the human craving for meaning be the term we preserve? Buddhist and Stoic traditions propose dissolving that craving instead: the absurd equally disappears, and nobody has to die. Camus never really answers this route; he files it under evasion.
  • His readings of other philosophers are widely judged unfair. Scholars generally take his Husserl to be a misreading (eidetic intuition is not a jump toward God), and his Kierkegaard and Jaspers to be polemical. This is the sharpness of an essay, not the care of scholarship.
  • The "ethic of quantity" and the Don Juan chapter have dated badly. Every absurd hero is male, and in the Don Juan chapter women are essentially items being counted. That register of virility is the hardest part of the book to read now.
  • The one to remember: Camus said this was only a starting point. In a later preface he described the book as arguing that even within the limits of nihilism it is possible to find the means to go beyond nihilism (paraphrased). Treating The Myth of Sisyphus as Camus's destination is the most common injustice done to him.

Camus beyond this book

The absurd is only the first of his cycles. The Rebel (L'Homme révolté, 1951) asks the next question: if nothing has an ultimate foundation, may I kill? This time he pushes "revolt" from a metaphysical posture into an ethic: "I rebel, therefore we exist." When a slave says no, he is not merely defending himself; he is asserting that something in him is common to everyone and not to be violated — solidarity grows out of what had been a solitary stance. With it comes the concept he cared most about, mesure (limit, measure): the moment revolt licenses killing in the present for the sake of a perfect future — the end of history, the complete revolution — it betrays its own origin and becomes what he called murder committed in the name of history. The book cost him his friendship with Sartre, whose circle held revolutionary violence to be justifiable in history and who could not accept Camus's refusal of any killing done in the future's name. Without this book you would think Camus's philosophy stops at scorn; in fact its real landing point is limit and solidarity.

Two more, briefly. The Plague (1947) puts the same thought into a concrete situation: the epidemic is the absurd made flesh — arbitrary, indiscriminate, killing children — and Dr Rieux's answer is not an explanation but continuing to treat patients: where there is neither God nor good odds, being an honest man means doing your job. The Fall (1956) is Camus dismantling himself: a "judge-penitent" confesses at length purely to earn the right to judge everyone else — his most merciless attack on his own moral high ground. As for The Stranger, Meursault is the absurd in novel form, and gets his own piece on this site.

Ten sentences

1. "There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide" — because whether life is worth living is the only question you must answer with an act, and you answer it again every day.

2. The absurd is not a property of the world nor a defect in you: it is the mismatch between a human being who insists on an answer and a world that keeps silent, and it vanishes the moment either term goes.

3. It is felt before it is understood — weariness (the day the why surfaces by itself), the strangeness of a stone, the man mouthing into a telephone behind glass, and the "tomorrow" we long for while it eats us.

4. Both exits are sealed: physical suicide deletes the human, the philosophical leap deletes the silence; one flees by dying, the other by hoping — and Camus will live on what he knows and on that alone.

5. What is left is revolt: the condemned man looking his executioner in the face and not turning away — it changes nothing, and it is the only life that has not lied to itself; that refusal is what gives life its value.

6. Abolish hope and you stop being hope's hostage: it is tomorrow that enslaves us, and once you see that death cuts every long plan in half, today belongs to you for the first time — that is absurd freedom.

7. With the eternal scoreboard gone, the standard shifts from quality to quantity: not living best but living most — yet "everything is permitted" is no cheer, only a bitter acknowledgement: the books are still audited, by people rather than by God.

8. Absurd creation describes without explaining and accepts that the work will be erased; creating is living twice, and that repetition is the whole payment — no immortality required to redeem it.

9. What matters in Sisyphus is not the pushing but the lucid walk back down: "there is no fate that cannot be surmounted by scorn," and when he says "the rock is his thing," the sentence has become his property.

10. "The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy." — which is not an instruction to love your drudgery: Sisyphus never made peace with the gods, and his happiness comes of seeing through every consolation. Camus went further himself; a decade later, in The Rebel, he supplied the half this book lacks — limit, and solidarity.
