The First 90 Days

The First 90 Days: Proven Strategies for Getting Up to Speed Faster and Smarter · Michael D. Watkins · 2003, updated and expanded 2013

中文 →

In One Sentence

The biggest danger in a new leadership role isn't lack of ability. It's carrying over the playbook that made you successful last time and acting before you've read the situation. Watkins breaks taking charge into ten challenges you can prepare for one by one, and the core is simple: diagnose what kind of situation you've inherited, then learn, negotiate, win and build your team by that situation's rules, so you cross the line where what you contribute equals what you consume as fast as possible.

Where It Sits

Watkins researched and taught leadership transitions at Harvard Business School, later joined IMD in Lausanne, and founded a consultancy that coaches executives through new roles. This is the de facto standard text on onboarding into leadership, and plenty of companies simply hand it to every newly appointed manager.

Core Arguments

One: transitions are the highest-leverage moments in an organization. When you first arrive, small actions get amplified. Get a few things right early and credibility snowballs, making each next step easier. Stumble early and credibility drains, people route around you, you get less information, your judgment gets worse, and a vicious cycle sets in. The first months don't decide how much you get done; they decide which cycle you enter.

Two: there's no universal playbook for taking charge; the playbook depends on the situation. A turnaround and a business riding high, a start-up and a realignment, call for nearly opposite moves. The most common failure is applying the cure for one situation to another.

Three: transitions can be systematically accelerated. Taking charge doesn't have to be sink-or-swim, left to talent and luck. It breaks down into learning, diagnosing, negotiating expectations, securing early wins, aligning the organization, building the team, creating alliances and managing yourself, and each of those has a method.

Key Concepts, Fully Unpacked

1. The breakeven point: the real scoreboard

What it is. Watkins borrows an accounting idea. When a new leader arrives, they consume value: people spend time briefing them, their decisions aren't yet reliable, the team waits to see. As they get up to speed, they start creating value. The moment your cumulative contribution catches up with your cumulative consumption is the breakeven point. In his surveys of senior executives, respondents estimated that a typical mid-level manager takes roughly six months to get there (paraphrased). The whole book is about pushing that curve to the left.

Schematic, not real data: both curves dip and then rise. An accelerated transition makes the dip shallower and crosses zero sooner.

Why it matters. It turns a vague worry ("am I ramping fast enough?") into questions you can actually discuss: what am I consuming right now, what am I contributing, and which moves would make the curve turn up sooner? It also reminds you that consuming value at the start is normal. You don't have to prove yourself through frantic activity; frantic activity is exactly what deepens the dip.

How it changes your view. You start managing the transition as an investment with a deadline. The learning costs up front have to be paid, but every hour should help the curve turn sooner. That's why Watkins doesn't say "get everything done in ninety days." Ninety days is a planning horizon short enough to force priorities and long enough to show early results.

2. "Promote yourself": your old strengths are the hazard

What it is. The opening chapter is called "Promote Yourself." When the appointment comes through, you've been promoted on the org chart, but not yet in your head. Under pressure people retreat to what they know and do best. The star salesperson now running the department can't resist working the big accounts personally; the top engineer turned manager can't resist rewriting the hardest piece of code. It feels comfortable, and organizationally it's a dereliction: your new job is getting things done through others, not doing them yourself.

Transition traps. Watkins lists the classics (paraphrased): sticking with what you know; falling prey to the "action imperative," the urge to do something, anything, right away; setting unrealistic expectations; attempting too much at once; arriving with "the" answer; learning the wrong things (all technical, no culture or politics); neglecting peers. They share one root: treating the new role as a bigger version of the old one.

What to do. He suggests consciously drawing a line: pick a mental start date and, before it, take stock. Which strengths that got you here still matter, which must you let go, and which do you simply not have yet? The higher you go, the more you must shift from depth to breadth, from solving problems to setting agendas, from direct control to influence.

How it changes your view. It keeps you wary of your own confidence. The thing you're surest you're good at is usually where you'll overstep in the new job. A promotion isn't a reward for your old work; it's a demand that you do a different job.

3. Accelerating learning: a learning agenda and three kinds of knowledge

What it is. The usual state of a new leader is drinking from a fire hose: information everywhere, wanting to read everything, understanding nothing. Watkins's fix is to treat learning as an investment that needs a plan. Draw up a learning agenda (the questions you most need answered, the assumptions about this place you need to test), then go after the answers systematically.

Three kinds of learning. Newcomers mostly learn the technical side (products, markets, finances, processes), because it feels most like work. Just as decisive are culture (the unwritten rules: who really decides, how conflict gets handled, what's rewarded) and politics (how influence flows: whose opinion counts, who's allied with whom). Get a technical question wrong and you can fix it; misread the culture or politics and your first move can cost you your credibility.

Five questions. One practical tool: ask people across levels and functions the same set of questions, then compare (paraphrased). What are the biggest challenges the organization faces? Why? What are the most promising untapped opportunities? What would it take to seize them? If you were me, what would you focus on? Ask ten people the same thing and the differences between their answers are themselves data. Where the front line and the executive floor tell different stories, that's often where the problem lives.

The right sources. Beyond your boss and direct reports, he points to front-line staff (closest to reality); "integrators," people whose roles span functions and who see the friction between them, such as operations or program managers; and outsiders like customers, suppliers and analysts. One source is often ignored: the organization's own history. What changes were tried before, and why did they fail?

How it changes your view. Learning isn't prep work before the job; in the first weeks it is the job. Which questions you ask, and whom you ask, is itself a signal to the organization. People will read what you care about from what you ask.

4. STARS: diagnose the situation first

What it is. This is the book's signature framework. Watkins sorts business situations into five types, abbreviated STARS:

SituationWhat it looks likeCore taskBiggest trap
Start-upStarting from nothing: no ready people, money or processesAssemble the resources and capabilitiesNo structure to lean on; spreading too thin
TurnaroundThe business is clearly in trouble, and everyone knows itCut back quickly and decisively to a defensible coreMoving too slowly, or cutting before telling salvageable from not
Accelerated growthThe business is expanding fastBuild structures, systems and people that can carry scaleRunning a scaling business with workshop habits
RealignmentLooks fine, but is drifting toward trouble nobody seesCreate urgency so people see the dangerDenial; you look like you're inventing a problem
Sustaining successA business that's performing wellPreserve its vitality and find the next levelChanging things just to leave your mark

The five STARS situations compared (as described in the book).

Examples. The same person with the same moves succeeds in one situation and fails in another. In a turnaround everyone already knows things are bad, so people want a decisive firefighter, and shutting down a money-losing line wins applause. Bring that style into a sustaining-success unit and you may cut out the very thing that made it work; the team concludes you don't get them. Realignment is the hardest: the numbers still look good and you have to persuade a comfortable group to change. Here Watkins recommends not orders but data, customer voices and external benchmarks that let people see the problem for themselves.

Situations come mixed. Real jobs are rarely pure. One product line may be a turnaround, another in accelerated growth, while back-office processes need realignment. STARS isn't a label to stick on; it's a way to split the business into pieces, diagnose each, and choose a playbook for each. The diagnosis is also the first thing to discuss with your boss (next section).

How it changes your view. It punctures the idea of universal leadership qualities. There is no good leader in the abstract, only a leader who fits the situation. Being a great turnaround manager says nothing about whether you can sustain success, and vice versa. Knowing which situations suit you also tells you where to look for your next role.

5. Negotiating success: five conversations with your boss

What it is. The relationship with your new boss, Watkins says, is the most important one in the transition, and making it work is your job, not theirs: you take one hundred percent responsibility for it. He breaks what you need to discuss into five conversations (not necessarily five meetings, but five topics):

  • Situational diagnosis: do we see the situation the same way? Which STARS type is it? How did it get here?
  • Expectations: what do you need from me in the short and medium term? What counts as success, and by when?
  • Resources: what do I need to deliver that: budget, people, your visible backing?
  • Style: how do you like to communicate? How often, in person or in writing, what do you want to hear about first?
  • Personal development: which of my gaps should I work on in this role?

A few don'ts (paraphrased). Don't stay away from your boss. Don't surprise them. Don't bring them only problems. Don't march through a checklist. Don't expect them to change their style for you. And the essence: underpromise and overdeliver. Set expectations too high in the transition and even good results will read as disappointment.

Why it matters. Many new leaders fail not because they get nothing done but because they get done things the boss wasn't expecting. Expectations are a contract you have to negotiate explicitly, not a consensus you can assume. Working through these five topics means defining success at the start, instead of discovering after ninety days that you were measuring with different rulers.

How it changes your view. "Managing up" stops being flattery and becomes negotiation and calibration. You aren't trying to please your boss; you're agreeing together on a deal you can actually deliver.

6. Early wins: earn credibility before you change the system

What it is. Early wins are visible results in your first months. Their value isn't the result itself but credibility: once people see you can get things done, they'll follow you into bigger changes later. Watkins frames change as coming in waves. The first wave is mostly learning and building credibility, with a few early wins. Only the second wave tackles deep changes to structure, process and culture. Between waves you leave time to observe and adjust.

How to choose. Pick problems that line up with your boss's expectations, genuinely matter to the organization, and can show results within months. Focus on two or three, not everywhere at once. How you win matters as much as what you win. If you want a team that takes more ownership and waits less for orders, you can't get your early win by pulling all-nighters alone. Win using the behaviour you want to spread; the first victory is itself a demonstration.

How it goes wrong. Early wins can be toxic: choosing superficial items because they look good in a status report, or winning so loudly that your predecessor's people feel repudiated. Watkins's reminder is that wins should be credited to "us," not "me," and must not trample what this organization values.

How it changes your view. Credibility becomes capital you must accumulate before you can spend it. Change costs it; mistakes debit it. The classic newcomer error is writing a large cheque while the account is still at zero.

7. Building the team, creating alliances, managing yourself

The team. You usually inherit a team someone else built. Watkins suggests assessing each person early but not hastily, on competence, judgment, energy, focus, relationships and trustworthiness (paraphrased), and then deciding where each one goes: keep in place, keep and develop, move to a better-fitting role, observe and decide later, replace (low priority), replace (high priority). He warns against two common mistakes: keeping people you know aren't working out for too long, and replacing people before you understand the situation.

Alliances. Your success depends on lots of people who don't report to you: peers, other functions, your boss's boss. Watkins suggests mapping influence. Who listens to whom? On your agenda, who are supporters, who are opponents, who's persuadable? Build relationships before you need them; otherwise, when you need support, you're just a stranger asking for something.

Yourself. The transition is when a leader is most isolated and most easily thrown off balance. The old support network stayed behind, the new one isn't built, and the family may be mid-move. He splits self-management into three: success strategies (the methods above), personal disciplines (planning regularly, protecting time to think, "going to the balcony" to see the whole field when emotions push you around), and support systems, above all an advice-and-counsel network with technical advisers, cultural interpreters and political counsellors, ideally both inside and outside the organization.

How it changes your view. Taking charge becomes something to engineer. People, relationships and your own energy are systems to design, not background that falls into place by itself.

The Essential Skeleton

What the book establishes: most failed leadership transitions come not from lack of ability but from doing the wrong things at the wrong time, and those mistakes are predictable, which means they're preventable. The through-line:

  • The problem: leverage is huge in transitions, so small early outcomes compound into virtuous or vicious cycles, yet most organizations leave new leaders to sink or swim.
  • Step one: adjust yourself. See that the new role isn't a scaled-up old one, and let go of the strengths that got you promoted.
  • Step two: learn and diagnose. Learn the technical, cultural and political landscape deliberately, and use STARS to see what situation you've inherited.
  • Step three: define success. On the basis of that diagnosis, agree with your boss on situation, expectations, resources, style and development.
  • Step four: build momentum. Secure early wins that match expectations and are won the new way, and bank credibility.
  • Step five: reshape the organization. Spend that credibility aligning strategy, structure, processes and skills, adjusting the team, and building alliances beyond your authority.
  • Throughout: manage yourself. Discipline and a support network keep your judgment intact through the loneliest stretch.

In one line: change yourself, then read the situation, then settle expectations, then earn credibility, and only then change the organization. The order matters more than any single technique; most crashes come from pulling a later step forward.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: ninety days is a hard deadline. It's a convenient planning horizon, not a magic number. Watkins's own survey data put breakeven at about six months. Reading the book as "deliver a report card within three months" pushes you straight into the action-imperative trap.

Misreading two: early wins mean manufacturing quick highlights. The book insists that early wins should match your boss's expectations, matter to the organization, and be won in the way you want to spread. Picking cosmetic results to look good is the very thing it warns against.

Misreading three: it's only for executives. Most examples come from middle and senior management, but the framework fits anyone entering a new situation: an engineer leading people for the first time, a program manager moving across divisions, an individual contributor joining a new company. What changes is only how much you have to align.

Criticism one: a thin evidence base. The book rests mainly on Watkins's interviews, teaching cases and consulting experience, plus executive survey estimates such as time to breakeven, not on controlled studies. The framework is clear and useful, but the claim that using it gets you to breakeven faster is practitioner wisdom; the book offers no rigorous causal evidence.

Criticism two: leader-centric. The whole book follows one new leader taking control, and assumes a clear hierarchy, a clear boss and authority flowing downward. In flat organizations, open-source communities and matrix teams, or when you're the one being led, a lot of the advice needs heavy rework. Critics also note that stressing the newcomer's command can underrate a team's own agency and accumulated wisdom.

Criticism three: a pull toward short-termism. If organizations turn "early wins" and "the 90-day plan" into performance metrics, they can reward visible small moves and avoidance of root problems that take longer to fix. Watkins softens this with his waves of change, but the book's structure does put its weight on the first few months.

Criticism four: tidy frameworks, messy reality. The five STARS types have clean edges, while real businesses are often several at once, and the situation itself changes when you arrive. The book acknowledges mixtures, but its diagnostic tools remain fairly static.

Watkins Beyond This Book

This book is about taking charge. Watkins's later Harvard Business Review article "How Managers Become Leaders" (2012) supplies the other half. When you move from running a function (say engineering or sales) to owning a whole business, you don't need a stronger version of the same skills. You need seven shifts of role (his "seismic shifts," paraphrased): specialist to generalist (you needn't know every function's details, but you must be able to judge its people and decisions); analyst to integrator (making collective trade-offs across functions); tactician to strategist (moving between detail and big picture, spotting patterns); bricklayer to architect (designing the organization's structure and systems instead of building by hand); problem solver to agenda setter (deciding which problems to solve); warrior to diplomat (dealing with governments, media and partners outside); and supporting cast to lead role (everything you say and do is amplified and interpreted).

Put together with this book, Watkins's full claim is that transitions are hard not because the new job is "more" but because it's "different". Every step up asks you to give up one identity and learn another.

Crossovers

  • Complexity science · increasing returns and lock-in: W. Brian Arthur showed that in systems with increasing returns, small early accidents get amplified and lock in the outcome (as in the VHS–Betamax fight). Both explain why the first moves carry outsized weight; but Arthur stresses that chance often decides, while Watkins assumes you can design the early events.
  • Psychology · thin slices: Ambady and Rosenthal (1993) found that ratings of silent video clips of teachers, from a few seconds to half a minute long, predicted end-of-term student evaluations. Both show first impressions form fast and are hard to reverse; but thin slices capture nonverbal warmth, while Watkins cares about visible results and methods.
  • Distributed systems · catch up on the log before you vote: when the Raft consensus algorithm adds servers, newcomers first join as non-voting members and replicate the log; only once caught up do they vote, so a lagging node can't stall the majority. That's "learn before you act" as engineering: someone not yet synced to the organization's real state shouldn't rush to vote on key decisions. The difference: a node copies state verbatim, whereas a person must rebuild culture and politics through interpretation, and the act of syncing changes what's being synced.
  • Buddhism · beginner's mind: Shunryu Suzuki wrote in Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind: "In the beginner's mind there are many possibilities, but in the expert's there are few." "Promote yourself" asks you to drop the expert's mind; but Watkins wants beginner's mind as a temporary tool for becoming an expert again fast, while Zen treats it as a practice to keep for good.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① Transitions are peak leverage: small early wins and losses snowball into virtuous or vicious cycles.

② The real scoreboard is breakeven, the moment the value you contribute catches up with the value you consume.

③ A promotion doesn't reward your old work; it asks you to do a different job. Your favourite strengths are where you'll overstep.

④ In the first weeks, learning is the job: the technical side, and even more the culture and the politics.

⑤ Ask ten people the same questions; the differences between their answers are data.

⑥ There's no good leader in the abstract: start-up, turnaround, accelerated growth, realignment and sustaining success each need a different playbook.

⑦ The relationship with your boss is yours to manage: settle situation, expectations, resources, style and development, and underpromise, overdeliver.

⑧ Early wins buy credibility, and how you win should model the behaviour you want.

⑨ Build alliances before you need them, or you'll arrive as a stranger asking for favours.

⑩ Sequence beats technique: change yourself, read the situation, settle expectations, earn credibility, and only then change the organization.
