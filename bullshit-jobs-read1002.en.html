Bullshit Jobs

Bullshit Jobs: A Theory · David Graeber · 2018

中文 →

In One Sentence

Rich societies are full of respectable, well-paid jobs that even the people doing them believe should not exist — and this is not a market failure but a political and moral arrangement: we would rather have people pretend to be busy than let them not work.

Where It Sits

Graeber was an American anthropologist who taught for years at the London School of Economics, helped popularise the "We are the 99%" slogan of Occupy Wall Street, and died in 2020. The book grew out of a short 2013 essay in a small magazine, "On the Phenomenon of Bullshit Jobs," which went viral and was translated into dozens of languages; he then solicited hundreds of first-hand accounts from readers and built this "theory" on them.

The Core Argument

  • A large share of paid work is, in the eyes of those who do it, entirely pointless — not hard, not badly paid, but such that the world would not be one bit different if it were never done. Graeber guesses this may cover a third, perhaps even half, of work in rich countries (a guess; see the criticisms).
  • Meaningless work wounds people. It brings not ease but depression, anxiety and self-loathing. Graeber calls it "spiritual violence": humans need to be the cause of something, and being denied that is itself an injury.
  • These jobs multiply, and survive market competition, because their logic is political and moral rather than economic: hierarchies and retinues inside organisations, plus an ethic that says work is virtuous in itself and suffering is what earns you your pay. The result is an inversion: the more obviously a job benefits other people, the less it tends to pay.

The Key Concepts, One by One

1. What counts as a bullshit job: the worker is the judge

Graeber's definition is carefully built. Paraphrased: a form of paid employment so completely pointless, unnecessary or pernicious that even the employee cannot justify its existence — even though, as a condition of employment, they feel obliged to pretend this is not the case.

Three parts of that sentence each rule out a misunderstanding:

  • The judge is the employee, not an outsider. Graeber does not try to settle grand questions like "do insurance brokers add value?" He asks only: do you think your job matters? His reasoning is that the person doing a job knows best what it actually consists of. An analyst who spends all day copying figures into spreadsheets for a report she knows nobody reads is better placed than any economist to say whether that report is useful.
  • Pretence is essential. A sinecure on its own is nothing new. What makes a job bullshit is that you must perform its importance — in meetings, in weekly updates, on your CV. That layer of pretence is where the pain lives: every day you take part in a collective lie.
  • It is not the same as a "shit job." Graeber draws the line deliberately. Cleaners, care workers and delivery riders do exhausting, poorly paid and often looked-down-on work, but it is useful; the world would get dirtier and worse the moment it stopped. Bullshit jobs are the reverse: often respectable, well paid, office-based and titled — just useless. One is bad conditions; the other is an empty core.

A gut test: if this role vanished tomorrow, would anyone notice? Let nurses strike for three days and the hospital seizes up. Let a "strategic synergy specialist" disappear for three months and perhaps only his boss notices — because the boss now has one fewer subordinate to mention in reports.

Graeber opens with a real news story that makes the point. Around 2010, a civil servant in Cádiz, Spain, was found to have skipped work for at least six years while still drawing his salary — discovered only when he was due a long-service award (paraphrased from press reports). The absurdity is not that he shirked. It is that his post sat empty for six years and nobody, and nothing, was affected.

The shift in perspective: usefulness is not measured by salary, title or GDP, but by the honest answer in the worker's own head. And once you let people answer honestly, the answers are startling.

2. Five kinds of bullshit job: a taxonomy

From readers' letters, Graeber distils five types. They are not mutually exclusive; one job can combine several.

TypeExists in order toTypical examples
FlunkiesMake someone else look or feel importantReceptionists where nobody visits, assistants who exist as executive décor, doormen
GoonsPush against other goons — because the other side hired someLobbyists, corporate lawyers, PR staff, telemarketers
Duct tapersPatch problems that should not existPeople who manually shuttle data between broken systems; people who apologise for an unreliable boss
Box tickersLet an organisation claim it is doing something it is notCompliance reports nobody reads, perfunctory staff-satisfaction surveys
TaskmastersManage people who need no managing, or invent bullshit tasks for othersMiddle managers who only assign and track; designers of new appraisal forms

The five types, sorted by the question: for whom, and why, does this role exist?

Flunkies are feudal display. A medieval lord kept a retinue not because its members did anything but because a big retinue was status. The modern version: an executive's importance is often measured by how many people report to him.

Goons follow the logic of an arms race. If every country disarmed, nobody would need an army; but as long as your rival has one, you need one too. Much corporate law, lobbying and PR is this kind of zero-sum mutual cancellation — its net contribution to society is close to zero, or negative. Notably, goons rarely think their work has no effect; they think it shouldn't exist.

Duct tapers are the easiest to spot. Graeber's correspondents include people whose whole job was retyping data from one format into another because two departments' software didn't talk to each other, and hiring a person was cheaper than fixing the software (or nobody had the authority to fix it). A duct taper is proof that an organisation would rather pay forever to hide a defect than repair it.

Box tickers manufacture evidence of caring. One correspondent worked in a care home, where her main duty was interviewing residents about what recreational activities they'd like and entering the answers on forms — which were then filed and never acted on (paraphrased). The document exists not to make anything happen, but so the organisation can prove its innocence when nothing does.

Taskmasters come in two kinds: those who supervise people who would work perfectly well on their own, which is merely redundant; and the worse kind, whose job is to generate bullshit for others — new reporting procedures, new KPIs, new layers of sign-off. They are the reproductive organs of bullshit.

Two extensions are worth keeping. First, "second-order" bullshit: the cleaner at a bullshit company does real work, but in service of a pointless whole. Second, "bullshitization": useful jobs being steadily eaten by paperwork. Teachers, nurses and academics all report the same thing — less time teaching, caring and researching, more time on forms, meetings and assessments. Many jobs are not bullshit through and through; they are being hollowed out from within.

3. Keynes's fifteen hours: technology should have freed us

Graeber's question starts from a historical puzzle. In 1930 the economist John Maynard Keynes wrote a short essay, "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," predicting that by the end of the century technology would let countries like Britain and the United States get by on roughly a fifteen-hour working week. He was right about the technology — if anything, too cautious — and completely wrong about the hours.

The usual explanation is consumerism: people chose more stuff over more leisure. Graeber isn't persuaded. Over the past century, he notes, the share of employment in work that actually makes things — farming, manufacturing, domestic service — has collapsed, while administrative, managerial, consulting, financial, sales and "professional service" work has ballooned. Productivity gains didn't turn into free time; they turned into a vast new zone of vague, office-bound positions. His claim: we have in effect reached Keynes's world — we've just found a respectable way to waste the extra hours, by having people pretend to work in offices.

This turns a piece of common sense on its head. We think capitalism ruthlessly strips out excess staff, and that "everyone has a job, but the jobs are fake" is the signature of Soviet-style inefficiency. Graeber's answer: capitalism also mass-produces fake jobs — it just dresses them better.

4. Two kinds of evidence: a poll and hundreds of letters

Where Graeber's numbers come from needs saying plainly. In 2015 the pollster YouGov asked British respondents whether their job made a meaningful contribution to the world. 37% said no, 50% said yes, and the rest weren't sure. A similar survey in the Netherlands found around 40%. That is the source of the book's much-quoted "more than a third."

The other half of the evidence is qualitative: after the essay spread, he received hundreds of personal accounts, quoted at length in the book. You hear these people in their own voices — a man inside a German military contracting chain describing how moving one soldier's computer to the next room required several layers of subcontractors and days of paperwork (paraphrased); people performing busyness in half-empty offices, filling the day with browsing, games or their own projects, flicking screens whenever the boss walks past.

The two kinds of evidence do different work: the poll gives a magnitude; the letters give the mechanism and the texture. Each has a weakness — the poll question is broad, and the letters are a self-selected sample (people who think their job is bullshit are more likely to write in). We'll come back to this.

5. Spiritual violence: why being paid to do nothing hurts

By textbook economics, someone on a good salary with nothing to do should be a winner: minimum effort, maximum reward. Yet Graeber's correspondents describe the opposite — depression, anxiety, insomnia, self-loathing, a shame they can't quite name; some said they felt they were slowly dying. Graeber calls this "spiritual violence."

Why? He draws on the German psychologist Karl Groos, who around 1901 observed the delight infants show when they first discover they can make something happen — nudge a pencil and it rolls away. Groos called it "the pleasure at being the cause." Later experiments found that when an infant's actions suddenly stop having any effect, the baby becomes upset and then withdraws. Graeber's inference: our sense of self rests largely on "I can have an effect on the world." A job you know has no effect dismantles that foundation, systematically, every day.

There is a second injury: compulsory pretence. You not only have nothing to do; you must act as if you do, while everyone knows otherwise. Graeber likens it to being trapped in a play nobody dares to stop — you are actor and audience at once, and at every appraisal you must testify to the play's importance. Many people would rather take hard, badly paid but useful work precisely to escape this "hypocritical misery."

The shift: work is not just an exchange of time for money; it is one of the ways people confirm that they exist. Unemployment hurts not only because of lost income, and a fake job can inflict the same damage with the income intact.

6. Managerial feudalism: why markets don't weed bullshit out

The sharpest objection: firms chase profit, so why would they pay people to do nothing? Shouldn't competition eliminate dead weight?

Graeber's answer is a structure he calls managerial feudalism. The economic logic of feudalism is not "produce more" but "extract wealth from producers and redistribute it among those at the top" — the lord takes the peasants' harvest and uses it to keep vassals, retainers and ceremony. Graeber argues that a growing slice of today's economy, especially finance, insurance and real estate (the familiar "FIRE" sector), works much the same way: profit comes more from rent-taking and lending than from making things. In such a system, you don't hire people for output; you can hire them for status, to enlarge your fiefdom, to hold your position in internal politics.

The mechanism inside the firm: a department head's budget, pay and clout track the number of people beneath him, so every manager has a reason to hire more and none to prove those people are useful. When cuts come, frontline "real" jobs often go first (on the books they are pure cost), while managerial and compliance roles can actually grow — someone has to manage the layoffs. Graeber also points out that a big corporation is internally a planned economy: resources are allocated by layers of bureaucrats, not by markets. Competition happens between firms, not inside them.

This is the book's most explanatory and most contested link. Its value is that it makes "organisational efficiency" a question rather than an assumption: a profitable company is not proof that each of its jobs creates value; it may profit precisely by extraction, and extraction needs a retinue.

7. The moral inversion: the more useful, the less pay

Graeber offers an uncomfortable rule of thumb: the more obviously a job benefits others, the less it tends to pay. Carers, teachers, cleaners, paramedics, childcare workers — their social value is barely disputable, yet they are perennially underpaid, while it is hard to match the salaries of lobbyists, private-equity partners and consultants to anything like equivalent social contribution. He cites a study by Britain's New Economics Foundation that roughly estimated the "social value" of several occupations, concluding that a hospital cleaner creates about ten pounds of social value for every pound of pay, while some financial jobs come out negative (paraphrased; the method is very rough, and Graeber treats it as illustrative).

The deeper question is why society finds this inversion natural. Graeber's answer is a psychology he calls "moral envy": someone stuck in a bullshit job comes to resent those who get to do meaningful work — you already have meaning, how dare you also want a decent wage? So striking teachers and Tube drivers get called selfish, while bankers' bonuses rarely provoke the same moral outrage. Meaning itself is treated as part of the pay, and deducted from the wage.

Underneath lies history. Graeber traces the idea that work is a virtue: a Puritan ethic that treated labour as discipline for fallen human nature and idleness as sin; then, in the industrial age, the notion of buying other people's time, so that a job's legitimacy comes not from what it achieves but from how many hours you hand over and how much you endure. Once suffering itself is a virtue, pointless work becomes morally safe — at least you're suffering.

Relatedly, Graeber stresses "caring labour": most genuinely meaningful work, he argues, is at heart a form of care — for people, for things, for the environment — and working-class labour has always been mostly caring rather than the factory image of "production." Because we picture work through the metaphor of production, we can't see the labour that actually holds society up.

8. The way out: decouple work from survival

Graeber says explicitly that he doesn't want to write a policy prescription, but he discusses one direction: universal basic income — an unconditional income for everyone, enough to live on. His reasoning differs from the usual "automation will take the jobs" case: if people didn't have to take a job they don't believe in just to survive, many bullshit jobs would vanish because nobody would do them; people would go and find something meaningful to do — he believed most would rather be busy with something useful.

The objection: lots of people would do nothing at all. Graeber's reply: lots of people already do nothing at all — they just have to pretend otherwise and suffer for it. Better to set people free than to make them pretend to work.

The Spine of the Book

The argument compresses into one line:

(1) The phenomenon: ask people whether their job matters, and in rich countries a third or more say no (a poll plus hundreds of testimonies). (2) The taxonomy: by whom-is-it-for, these jobs fall into five kinds — display, opposition, patching, box-ticking, task-making. (3) The harm: people need to be causes; pointlessness plus forced pretence amounts to spiritual violence. (4) The cause: productivity gains didn't become leisure but, inside a structure of managerial feudalism, became retinue jobs; incentives inside firms reward expansion rather than output. (5) Why we tolerate it: a morality that treats work as virtue and suffering as legitimacy, plus "moral envy" toward meaningful work, keeps useful work underpaid and useless work respectable. (6) The way out: decouple income from work, so that people can refuse bullshit.

In the end Graeber does not establish a precise percentage. He establishes a change of lens: in our society, "work" has long been not just a means of production but a moral obligation and a form of social control — which is why, even when it's useless, we invent it.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "bullshit jobs" means grim, exhausting jobs. The opposite. Graeber explicitly separates the two: shit jobs are useful but badly treated; bullshit jobs may be well treated but useless. Many readers project complaints about overtime and low pay onto this book, and that is a category mistake.

Misreading 2: all middle management, all admin, all finance is bullshit. Graeber's definition rests on the worker's own judgment; he doesn't declare whole professions bullshit, and the same title can be useful at one firm and pointless at another. Reading the book as "any job I look down on is worthless" violates its own method.

Misreading 3: this is about modern people being lazy. The book argues the reverse: people want to do meaningful things, and the suffering comes from not being allowed to.

The strongest criticism is empirical:

  • The numbers are probably far too high. Dutch economists Robert Dur and Max van Lent (2019), using cross-national data from the International Social Survey Programme (ISSP), found that about 8% of workers consider their job socially useless — nowhere near a third. British researchers Magdalena Soffia, Alex Wood and Brendan Burchell, using the European Working Conditions Survey (EWCS), found that about 4.8% of employees "rarely or never" feel they do useful work — and that this share fell rather than rose between 2005 and 2015.
  • The distribution is wrong. Soffia and colleagues also found that people who see their jobs as useless are not concentrated in the respectable sectors Graeber predicted — finance, management, consulting; plenty of workers in jobs he would class as "shit but useful" (some cleaning, sales and service roles) feel the same. Their conclusion: feeling that your work is pointless has more to do with bad management, lack of autonomy and lack of respect — what Marx called "alienation," the estrangement of workers from their labour, its products and one another — than with the job really being useless. The paper's title says it all: "Alienation Is Not 'Bullshit'" (2022).
  • Workers may not be the best judges. Graeber's method relies on self-assessment, but someone inside a huge organisation sees only a part and may not see the legal, risk or coordination role played by their "box-ticking" — like a keystone unaware that the whole arch rests on it. Shortly after the 2013 essay, The Economist ran a rebuttal arguing, in effect, that a finely divided, globalised economy needs vast amounts of coordination, management and compliance, and that roles which look empty are really the necessary cost of complexity (paraphrased).
  • "Managerial feudalism" lacks direct evidence. It works better as a provocative frame than as a tested model; economists would point out that management bloat can also be explained by more regulation, more organisational complexity, and new functions created by information technology.

Fairly put: Graeber's numbers are probably too high, but the phenomenon he captured — a growing meaningless portion inside a great deal of work, and the psychological damage it does — is real in many people's experience. And the critics' "alienation" and Graeber's "bullshit" point at the same pain; they disagree on the diagnosis — one says the job itself is empty, the other says bad management empties the person.

Graeber Beyond This Book

This book is one stretch of a larger project. All his life Graeber asked: how were the economic and social arrangements we take as natural and inevitable actually made?

Debt: The First 5,000 Years (2011): he dismantles the textbook origin story in which barter came first and money second — the anthropological record contains almost no barter economies, and credit and IOUs are far more common. Debt, he argues, came before coins and has always carried moral weight ("you must pay your debts" feels self-evident), and historic debt crises have often ended in amnesties or uprisings. He also proposes "baseline communism": beneath every society runs everyday mutual aid on the principle "from each according to ability, to each according to need" — ask a colleague for a pen and nobody charges you.

The Utopia of Rules (2015): a book on bureaucracy, and a sibling to this one. He states an "iron law of liberalism": any market reform made in the name of reducing government interference ends up increasing the total number of regulations and bureaucrats — because sustaining a "free market" takes enormous quantities of rules, compliance and enforcement. That is precisely the soil in which box tickers and taskmasters breed.

The Dawn of Everything (2021, with archaeologist David Wengrow): the two attack the linear story in which humanity moves inevitably from small egalitarian bands to farming, states and inequality, using archaeological evidence that early humans experimented with many social forms, sometimes switching seasonally between hierarchy and equality. They emphasise three basic freedoms: to move away, to disobey, and to reorganise social relations. Set against this, the subtext of Bullshit Jobs is that today's work regime is also a choice, not a fate.

Crossovers

  • Evolutionary biology · Zahavi's handicap principle: Israeli biologist Amotz Zahavi argued that a peacock's tail is a credible signal of fitness precisely because it is costly and cumbersome. Flunky jobs are handicaps too: being able to afford useless people signals status. The difference: the peacock pays for its own tail, while a retinue's cost is usually passed to shareholders and frontline staff.
  • Psychology · self-determination theory: Edward Deci and Richard Ryan hold that people have three basic psychological needs — autonomy, competence and relatedness — and that motivation and well-being collapse without them. A bullshit job strips both competence (my actions have effects) and autonomy (I must perform). That gives Graeber's "spiritual violence" and his critics' "alienation" one shared underlying mechanism — the two camps may be closer than they look.
  • Distributed systems · coordination overhead and Brooks's law: in The Mythical Man-Month, Fred Brooks observed that adding people to a late software project makes it later, because communication paths grow with the square of headcount. Many taskmasters and duct tapers are coordination cost made flesh. The difference: in systems, coordination overhead is a real, necessary cost; Graeber thinks much of it is self-generated.
  • Security engineering · Schneier's "security theater": cryptographer Bruce Schneier coined the term for measures that make people feel safer without making them safer, like some airport screening rituals. It's the same structure as box-ticking: the output is the appearance of action. The difference: security theater sometimes really does reassure the public or deter outsiders, whereas an unread report has no audience at all.
  • Buddhism · right livelihood: the Buddha counted earning a living rightly as part of the path, ruling out trades that harm others — dealing in weapons, poisons, slaughter. Graeber's goons connect here: zero-sum opposition is itself a harm. The difference: right livelihood is about not harming; Graeber is about not meaning anything — a harmless but empty job may be no fault at all in Buddhist terms.

The Book in Ten Sentences

1. A bullshit job is one that even the person doing it cannot justify, yet must pretend is important.

2. It is the opposite of a shit job: shit jobs are useful but mistreated; bullshit jobs are respectable but useless.

3. Five archetypes — flunkies, goons, duct tapers, box tickers, taskmasters: ask "who is this role really for?" and you'll recognise them.

4. Keynes's fifteen-hour week has effectively arrived — we just spend the extra hours pretending to work.

5. People need "the pleasure at being the cause": take it away, add compulsory pretence, and you have spiritual violence.

6. Markets don't weed bullshit out because it obeys a different logic: in a rent-taking economy, headcount is status.

7. The more obviously a job helps others, the less it tends to pay — meaning is treated as a deduction from the wage.

8. Once suffering itself is a virtue, pointless work becomes morally safe.

9. Empirical studies put the share far below a third and trace the pain more to alienation from bad management — but both sides agree that people are genuinely harmed by meaningless work.

10. Graeber's final point: today's work regime was made, so it can be remade.
