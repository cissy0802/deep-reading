Leadership BS

Leadership BS: Fixing Workplaces and Careers One Truth at a Time · Jeffrey Pfeffer · 2015

中文 →

In One Sentence

Year after year the leadership industry preaches modesty, authenticity, truthfulness, trustworthiness and care for others, yet the people who reach the top rarely behave that way, and employees are no happier with their bosses for all the sermonizing. The sermons fail because they mistake what leaders should be for what leaders actually are. Pfeffer wants readers to pry those two apart: first see what leaders really do and what really gets rewarded, then talk about fixing workplaces, and about protecting your own career.

Where It Sits

Pfeffer is a professor of organizational behavior at Stanford's Graduate School of Business. As far back as 1977 he published a well-known paper, "The Ambiguity of Leadership," questioning the popular belief that organizations rise and fall mainly on their leaders. This book is his indictment of the entire leadership industry, and it is the premise behind his Power (read972 on this site): that book teaches you how to get power; this one explains why the virtue talk in leadership bestsellers won't help you.

Core Claims

  • The leadership industry has failed, and the failure is documented. Companies pour vast sums into leadership training, speakers and books (industry estimates Pfeffer cites run to billions of dollars a year in the U.S. alone; paraphrased), yet outcome measures such as employee engagement, trust in leaders and managerial failure rates have barely budged in decades. Huge inputs, flat outputs, and almost nobody held accountable.
  • The root of the failure is passing off "ought" as "is." The five virtues the industry sells (modesty, authenticity, truth-telling, trustworthiness, putting others first) are normative aspirations (claims about how things should be), not descriptive facts (claims about how things are). In the real world many successful leaders are narcissistic, skilled at performing, willing to lie, willing to break trust, and look after themselves first; and those behaviors often go unpunished or are even rewarded.
  • To change workplaces, accept the truth first; to protect yourself, don't count on organizations or leaders to protect you. His prescription: look at evidence rather than stories, measure leaders' actual behavior and its consequences and hold them accountable; and, for individuals, learn to "take care of yourself," minding your own interests the way leaders mind theirs, because nobody else will.

The Core Concepts, One by One

1. The leadership industry: a business that measures satisfaction, not results

What it is. By the leadership industry Pfeffer means the whole supply chain built around "how to be a good leader": corporate training and executive education, inspirational speakers, consultants, bestsellers, business-school courses, assorted leadership assessments. Its product is a story about how leaders ought to be; its customers are mostly companies and their executives.

The ledger. Pfeffer sets inputs beside outputs. Inputs: billions in training budgets every year and shelves of leadership books. Outputs: Gallup's engagement surveys have shown for years that only about three in ten U.S. employees are "engaged" at work, and far fewer worldwide; the Edelman Trust Barometer has found persistently low public trust in business leaders; and research on managerial derailment (high-potential managers who are demoted, fired or stalled because of behavior or relationship problems) estimates that a large share of managers, by some estimates around half, ultimately fail (paraphrased). If a patient took a drug for decades and none of his numbers moved, a sensible person would question the drug, not raise the dose.

Why nobody questions the drug. Because the industry hardly measures itself. Training evaluation has a classic framework, the Kirkpatrick model: level one is participants' reaction (did they like the course), level two is what they learned, level three is whether their behavior changed, level four is whether organizational results improved. Pfeffer notes that leadership training overwhelmingly stops at level one, a post-course rating form the trade calls a "smile sheet." A course that leaves people fired up counts as a success; whether they go back and become better bosses, nobody tracks. Speakers are paid by the applause and books judged by sales, and both reward making people feel good, not making workplaces better.

How it changes your view. Next time you hear a moving leadership story, ask: has this claim been tested? Tested against the audience's feelings, or against what actually happens to employees? That question turns you from a moved listener into an auditor.

2. Modesty: the sermons praise it, reality rewards narcissism

The sermon. The best-known version comes from Jim Collins's Good to Great: he called the CEOs who led companies through a breakthrough "Level 5 leaders," marked by personal humility plus professional will: low-key, quick to credit the team.

The reality. Pfeffer marshals the opposite evidence. A 2008 study by the social psychologist Amy Brunell and colleagues found that in leaderless group discussions, people higher in narcissism were more likely to emerge as leaders. (In psychology, narcissism means a personality tendency toward an inflated self-image, a craving for attention and admiration, and a sense of being special.) His Stanford colleague Charles O'Reilly and coauthors studied tech-company CEOs and found that the more narcissistic the CEO, the higher his pay and the bigger the gap between his pay and that of his top team. And look at business icons: Steve Jobs, Oracle's Larry Ellison; none is famous for modesty.

The mechanism. Why do narcissists rise? Because when information is thin, people use confidence as a stand-in for competence. Experiments by Berkeley's Cameron Anderson and colleagues found that overconfident people are rated by peers as more competent and gain higher status in groups, even when they are not actually better (paraphrased). Narcissists promote themselves, volunteer for visible work and dominate meetings, which are exactly the things most visible at the moment someone is picked to lead. Modesty is a virtue in stories and often an invisibility cloak in a promotion race.

How it changes your view. Pfeffer isn't saying narcissism is good. He's saying that if you believe quiet good work will eventually be noticed, while watching a showier colleague shoot past you, you'll end up confused, aggrieved, even doubting yourself. Once you see the mechanism, you at least know the problem isn't that you're not good enough; it's what the selection process rewards.

3. Authenticity: leaders don't need to "be themselves," they need to play the part

The sermon. The leading voice of "authentic leadership" is Bill George, former CEO of Medtronic and later a Harvard Business School professor, whose Authentic Leadership and True North urge leaders to know their values and stay true to them, consistent inside and out.

Pfeffer's rebuttal. Authenticity literally means acting as your true self, but leadership often requires doing the opposite of what you feel in the moment: projecting certainty while afraid, cooperating with someone you can't stand, staying calm when you want to explode. A leader who "authentically" shows panic in a crisis only spreads it. Leaders are more like actors: a good actor doesn't "be himself"; he makes the emotions the role needs come through convincingly.

The evidence: self-monitoring. The psychologist Mark Snyder introduced the concept of self-monitoring: high self-monitors watch the situation and others' reactions closely and adjust what they say and do; low self-monitors act on their inner states wherever they are, and are in that sense more "authentic." Organizational research finds high self-monitors more likely to emerge as group leaders and to win promotions; a multi-year study of MBA graduates by Martin Kilduff and David Day is one example. In other words, what gets rewarded is adaptability, not fidelity to self.

How it changes your view. "I refuse to fake it" is often treated as integrity. Pfeffer's reminder: leadership is a job with role requirements, and acting as the situation demands is not hypocrisy but professional skill. The real question is not "am I being myself?" but "what does this situation need, and can I supply it?"

4. Telling the truth: lying is common and often unpunished

The sermon. Leaders must be honest and transparent, because once integrity goes, everything goes.

The reality. Pfeffer starts by noting that lying is an everyday human habit: diary studies by the psychologist Bella DePaulo found that college students lie about twice a day and community adults about once. And lies are hard to catch: a meta-analysis by Charles Bond and DePaulo showed people tell truth from lies only about 54% of the time, barely better than a coin flip. For the powerful, lying is even easier: research by Dana Carney and colleagues at Columbia found that people placed in high-power roles showed fewer physiological and emotional signs of stress when lying than low-power people did, making them harder to detect (paraphrased).

Why lying often works. Pfeffer points to a more unsettling mechanism: lies can be self-fulfilling. A founder pitches investors and customers a product that doesn't exist yet, then builds it with the money and orders that follow; a leader declares "we will get this done," people work harder because of it, and it does get done. Those around Steve Jobs called his gift a "reality distortion field": he could make people believe impossible deadlines and goals, and often enough those goals were met. In hindsight the original falsehood gets retold as vision. Add short memories and the way success excuses the past, and the cost of lying is far smaller than the ethics lectures claim.

How it changes your view. This isn't an endorsement of lying but a warning: don't assume that truth will out and liars will be found out as a matter of course. If an organization wants honesty, it has to build mechanisms that make lying costly, rather than rely on leaders' consciences.

5. Trust: everyone calls it essential, and breaking it is routine

The sermon. Trust is the bedrock of leadership; leaders must keep their word and build trust.

The reality. Pfeffer catalogs everyday betrayals: companies promise no layoffs and then lay people off, promised pensions and benefits get cut, assurances given to employees in a merger are quietly voided. His observation is that those who break trust often pay little for it, because breaking it frequently benefits them (saving money, escaping a bind, moving first), the betrayed party lacks the power to retaliate, and onlookers soon forget. The value of trust is real, but so is the temptation to break it, and the temptation often wins.

How it changes your view. Turn trust from an expectation about someone's character into a judgment about incentives. Don't ask "is this person trustworthy?" Ask "in this situation, does keeping faith pay for them, and what would breaking it cost them?" That is how negotiators and contract-writers think, and how you protect yourself.

6. Taking care of others: "leaders eat last" and the norm of self-interest

The sermon. Simon Sinek's 2014 bestseller Leaders Eat Last takes its title from a U.S. Marine Corps custom: at mealtime officers go to the back of the line and the troops eat first. Earlier still, Robert Greenleaf's 1970 idea of "servant leadership" held that a leader is first a servant who puts followers' growth and well-being ahead of his own.

The reality. Pfeffer answers with data: over decades, U.S. CEO pay has soared relative to ordinary workers (the Economic Policy Institute's CEO-to-worker pay ratio runs in the hundreds to one; paraphrased), and many executives collect handsome bonuses while cutting jobs and benefits. Real leaders mostly eat first, and they eat the most.

The mechanism: the norm of self-interest. Pfeffer borrows a concept from the Stanford psychologist Dale Miller, the "norm of self-interest": in Western and especially American culture, people not only expect others to act on their own interest but regard doing so as normal and rational, to the point of looking for a selfish motive behind altruistic acts before they make sense. Under that norm, a leader who puts himself first is not surprising; he's seen as reasonable.

Hence "take care of yourself." This is the book's most practical and most jarring lesson: since organizations and leaders won't put you first, you have to look after your own career interests: don't stake your loyalty on one company, track your market value, leave when the time is right. Employment, Pfeffer stresses, has become increasingly transactional, and expecting it to behave like a family only guarantees you'll be blindsided on layoff day.

7. Why the myths don't die: good stories, survivors and the romance of leadership

What it is. If these claims fit reality so poorly, why do they endure? Pfeffer offers several mutually reinforcing reasons.

First, good stories sell. Audiences want uplift and speakers and authors happily supply it; a talk about how leaders are often narcissistic, dishonest and self-serving rarely gets a second booking. Second, the winners write the narrative. Leaders' memoirs, interviews and case studies are polished, after-the-fact versions told by the successful, with the struggle, scheming and betrayal edited out and the humility and teamwork played up. That is survivorship bias: we hear only from those who made it, and only what they choose to tell. Third, we over-credit leaders to begin with. In 1985 the organizational scholar James Meindl and colleagues described the "romance of leadership": people tend to attribute an organization's success or failure to its leader personally, underweighting structural factors like industry cycles and luck, and the more extreme the performance, the stronger the attribution. This runs straight back to Pfeffer's own 1977 "Ambiguity of Leadership."

Fourth, confusing ought with is pays. For leaders, the virtue story is a respectable cloak; for employees, believing that good people get rewarded is more comfortable than facing a harsh reality. Everyone draws a little comfort from the myth, so nobody has a reason to puncture it.

8. What to do: measure, hold accountable, look after yourself

Pfeffer's prescription is unromantic and comes down to three points.

One, replace stories with evidence. Just as medicine moved from eminent physicians' experience to evidence-based medicine, management should look at data on which practices actually improve employees' lives and organizational results. This is the evidence-based management he championed with Robert Sutton.

Two, measure leaders' actual behavior and its consequences, and hold them accountable. Not what leaders say or how many courses they've attended, but the engagement, turnover and health of the people who work for them; and let those numbers genuinely affect leaders' pay and tenure. Better leaders won't come from more inspirational courses but from changing the incentives they face.

Three, for individuals: take care of yourself and learn to operate in the real world. Understand which behaviors your organization actually rewards; when necessary, adapt to the situation, manage your image and defend your interests as leaders do; and never entrust your career security to anyone's good will.

The Essential Skeleton

The whole book on one line: (1) the leadership industry spent heavily without improving workplaces (evidence: engagement, trust, derailment rates) → (2) because it sells "ought" rather than "is" (five virtues checked against reality: narcissism rewarded, role-playing beating authenticity, lying common and effective, betrayal rarely punished, leaders putting themselves first) → (3) these myths survive because they sell, are told by winners, and feed our romantic urge to credit leaders → (4) so changing workplaces requires accepting the truth and then changing incentives through measurement and accountability, while individuals look after themselves.

What does he prove it with? Three kinds of material: large-scale survey data (engagement, trust, pay ratios); psychology and organizational-behavior research (narcissism and leader emergence, self-monitoring, how often people lie and how poorly they detect lies, the norm of self-interest); and a great many corporate cases. The decisive move is methodological: you cannot infer "is" from "ought," nor change "is" by preaching "ought" ever louder. To change reality you first have to understand what sustains it.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: Pfeffer is endorsing narcissism, lying and betrayal. He isn't. His stance is descriptive: these behaviors are common in practice and often rewarded. He is angry about it, not admiring; the book's whole motive is that workplaces are bad and employees suffer. But readers should notice that his advice to "take care of yourself" and to perform as the situation requires does slide back and forth between description and recommendation.

Misreading two: since virtue doesn't work, forget virtue. What he says is that preaching virtue doesn't work, not that virtue is worthless. He wants good practices backed by institutions, not by talk.

Criticism one: he conflates is and ought himself. Pfeffer faults the industry for treating "should" as "is," yet he often slides from "successful leaders lie and self-promote" to "so you should perform more and look after yourself more," an equally unargued leap. Common is not the same as effective, and effective for personal advancement is not the same as effective for the organization.

Criticism two: selective evidence and anecdote. The book leans heavily on celebrity cases (Jobs, Ellison and others), and celebrity cases are themselves hand-picked; finding a narcissistic successful CEO is as easy as finding a humble one. More systematic evidence paints a finer picture: a 2015 meta-analysis by Emily Grijalva and colleagues found that narcissism predicts becoming a leader but is essentially unrelated to being an effective one, and the relationship is an inverted U: moderate narcissism does best, extreme narcissism hurts. That shows "getting picked" and "doing well" are different things; Pfeffer's account of the first holds, but it doesn't extend to the second.

Criticism three: he underweights research favoring "good" leadership. Large meta-analyses link transformational leadership (moving followers beyond self-interest through vision, inspiration and individual attention) to follower satisfaction and leader effectiveness (Judge & Piccolo, 2004); later meta-analyses also found servant and ethical leadership add explanatory power for employee attitudes and performance (e.g. Hoch et al., 2018, after the book). These studies have flaws, but they suggest "virtue doesn't work" is too hasty a conclusion; the problem may lie more in the gap between preaching and practice than in the virtues themselves.

Criticism four: self-fulfilling cynicism. The management scholar Sumantra Ghoshal's well-known 2005 essay "Bad Management Theories Are Destroying Good Management Practices" argued that when business schools teach students to see people as purely self-interested, the students start acting that way, and the theory becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy. If Pfeffer's "take care of yourself" were widely adopted, it could deepen exactly the low-trust workplace he deplores.

Criticism five: immodesty doesn't pay equally. The psychologist Laurie Rudman found that self-promoting women face a "backlash effect": rated more competent but less likable, and therefore hired less. The behaviors Pfeffer describes as rewarded don't reward everyone symmetrically.

Pfeffer Beyond This Book

Read on its own, this book can make Pfeffer look like a cynic preaching self-interest. Set against his larger body of work, the picture is fuller. His real through-line is that how organizations treat people is a question that can be measured and improved with evidence.

The Human Equation (1998). This earlier book stands squarely on the side of treating employees well: he gathered evidence that "high-commitment" practices (employment security, selective hiring, self-managed teams, high pay tied to performance, extensive training, reduced status differences, information sharing) produce markedly better business performance. So he does believe treating people well works; what he doesn't believe is that leaders will do it because they sat through a seminar.

Hard Facts and The Knowing-Doing Gap (with Sutton). The first argues for evidence-based management; the second shows how companies often know what to do and don't do it, substituting meetings and reports for action. Leadership BS's "ignore the stories, look at the evidence" continues that thread.

Dying for a Paycheck (2018). Here the anger of this book becomes a public-health argument: he and his coauthors estimated that workplace stressors in the U.S. (long hours, lack of health insurance, job insecurity, work-family conflict and the like) account for roughly 120,000 excess deaths and about $190 billion in health spending a year. Bad leadership doesn't just make people unhappy; it measurably damages their health. For his systematic account of how to acquire power, see Power (read972).

Crossovers

  • Philosophy · Hume's is-ought problem: Hume argued you cannot derive how things ought to be from how they are alone. Pfeffer's method is its mirror image: don't pass off "ought" as "is." Alike in demanding the two kinds of claim be kept apart; unlike in that Hume cared whether the inference is valid, Pfeffer about seeing reality before trying to change it.
  • Chinese philosophy · Xunzi's "goodness is artifice": Xunzi held that human nature is bad and goodness is wei, not falsehood but deliberate cultivation shaped by ritual. Alike in seeing good conduct not as a "true self" expressed but as something built by practice and institutions; unlike in that Xunzi trusted ritual to transform people, while Pfeffer has almost no hope sermons will.
  • Evolutionary biology · Trivers on self-deception: Robert Trivers proposed that we deceive ourselves the better to deceive others convincingly. Alike in explaining why the most confident liars are hardest to catch; unlike in that Pfeffer mostly discusses deliberate, strategic lying.
  • Evolutionary biology · Zahavi's handicap principle: Amotz Zahavi argued that only costly, hard-to-fake signals are reliable, like a peacock's tail. Leaders' talk of humility and care is a cheap signal; Pfeffer says watch costly behavior (how pay is split, who goes first in layoffs). Unlike in that nature enforces the cost physiologically, whereas organizations must manufacture it through accountability.
  • Distributed systems · Byzantine fault tolerance: Lamport and colleagues' 1982 "Byzantine Generals Problem" assumes some nodes lie and send contradictory messages, and shows the honest nodes can still agree as long as fewer than a third are traitors. Engineers don't exhort nodes to be honest; they design protocols that work even when some lie. That is Pfeffer's advice for workplaces: don't count on leaders' honesty, build measurement and accountability. Unlike in that nodes aren't swayed by corporate culture; people are.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. The leadership industry spent a fortune and didn't make workplaces better: engagement, trust and managerial failure rates have barely moved in decades.

2. It asks whether participants liked the course, never whether employees' lives improved.

3. The root error is passing off what ought to be as what is; you can't change a reality you refuse to see.

4. Modesty is a virtue in stories and often an invisibility cloak in a promotion race; narcissists are more likely to be chosen to lead.

5. Leaders don't need to "be themselves" but to play the part the situation needs; high self-monitors rise faster.

6. Lying is common, hard to detect and can be self-fulfilling; it costs far less than the ethics lectures claim.

7. Don't ask whether someone is trustworthy; ask whether keeping faith pays for them here.

8. "Leaders eat last" is a slogan; in reality leaders eat first and eat most, so take care of yourself.

9. The myths survive because they sell, are told by winners, and feed our romantic urge to credit leaders for outcomes.

10. Leaders change not through more inspirational courses but when their behavior is measured and they are held accountable; and remember that "common" is not "right," so don't let seeing reality clearly become an excuse to drop your own floor.
