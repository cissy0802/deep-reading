Thus Spoke Zarathustra

Also sprach Zarathustra · Friedrich Nietzsche · 1883–1885

中文 →

In One Sentence

Once the thing that underwrote every value collapses, nobody is left to answer the question of how you ought to live — and this book does exactly one thing from beginning to end: it teaches you to become your own legislator in a world with no meaning stored in the sky, and to love this life enough to want it back, identical, an infinite number of times.

Coordinates

Nietzsche (1844–1900) held a chair in classical philology at Basel at twenty-four, resigned at thirty-five for reasons of health, and spent the following decade writing alone in cheap boarding houses in Italy and Switzerland. He collapsed on a street in Turin in 1889 and never recovered his mind. Zarathustra was written in four parts between 1883 and 1885; he considered it his most important book, and subtitled it "A Book for All and None." Its form deliberately mimics the aphoristic cadence of scripture — a prophet comes down from a mountain to preach — except that what he preaches is the exact inverse of the gospel. This is an anti-gospel. The name in the title is Zoroaster, the ancient Persian prophet who first cast the world as a war between good and evil; Nietzsche brings him back precisely so that he can dismantle, with his own hands, the opposition he invented.

The Central Claims

  • "God is dead" is a diagnosis, not a slogan. It does not say that no deity exists. It says that the transcendent world which once guaranteed truth, morality, and meaning has lost its credibility from inside European culture — and that almost nobody has yet noticed the floor is gone.
  • Man is not a destination but a bridge. Meaning can no longer be collected from above; it has to be made here, on the ground. Nietzsche's name for the human being who can make it is the Übermensch (overman). Its opposite is not a villain but the last man — the one who no longer wants anything, only comfort.
  • What drives life is neither survival nor pleasure but growth and self-overcoming — Nietzsche's will to power (Wille zur Macht). And how far a person can affirm life is measured by a single test: eternal recurrence — if this life had to return exactly as it was, endlessly, could you bear it?

The Core Concepts, One by One

God Is Dead: not an announcement but an obituary

The famous line does not first appear here but in The Gay Science §125: a madman runs into the marketplace at noon with a lantern, shouting "I seek God!" The unbelievers around him laugh. He turns on them and says it — "God is dead. And we have killed him, you and I." Then come the questions that matter (paraphrased): how did we drink up the sea? Who gave us the sponge to wipe away the entire horizon? What did we do when we unchained this earth from its sun? Is there still any up or down? Is empty space not breathing on us? Finally he throws his lantern to the ground: "I have come too early. This deed is still on its way." In Zarathustra the same diagnosis arrives quietly: Zarathustra descends, meets an old hermit praising God in the forest, and once alone says to himself — "Could it be possible? This old saint has not yet heard in his forest that God is dead."

Notice the tone: the madman is horrified, not triumphant. Nietzsche is not celebrating atheism — late nineteenth-century European intellectual life was already thick with atheists, and announcing it would have been news to nobody. His point is aimed at the people who dropped God and carried on as before: they threw out the guarantor and kept the whole furnished house — objective morality, human dignity, historical progress, the conviction that truth is worth having. The load-bearing wall is gone. The ceiling simply hasn't come down yet.

The deeper move: God was not killed by science. Christianity killed him. Two thousand years of Christian discipline trained Europeans into an absolute honesty — bear no false witness, do not deceive yourself, prefer truth to comfort. That bred-in-the-bone will to truth eventually turned its guns around, began demanding evidence for faith itself, and severed its own root. Christian morality executed the Christian God. Which is why the damage cannot be repaired by believing again: what stopped people believing was the honesty that belief itself cultivated.

What follows is nihilism, and Nietzsche's definition is precise: the devaluation of the highest values by themselves. Nobody defeated them; they lost their force internally, like a cheque drawn on a bank that still exists but no longer pays. It comes in two forms. Passive nihilism is exhaustion — nothing matters, so retreat into numbness, distraction, or a substitute faith (nationalism, the cult of science, anything that will still tell you what you ought to do). Active nihilism treats the failure of the old values as a cleared site, the precondition for making new ones. The whole book is about how to get from the first to the second.

What this changes in how you see things: it hands you a knife for your own convictions. Everyone is equal; life has intrinsic worth; people must never be used merely as means. You probably hold these as self-evident. Nietzsche's question is: did you actually argue your way to them, or did you inherit them as furniture from a church you no longer attend? He is not telling you to throw the furniture out. He is telling you to admit you never paid for it.

The Last Man: the real antagonist of this book

Zarathustra's first public speech is the most important passage in the book and the least quoted. He preaches the overman in the marketplace; the crowd laughs, assuming he is the warm-up act for the tightrope walker. He fails. So he tries a different tack and describes what he calls the most contemptible thing — the last man:

He is like this (paraphrased). He has left the regions where life was hard, for he needs warmth. He still works, because work is entertainment — but he takes care that the entertainment does not tire him. He no longer becomes poor or rich: both are too burdensome. Nobody wants to rule any more, and nobody wants to obey: both are too burdensome. "The earth has become small, and on it hops the last man, who makes everything small." He has his little pleasure for the day and his little pleasure for the night — but he has a high regard for health. "We have invented happiness," say the last men, and they blink.

Then comes the cruellest sentence Nietzsche ever wrote: the crowd hears him out and cheers — "Give us this last man, O Zarathustra! You can keep your overman!" The entire marketplace votes, on the spot, for the future he has just described with contempt.

The mechanism is what gives the passage its force. The last man is not the product of moral decay. He is the product of every good thing carried to its conclusion: safety, comfort, equality, health, the elimination of risk. He has done nothing wrong. His single defect is that he no longer wants anything. In Nietzsche's image: "Alas, the time is coming when man will no longer shoot the arrow of his longing beyond man, and the string of his bow will have forgotten how to whir." The last man is nihilism in its most comfortable form — he never has to face the death of God, because he stopped asking that kind of question long ago.

The blink is the whole point. To blink is to share an open secret: we both know this isn't real happiness, but let's not say so — saying so spoils it. The last man is not a dupe. He is a collaborator.

Why the concept has only got sharper: Nietzsche described this person in 1883, before recommendation algorithms and sleep scores on wearables. The checklist has since been filled in almost line by line: risk outsourced to insurance and institutions, longing downgraded to entertainment, health itself revered as the highest value, the little pleasure for the day and the little pleasure for the night spent inside an endless feed. The passage is uncomfortable not because it is unkind but because it is accurate. (Honesty requires adding: this is a cultural judgement, not a testable proposition. Nietzsche offered no evidence — and could offer none — that comfort must produce spiritual atrophy.)

The Overman and "the meaning of the earth": a bridge, not a destination

Half the wreckage around Übermensch comes from translation. "Superman" suggests a comic-book character or a biological super-breed. Nietzsche means something else entirely: a human being who creates values rather than collecting them from an authority. His only definition, given in the prologue, is one line: "The overman is the meaning of the earth. I beseech you, my brothers, remain faithful to the earth, and do not believe those who speak to you of otherworldly hopes."

"Faithful to the earth" is the ethical core of the book. Nietzsche has a name for the two-thousand-year-old manoeuvre he is refusing — backworldsmanship (Hinterweltlerei): placing the "real world" behind or above this one. Plato's forms, the Christian heaven, and their secular heirs (the end of history, the society after the revolution, life after retirement). Their shared structure is this: an elsewhere is invoked to issue a receipt for the suffering of here. And the people who invent these elsewheres, Nietzsche notes, are typically those who suffer here — the sick, the tired. It is a metaphysics of the exhausted. To be faithful to the earth is to refuse the receipt: no elsewhere is permitted to redeem this place.

The companion move is his rehabilitation of the body. In "On the Despisers of the Body" (paraphrased): "The body is a great reason… what you call 'spirit,' my brother, is also an instrument of your body, a little instrument of your great reason." To cut a person into a noble soul and a base flesh is backworldsmanship applied to anatomy; what thinks, evaluates, and desires is the whole organism.

So what does the overman actually look like? Nietzsche almost never says — and that is not an oversight but a necessity. Any specification he issued would instantly become a new "thou shalt," a new idol, when the whole definition of the overman is self-legislation. What he offers instead is an image, the most famous in the book:

"Man is a rope, tied between beast and overman — a rope over an abyss. A dangerous crossing, a dangerous wayfaring, a dangerous looking-back, a dangerous trembling and stopping. What is great in man is that he is a bridge and not an end; what can be loved in man is that he is an Übergang (a going-over) and an Untergang (a going-under)."

Every detail is doing work. The abyss below is nihilism. "A dangerous looking-back" is the wish to retreat into the age that still had a God. "A dangerous stopping" is the last man. And the rhyme in the final clause carries the argument: Übergang and Untergang. To reach the far side, the self you are now has to go under. Anyone thinking "I shall become an overman" has already missed it — it is not an identity but the movement of willingly destroying the self you currently are, again and again.

The Three Metamorphoses: camel, lion, child

Part One, section one — the most usable page in the book.

The sequence cannot be skipped: a lion that never carried a load is empty, and anyone who stops at the lion stays defined by what he opposes.
StageWhat it doesIts wordWhy it cannot stop there
CamelBears weight. It kneels and asks to be loaded: tradition, discipline, hard learning, other people's values — then carries them into the desert"Thou shalt"It reveres everything, so it can evaluate nothing. The strength is real, but the strength is still someone else's

The necessity is in the order. Why the camel first? Because creating values takes strength, craft, and content, and those grow only out of carrying a tradition — the No of someone who has never seriously learned anything is hollow. Nietzsche is his own evidence: he could take Western morality apart because he had first spent a decade doing the drudgery of classical philology. Why not stop at the lion? Because negation is parasitic. Define yourself as against tradition and you no longer know who you are once it disappears; what you have is freedom from, never freedom to. Why does it end with a child? The key word is forgetting. The child no longer has to remember what it is against, so its action comes from itself — "a wheel rolling out of its own centre" — rather than being pushed.

The practical value of the model is that it locates you. Crushed under a pile of shoulds, living as though repaying a debt: you are the camel, and the way out is not to carry more but to learn to refuse. Having demolished everything, believing nothing, living on irony, and producing nothing: you are stuck at the lion — the most crowded stop of our era, and the one most often mistaken for the destination. Doing quietly what you take to be worth doing, without needing an opponent to supply your power: that is the child.

Will to Power: not wanting to win, wanting to grow

In this book the idea appears under the name self-overcoming: "Wherever I found the living, there I found will to power; and even in the will of the servant I found the will to be master." And the sharper line: "Life itself confided this secret to me: 'Behold,' it said, 'I am that which must always overcome itself.'"

The word "power" (Macht) has caused more damage than "overman." It does not mean domination over others; it means the exercise and increase of force, the capacity to overcome resistance. A plant cracking concrete to reach light, a lifter adding five kilos next week, a composer wrestling three months with an orchestration — all will to power. Domination is one of its poorest expressions: it is precisely those who cannot discharge force anywhere else who end up discharging it on other people.

It is meant to displace two more popular answers. Schopenhauer's will to live (the basic drive is to survive): if staying alive were the supreme principle, there would be no explaining why organisms routinely risk death in order to become stronger — exposing themselves to court, fighting over territory, climbing a mountain. And utilitarian pleasure-seeking: pleasure is not the goal but the signal emitted when resistance is overcome. A creature genuinely maximising pleasure would lie still — which is the last man.

What it changes: it reclassifies suffering. If pleasure is the goal, resistance and want are evils to be removed. If growth is the goal, resistance is the only raw material growth has — a life with no resistance in it is not happiness but atrophy. Nietzsche later compressed this into the line from Twilight of the Idols: "What does not kill me makes me stronger." (Motivational-poster culture has flattened it beyond recognition, but that mechanism is what it originally meant.)

One honest label is required: the fat volume sold as The Will to Power is not a book Nietzsche wrote. It was assembled after his collapse by his sister Elisabeth, who selected, ordered, and stitched together unpublished notes. So any account presenting "will to power" as a finished doctrine deserves a discount: in the books he actually published, it functions as a recurring explanatory hypothesis, not a completed system.

Eternal Recurrence: the greatest weight

Nietzsche called this his "most abysmal thought" and the fundamental conception of the book. Its fullest statement is again in The Gay Science (§341, titled "The Greatest Weight"), roughly:

Suppose one day, in your loneliest loneliness, a demon crept after you and said: "This life as you now live it and have lived it you will have to live once more and innumerable times more; and there will be nothing new in it, but every pain and every joy and every thought and sigh and everything unspeakably small or great must return to you, all in the same succession and sequence — even this spider and this moonlight between the trees, and even this moment and I myself." Would you throw yourself down and gnash your teeth and curse him? Or have you once experienced a tremendous moment when you would have answered him: "You are a god, and never have I heard anything more divine"?

In the book itself it arrives in two scenes. The first is "On the Vision and the Riddle." Zarathustra climbs carrying a dwarf on his back — "the spirit of gravity," his own heaviness and disgust — and stops at a gateway inscribed Moment, where two infinite paths, one running backwards and one forwards, meet. His question: if both directions are infinite, everything that can happen must already have happened — so this very instant must have been here countless times before and must return countless times again. The dwarf reduces it, contemptuously, to "all truth is crooked; time itself is a circle" — and Zarathustra rounds on him in fury: "Spirit of gravity, do not make it too easy for yourself!" This matters: turning eternal recurrence into an amusing cosmological proposition is itself a way of dodging it.

The second scene follows immediately. A young shepherd lies on the ground with a black snake down his throat, biting fast. Zarathustra pulls and cannot free him, then shouts: "Bite! Bite its head off!" The shepherd bites, spits out the head, and springs up — "no longer shepherd, no longer human — one transformed, surrounded with light, laughing!" The reading is not obscure: the snake is the thought itself in its nauseating aspect — every humiliation, every failure, every pointless pain, returning unaltered forever. No one can pull it out for you. You bite it off yourself. The laughter afterwards is the strongest image of affirmation the book has.

Why is this the terminus rather than an eccentric cosmological guess? Because it seals off, in one move, every strategy that redeems the present with the future. If time is a circle there is no end point, no last judgement, no destination of history, no "one day it will all have been worth it." No moment can serve as a means to somewhere else; each one has to stand on its own. That is how it locks into the death of God: God's death removed the meaning stored above, and eternal recurrence removes the future as the emergency exit, driving the entire weight back into now.

The harder half is in "On Redemption." The will has one fundamental impotence: it cannot will backwards; it is powerless against every "it was" (Es war). Irreversible time is the stone the will cannot roll. And that impotence ferments into a specific poison — "This, indeed this alone, is what revenge itself is: the will's ill will toward time and its 'it was.'" Unable to change the past, we punish instead: ourselves, others, existence as such. The entire metaphysics of guilt and punishment, and what Nietzsche calls ressentiment — the resentment that, unable to strike back, turns inward and rewrites the table of values to demean the strong — is rooted here. The only antidote is to transform "it was" into "thus I willed it" — not pretending the past was lovely, but claiming it into the one life you affirm. This is what he would later name amor fati: not merely enduring necessity but loving it.

What it changes: it converts "how has my life gone" from a ledger you settle at the end into a test available at any moment — this, identically, endlessly: do you want it? The question is a live vote on everything you are currently doing. This job, this relationship, these hours tonight: would you have them eternally? It does not need to be physically true to reorganise what a person chooses.

The Argument, Distilled

The form is a prose poem; the logic is a straight line in six steps.

  • 1. Diagnosis. God is dead — not an atheist manifesto but the collapse of transcendent guarantee, brought about by the will to truth that Christianity itself cultivated. The consequence is nihilism: the highest values devalue themselves.
  • 2. Identify the real danger. Not wickedness but the last man — nihilism lived comfortably, the arrow of longing no longer fired at any star, and a blink to seal the deal. Crowds choose this willingly.
  • 3. Set a direction. The overman is the meaning of the earth. Meaning can no longer be collected elsewhere, so it must be made here; be faithful to the earth and refuse every "backworld." Man is a rope, a bridge, a going-over — not an end.
  • 4. Give the path. The three metamorphoses: camel bears, lion refuses, child creates. No stage can be skipped and none can be the last stop but the third.
  • 5. Supply the dynamics. Will to power as self-overcoming. The basic drive is neither survival nor pleasure but growth — so resistance is not the enemy of happiness but the raw material of growth.
  • 6. Apply the final test. Eternal recurrence closes the future as an escape route and forces the full weight into this instant; its counterpart is rewriting "it was" into "thus I willed it," which dissolves the spirit of revenge. Passing the test is amor fati.
  • What does it actually establish? Honestly: it does not prove, it persuades. There is not one rigorous argument in the book — no premises, inferences, or rebuttals; only images, aphorisms, parody, and rhythm. What genuinely stands is the diagnosis: what happens to a culture once its values lose their foundation, which reads a century and a half later with unpleasant accuracy. What does not stand is the prescription: the content of the overman is left blank, eternal recurrence was never established as cosmology, and will to power as a universal principle is a hypothesis. Read it as an exceptionally sharp cultural diagnosis and a set of self-tests — not as a programme you can follow.

Misreadings & Serious Objections

  • The big one: Nazi appropriation. The chain of facts is clear. Nietzsche's sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche was a fervent German nationalist and antisemite (her husband was an antisemitic agitator; the pair founded a "pure Aryan" colony in Paraguay). After his collapse she took control of the manuscripts, manufactured The Will to Power, and ran the Nietzsche Archive as an institution courting the Nazi regime. Nietzsche himself, in the books he published, attacks antisemites and German nationalism repeatedly (one reason for his break with Wagner), and this book contains the line: "State is the name of the coldest of all cold monsters. Coldly it lies, and this lie crawls out of its mouth: 'I, the state, am the people.'" Reading it as a totalitarian handbook is textually indefensible.
  • But do not over-launder him. Nietzsche was no Nazi, yet he did supply material that could be appropriated: he openly advocates hierarchy, disdains "herd morality" and pity, mocks demands for equality (he calls preachers of equality "tarantulas of revenge," claiming their true motor is resentment dressed as justice), and uses a vocabulary of "breeding" that later proved catastrophically easy to twist. Saying he was misread is correct; saying he was innocent is protecting him.
  • The overman is not biological, and not the "strong man" of self-help. It has nothing to do with race, physique, wealth, or rank — Nietzsche's nearest approximations are creators like Goethe and Beethoven, not conquerors. Reading it as "get to the top" is the most last-man reading available, since it still chases a value awarded by someone else's ranking.
  • "God is dead" is not a victory cry. The speaker is a madman, the register is dread, and he adds that he has come too early. Using it as a quip against believers turns an earthquake into a wisecrack.
  • The strongest formal objection: the aphoristic style makes the book unfalsifiable and untestable. With no arguments there are no premises to attack, and the poetic surface lets any reader cut out the Nietzsche they wanted — existentialists, postmodernists, fascists, startup founders, all with chapter and verse. A text in which everyone can prove themselves right pays for that in rigour.
  • The cosmological argument for eternal recurrence fails. In unpublished notes Nietzsche tried to prove it physically (finite quantity of force, infinite time, therefore every configuration must recur). Finite energy does not entail a finite number of states, and infinite recurrence does not entail recurrence in the same sequence. Most scholars — and the book's own handling of it — take it as an ethical test rather than a physical claim; that does not weaken it, but the difference matters.
  • His remarks on women do not survive scrutiny. The notorious line from "On Little Old and Young Women" — "You are going to women? Do not forget the whip!" — is spoken by an old woman, and scholars often invoke that irony in his defence. But a contextual defence covers one sentence; it does not cover the disparagement of women recurring across several of his books. This is a real and now-discarded part of his thought, and it need not be explained away.
  • The most substantive philosophical objection: he does not understand fragility. Critics in the line of Martha Nussbaum argue that his contempt for pity presupposes that suffering can always be converted into strength — which ignores the suffering that does not strengthen anyone and simply crushes them: hunger, chronic illness, destitution, bereavement. "What does not kill me makes me stronger" holds in a study and fails in a refugee camp; underrating material deprivation and bodily vulnerability leaves his ethics inoperative exactly where ethics is needed most.
  • The least-mentioned point, and Nietzsche planted it himself: Zarathustra is also the target of the satire. He fails in the marketplace; he descends and retreats to his cave over and over; his animals turn his abysmal thought into a catchy jingle and he snaps that they have made it a hurdy-gurdy song; in Part Four he hosts a group of self-declared disciples, the "higher men," who promptly start worshipping a donkey. The book keeps ironising the prophet it invented — so anyone reading it as scripture is reading it as exactly the thing it most wants to demolish.

Ten Sentences

1. "God is dead. And we have killed him, you and I." The man who says it is horrified, not celebrating. The claim is not that no deity exists but that the guarantor of truth and morality has collapsed — while we go on using the furniture it held up.

2. God was killed by Christianity, not science: two thousand years of training in absolute honesty produced a will to truth that eventually turned on faith itself. Which is why believing again is not an available road back.

3. Nihilism has a precise definition — the devaluation of the highest values by themselves. Nobody defeated them; they became a cheque drawn on a bank that still exists but no longer pays.

4. The antagonist of this book is not a villain but the last man: he has invented happiness and blinks about it, he has his little pleasure for the day and his little pleasure for the night, he reveres health, and he no longer shoots the arrow of longing at any star. The cruellest stroke: the crowd cheers and asks to be turned into him.

5. "The overman is the meaning of the earth. Remain faithful to the earth, and do not believe those who speak to you of otherworldly hopes." Every elsewhere — heaven, the end of history, life after retirement — is a receipt issued for the suffering of here, and Nietzsche refuses to accept it.

6. "Man is a rope tied between beast and overman — a rope over an abyss. What can be loved in man is that he is a going-over and a going-under." The overman is not a status you reach but the willingness to let the self you are now go under, repeatedly.

7. The three metamorphoses cannot be skipped: the camel takes up "thou shalt," the lion roars No at the dragon, the child says the sacred Yes. A lion that never carried a load is empty; anyone who stops at the lion stays defined by what he opposes.

8. "Wherever I found the living, there I found will to power." Not domination but the overcoming of resistance; domination is what those who cannot discharge force elsewhere resort to. Which reclassifies suffering: resistance is not the enemy of happiness but the only raw material growth has.

9. The greatest weight: this life again, identically, endlessly — every pain, every humiliation, in the same order. Do you curse, or do you answer "you are a god"? It closes the future as an escape route and forces the whole weight into now; it need not be true to change what you do tonight.

10. The will cannot roll the stone called "it was," and that impotence ferments into revenge — against yourself, against others, against existence. The only antidote: transform "it was" into "thus I willed it" — not pretending the past was good, but claiming it into the one life you affirm. That is amor fati.
