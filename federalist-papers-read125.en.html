The Federalist Papers

The Federalist Papers · Alexander Hamilton, James Madison & John Jay (writing as "Publius") · 1787–1788

中文 →

In One Sentence

Don't count on the people in power being good. Treat ambition, self-interest and faction as design constraints, make power check power and interest cancel interest, and a government run by flawed people can go a long time without turning on its citizens. The Federalist is the design document for that piece of political engineering, written in the months while the draft U.S. Constitution waited to be ratified.

Where It Sits

In 1787 the Philadelphia Convention produced a draft constitution that needed nine of the thirteen states to ratify it. New York was the loudest holdout, so three men took the pen name of Publius (after Publius Valerius Publicola, a founder of the Roman Republic) and ran 85 essays in New York newspapers: roughly 51 by Hamilton, 29 by Madison and 5 by Jay. It began as a set of op-eds written to win a vote, and ended up as the most authoritative "original manual" for the U.S. Constitution and a classic of Western political theory.

Core Claims

  • Human nature can't be changed, only arranged. Ambition, self-interest and party spirit come with being human. Abolishing them would mean abolishing liberty; the workable path is to set them against each other.
  • A large republic is safer than a small one. Against the received wisdom (Montesquieu: republics only work in small states), the bigger the territory and the more varied its interests, the harder it is for any single faction to assemble a majority that can oppress everyone else.
  • Good government must be strong and restrained at once. Under the Articles of Confederation the central government was too weak to tax or keep its treaties. The new one needed real power, and that power had to be split up and made to watch itself: "you must first enable the government to control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself" (No. 51).

Put the three together: where you can't rely on virtue, substitute structure for it.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Faction: you can't remove the cause, so you control the effects (No. 10)

What it is. Madison defines a faction precisely: a number of citizens, whether a majority or a minority of the whole, united by some common passion or interest that is adverse to the rights of other citizens or to the permanent and aggregate interests of the community. Two points matter. A faction isn't necessarily a conspiracy of the few; a majority can be a faction too. And the test isn't numbers, it's whether the group is trampling someone else's rights or the common good.

The mechanism: diagnose, then pick a treatment. There are only two ways to deal with faction, Madison says: remove its causes or control its effects. Removing the causes could be done in two ways. One is to destroy liberty, and here he gives the famous image: "Liberty is to faction what air is to fire." Take away the air and the fire dies, but you wouldn't abolish air to prevent fires, because air is what keeps animals alive. The other is to give everyone the same opinions, passions and interests, which can't be done. As long as human reason can err and is free to operate, opinions will differ, and as long as people's abilities differ, property will be unequal. Hence his conclusion: "The latent causes of faction are thus sown in the nature of man." And the most common and durable source of faction has always been the various and unequal distribution of property: those who own and those who don't, creditors and debtors, landed, mercantile and manufacturing interests.

If the cause can't be removed, only the effects are left to control. A minority faction is easy: in a republic the majority simply outvotes it. The real danger is a majority faction. In a "pure democracy," where citizens assemble and decide everything directly, once a majority shares a passion (cancel the debts, divide the land) nothing stops it from sacrificing the minority. To Madison, that is why the ancient Greek city-states were so turbulent and short-lived.

Two remedies: representation and size. First, representation passes public opinion through a smaller elected body that may "refine and enlarge the public views." He isn't naive about it; in the next breath he admits the process could just as easily elect schemers. Second, and the most original move in the essay: extend the sphere. "Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens; or if such a common motive exists, it will be more difficult for all who feel it to discover their own strength, and to act in unison with each other."

An example. In a single county where seventy percent of voters are indebted farmers, it's easy to agree in the tavern on a legislature that will pass a paper-money act and legally eat their creditors' property. In the 1780s this was actually happening in states like Rhode Island, and Shays' Rebellion in Massachusetts (1786–87) went as far as armed resistance to taxes and debt collection. Scale the same question up to thirteen states, though, and the interests of southern planters, northern merchants, western settlers, fishermen and artisans all cut across each other. No single passion easily commands a national majority, and even if it did, coordinating across hundreds of miles would be hard.

An analogy. It works like a portfolio. Put everything on one stock and its crash wipes you out; spread across hundreds of uncorrelated assets and no single crash can sink the whole. Madison applied diversification to political passion. The historian Douglass Adair later showed that the idea owed a good deal to David Hume's essay "Idea of a Perfect Commonwealth," which had argued that a large state suits a republic better because its distant parties find it hard to combine.

Why it matters. For two thousand years, from Aristotle to Montesquieu, the consensus was that republics had to be small, since they depended on civic virtue and sameness and would fall apart if they grew. No. 10 turns that around: diversity isn't the enemy of a republic, it's the republic's insurance.

How it changes the way you see. When you look at a society where everyone is quarreling and interests are splintered, your first thought may be that the place is ungovernable. Madison would say that's exactly why minorities are safe there: no single bloc can dominate. What should alarm you is the moment everyone collapses into one identity with one enemy.

2. "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition": auxiliary precautions (No. 51)

What it is. This is the most quoted passage in the book, and nearly every line is worth knowing by heart: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary. If angels were to govern men, neither external nor internal controls on government would be necessary. In framing a government which is to be administered by men over men, the great difficulty lies in this: you must first enable the government to control the governed; and in the next place oblige it to control itself." Depending on the people through elections is the primary control on government, "but experience has taught mankind the necessity of auxiliary precautions."

The mechanism: weld private interest to the office. In Madison's words: "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition. The interest of the man must be connected with the constitutional rights of the place." Don't expect legislators, presidents or judges to defend the separation of powers out of public spirit. Arrange things so they defend it out of self-interest. A president who wants to keep his own power will resist congressional overreach; senators protective of their standing will scrutinize presidential nominees. Each branch needs "a will of its own" and should depend as little as possible on the others for appointment and pay, which is why judges serve during good behavior and their salaries can't be cut while in office. He calls it the policy of "supplying, by opposite and rival interests, the defect of better motives." You don't need good people, only mutually suspicious people standing in the right places.

An example. The Constitution is full of these gears. Congress legislates and the president can veto; a two-thirds vote of Congress can override. The president nominates ministers and judges, but only with the Senate's "advice and consent." He commands the army, but only Congress can declare war and fund it. He nominates judges, but once they're seated neither he nor Congress can remove them for an unwelcome ruling. Every time, one branch's power is carved from the edge of another's.

An analogy. It's the old cake rule: one person cuts, the other chooses first. The cutter needn't be a saint. Afraid of being left with the small piece, he'll cut as evenly as he can. Institutional design doesn't change the person holding the knife; it changes his incentives.

Why it matters. It turns politics from a moral question (how do we raise good rulers?) into an engineering one (how do we arrange interests?). It doesn't deny that virtue exists; it refuses to bet the government's safety on it.

How it changes the way you see. When an organization goes wrong, you ask less "who's the villain?" and more "where does the private interest of whoever holds this seat point?" The test of a good institution: put a more selfish person in the chair, and does it still work?

3. Separation isn't isolation: why "parchment barriers" fail (Nos. 47–48)

What it is. Opponents attacked the draft for mixing the branches up. The president could veto laws, the Senate shared in appointments and treaties: wasn't that a violation of Montesquieu's separation of powers? Madison grants the principle first: "The accumulation of all powers, legislative, executive, and judiciary, in the same hands … may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny." Then he rereads Montesquieu, "the oracle who is always consulted" on the subject. The British constitution Montesquieu admired had its branches thoroughly intertwined. What he really meant, Madison argues, was only that the whole power of one department shouldn't be exercised by the same hands that hold the whole power of another.

The mechanism: lines on paper get swallowed by the strong. No. 48's key judgment is that simply writing "no branch shall encroach" into a constitution amounts to "parchment barriers": words on parchment don't hold back real ambitions. In a republic the most dangerous drive comes from the legislature. It sits closest to popular passion, holds the purse, and its powers are the least precisely bounded, so it is "everywhere extending the sphere of its activity, and drawing all power into its impetuous vortex." He points to Virginia and Pennsylvania, where state legislatures had repeatedly invaded executive and judicial business and the separation clauses on paper meant nothing.

So the remedy is, precisely, incomplete separation. Give each branch some "constitutional means" against the others so it can push back on the spot. The legislature is strongest, so split it in two (a House and a Senate, chosen differently and serving different terms) and give the executive a veto. Branches kept completely apart end up swallowed by the strongest; branches that bite into each other can hold a balance.

An analogy. If a property line exists only on a map, the neighbor's fence soon creeps over it. You need someone standing on the line with tools in hand. Or think of an arch: each stone presses on its neighbors, and that mutual pressure, not keeping the stones apart, is what holds the arch up.

How it changes the way you see. When you read a rule that says "Department X shall not interfere in Y," you'll ask: if X does interfere, who has the motive and the means to shove it back right away? If nobody does, the rule is parchment.

4. The compound republic: law that reaches individuals, plus a "double security" (Nos. 15, 39, 51)

What it is. The first half of the collection, mostly Hamilton's, argues that the Articles of Confederation had to go. Its "great and radical vice," he says, was legislating for states as collective bodies rather than for the individuals who make them up (No. 15). Congress could requisition money and troops from the states, but when a state refused, Congress had no remedy except to make war on it, which is to say civil war. So the revolutionary debts went unpaid, foreign treaties went unhonored, states put up tariffs against each other, and the union was, in practice, a suggestion.

The mechanism: from a league to a government. The Constitution's revolutionary step was that federal law reaches every citizen directly. The federal government collects its own taxes, runs its own courts and enforces its own laws without going through the states. That turns an alliance of states into an actual government. It also raises a new fear: won't the center devour the states? Madison's answer in No. 39 is carefully balanced. The new system is "neither wholly national nor wholly federal." The Senate gives each state equal votes (federal), the House is apportioned by population (national), amendments need the states' approval (federal), and laws operate on individuals (national). No. 45 adds that the powers delegated to the federal government are "few and defined," while those left to the states are "numerous and indefinite."

Double security. In No. 51 Madison combines the vertical division (federalism) with the horizontal one (separation of powers) and calls the result a "compound republic." Power surrendered by the people is first divided between two levels of government, then subdivided among separate branches within each. "Hence a double security arises to the rights of the people. The different governments will control each other, at the same time that each will be controlled by itself."

An analogy. Picture a franchise association, where headquarters can only ask franchisees to pay their dues, turning into a corporate group that can pay staff and set rules directly. To reassure the regional offices, the charter spells out that headquarters handles finance, brand and external relations, everything else stays local, and every region has a seat on the board.

How it changes the way you see. "Centralize or decentralize" isn't a single slider. You can allocate it issue by issue: which level should own a task depends on how far its effects spill over and who can actually carry it out. Defense and currency need to be unified; schools and policing can stay local.

5. An energetic executive and the "least dangerous" judiciary (Nos. 70, 78)

The executive needs energy. Hamilton opens No. 70 bluntly: "Energy in the executive is a leading character in the definition of good government." Without it you can't repel foreign attack, enforce the laws steadily, or protect property against riot and faction. Its ingredients are unity (one president, not a council), duration (a term long enough to accomplish something), adequate provision for its support, and competent powers. His strongest argument is about accountability. In a plural executive, everyone blames everyone else when things go wrong, so plurality "tends to conceal faults and destroy responsibility" and the public never knows whom to hold to account. With one person in charge, credit and blame are plain to see, and that visibility is itself a check.

The judiciary is the "least dangerous" branch. No. 78 is just as neat. The executive holds the sword and the legislature holds the purse, while the judiciary has "neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment," and needs the executive even to enforce its own rulings. Being weakest, it needs the most protection, so judges hold office during good behavior and needn't fear offending the powerful. Then comes the far-reaching corollary. The Constitution is the fundamental law expressing the will of the people, while statutes express only the will of the people's agents; when the two conflict, courts must follow the Constitution over the statute. That is the theoretical basis of judicial review (a court's power to void an unconstitutional law), which Marbury v. Madison formally established in 1803.

An analogy. The executive is a ship's captain: five captains voting on when to turn the wheel won't do, and when something goes wrong one person has to answer for it. The judiciary is the referee. It has no ball and can't play, but its whistle has to be respected, and that only works if nobody can fire the referee for an unwelcome call.

How it changes the way you see. These two papers remind you that limiting power isn't the same as weakening it. A government too weak to keep its promises threatens liberty too, just through riot, default and disorder instead. Good design asks two questions together. Does this office have enough power to get things done? And when it gets them wrong, can we find who's responsible and correct it?

The Spine of the Argument

The 85 papers compress into one chain:

  • Step one: the status quo can't last. Under the Articles the "league" can't tax, keep treaties or stop the states from preying on each other. Left alone, it ends either in rival confederacies or in foreign powers picking the states off one by one (Nos. 1–36). So the country needs a national government with real power that acts directly on individuals.
  • Step two: but power will be abused. Men aren't angels, faction can't be eliminated, and officeholders expand their reach (Nos. 10, 47–51).
  • Step three: so rely on structure, not virtue. Dilute majority factions in a large republic, filter opinion through representation, build a double security out of two levels of government and interlocking branches, and let ambition in each office counter ambition in the others (Nos. 37–51).
  • Step four: test each institution. House, Senate, presidency and courts are defended one at a time against the twin standard of "strong enough and checked enough" (Nos. 52–83).
  • Conclusion: No. 1 states the stakes at the very start. This generation must decide whether societies of men are really capable of establishing good government "from reflection and choice," or are forever destined to depend for their political constitutions "on accident and force."

What the book establishes, then, isn't that the Constitution is perfect. It's a more general claim: an institution is only as good as the way it runs in the hands of the worst people who might hold it.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: it's a detached work of political philosophy. It's first of all campaign literature, serialized in newspapers, rushed out to answer opponents, written to win New York's ratification, and sometimes careful to play down the Constitution's more worrying features. Its actual effect on the New York vote may have been modest: the state squeaked through 30 to 27 in July 1788, by which time ten states had already ratified. Read it remembering who's speaking: an advocate pleading a client's case, not a judge deciding one.

Misreading 2: it argues for small government. The opposite. Hamilton's half is a defense of a far stronger central government than the country then had: powers connected with defense "ought to exist without limitation" (No. 23), and the taxing power has to be complete. The book restrains power, but only after creating enough of it.

Misreading 3: it's thoroughly cynical about human nature. In No. 55 Madison says outright that there is a degree of depravity in mankind requiring circumspection and distrust, but also qualities in human nature that justify a portion of esteem and confidence, and republican government presupposes those qualities more than any other form does. The stance is "don't bet on virtue," not "virtue doesn't exist"; voters still need enough sense to choose decent representatives. The same paper has the famous line: "Had every Athenian citizen been a Socrates, every Athenian assembly would still have been a mob." The problem is numbers and passion, not individual character.

Misreading 4: the authors spoke with one mind. Madison and Hamilton parted ways within a few years. As Treasury Secretary, Hamilton pushed a national bank and broader federal power; Madison allied with Jefferson against him, and the two ended up leading rival parties. "Publius" was a temporary alliance, not a single mind. Even who wrote which paper was argued over for more than a century. Twelve were disputed until 1964, when the statisticians Frederick Mosteller and David Wallace used the frequencies of function words (Hamilton used "upon" constantly and Madison almost never; Madison wrote "whilst") to make a strong statistical case that Madison wrote them.

Criticism 1: silence on slavery, and complicity. No. 54 defends the three-fifths clause, which counted each enslaved person as three-fifths of a person for apportioning seats, with Madison speaking through "one of our Southern brethren" to argue that slaves are both persons and property. A collection built around protecting minority rights from majority power barely confronts the largest denial of rights of its day. Madison himself was a slaveholder.

Criticism 2: whose rights are being protected? In An Economic Interpretation of the Constitution (1913), the historian Charles Beard seized on No. 10's line about unequal property being the most durable source of faction. He argued that the Constitution was essentially a project of bondholders and commercial elites protecting their assets from a debtor majority. Forrest McDonald and others later knocked down much of Beard's specific evidence, but the point that the counter-majoritarian design tilts toward protecting existing property remains a serious criticism.

Criticism 3: Madison's logic doesn't hold up. In A Preface to Democratic Theory (1956), the political scientist Robert Dahl argued that the key terms of "Madisonian democracy" (faction, tyranny, rights) are loosely defined, and that a system built to prevent majority tyranny often ends up handing a veto to well-organized minorities, for example a bloc able to obstruct business in the Senate. The more checks there are, the harder the status quo is to change, and the status quo may itself be unjust.

Criticism 4: the large-republic premise has expired. Extending the sphere worked partly because like-minded people found it hard "to discover their own strength" and act together. National newspapers, broadcasting and social media have all but erased that premise. One passion can now be coordinated nationwide overnight, and the two major parties look much more like durable national factions than Madison imagined. The Anti-Federalists warned of this at the time. "Brutus," usually thought to be Robert Yates of New York, argued that a large republic would put representatives out of voters' reach and slide toward aristocracy. Their insistence won the first ten amendments, the Bill of Rights, even though Hamilton had called a bill of rights "not only unnecessary … but would even be dangerous" in No. 84, reasoning that listing rights the government can't violate implies it has powers nobody listed. On that point, history sided with his opponents.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · Byzantine fault tolerance: The "Byzantine Generals" problem (Lamport and colleagues, 1982) designs systems that don't assume every node is honest. As long as fewer than a third are traitors, the honest nodes still reach agreement. It's the same engineering stance as "if men were angels," treating misbehavior as a default input. The difference: the protocol's cap on traitors is a hard assumption, while Madison relies on interests being too varied for traitors to coordinate, and he can't prove any such threshold.
  • Evolutionary biology · cheater cells in multicellular life: A multicellular body depends on every cell restraining its own replication. Tumor-suppressor systems such as p53 spot and eliminate cells that reproduce only for themselves. Cancer is a cell lineage that has escaped those checks, a faction swallowing the whole (see Athena Aktipis, The Cheating Cell). The difference: a body can execute its cheaters, but a republic can only check its factions, never abolish them.
  • Social psychology · group polarization: Experiments keep finding that like-minded people who discuss an issue come out more extreme than they went in (Myers and Lamm, among others). That is Madison's small republic: homogeneous people heating each other's passions. The internet lets like-minded people anywhere in the nation gather at any moment, which removes the physical limits Madison's argument counted on.
  • Chinese thought · Han Feizi: Han Feizi also refused to wait for people to become good. The sage, he wrote (paraphrased), doesn't rely on people doing good for him; he makes it impossible for them to do wrong. Both build institutions on distrust of human nature. The difference: Han Feizi's system exists so the ruler can control his subjects, and the ruler himself is unchecked. The Federalist aims the same distrust first at whoever holds power.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① If men were angels, no government would be necessary; if angels governed men, no controls on government would be necessary.

② The hard part of framing a government: first enable it to control the governed, then oblige it to control itself.

③ Ambition must be made to counteract ambition; tie the officeholder's interest to the constitutional rights of the office.

④ Liberty is to faction what air is to fire, and you don't abolish air to prevent fires.

⑤ The causes of faction are sown in human nature, and unequal property is the most durable; if you can't remove the cause, control the effects.

⑥ Extend the sphere: the more varied the interests, the harder it is for any passion to assemble a majority against the minority.

⑦ Separation of powers written on paper is a parchment barrier; only branches that can strike back hold their lines.

⑧ Energy in the executive is a leading mark of good government, and with one person in charge, someone answers for the results.

⑨ The judiciary has neither sword nor purse, only judgment, so it is the weakest branch and the one that most needs independence.

⑩ Where you can't count on virtue, substitute structure: judge an institution by how it runs in the hands of the worst people who might hold it.

Deep Read · You now have the through-line of The Federalist Papers: faction and the large republic, ambition counteracting ambition, parchment barriers and interlocking branches, the double security of a compound republic, an energetic executive and the least dangerous branch, plus its limits as campaign literature, its silence on slavery, and the Bill of Rights the Anti-Federalists won back.
