An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding

David Hume · 1748

中文 →

In one sentence

A thirty-seven-year-old Scot took a startlingly thin book, lifted the floorboards under the whole edifice of human knowledge, and pointed: the causes, the regularities, the sun that will rise tomorrow — none of it rests on the steel beam of reason you assumed was down there. What actually holds it up is something far softer and far less rational: habit. And the remarkable thing is that having said this, he does not urge you to despair. He only insists you know what you are standing on.

Where it sits

David Hume (1711–1776) was a central figure of the Scottish Enlightenment and the last link in the British empiricist chain — Locke, Berkeley, Hume — as well as the man who followed that chain to its end and into territory nobody found comfortable. The 1748 book is not new work: it is a recasting of Book I of the Treatise of Human Nature, written when he was twenty-eight, which in his own phrase "fell dead-born from the press." He decided the problem lay not in the thought but in the bulk and the manner, so he cut it to twelve sections and rewrote it in a far more supple prose. Its position is this: nearly every later argument about why we are entitled to believe what we believe is still being fought on the ground these twelve sections mapped out — Kant credited Hume with waking him from his "dogmatic slumber," and twentieth-century logical positivism, philosophy of science, and today's question of why a model should be trusted outside its training data all trace back to a book you can finish in an afternoon and never quite escape.

The central claims

  • Every material in the mind comes from experience. No idea that is not the copy of some impression; a word with no impression behind it is an empty word — a blade with which he cut away most of the metaphysics that preceded him.
  • We never observe a cause, only one thing reliably following another. The necessary connexion is not out in the world; it is in you — a groove worn into the mind by repetition.
  • Nothing can make the step from past to future on your behalf. It is not a truth of logic, and experience cannot establish it without circularity. What actually carries it is custom — not reason, but a mechanism older than reason. So what Hume offers is not a solution but a sceptical solution: the justification cannot be produced, yet life goes on, because nature never entrusted this particular job to our unreliable reasoning in the first place.

The core ideas, one at a time

Impressions and ideas: two currencies of the mind, and a scalpel

Hume begins with something almost too simple. Everything that turns up in the mind falls into two classes. Impressions are the immediate, forceful, vivid ones — the light you are seeing now, the sound you are hearing, the pain in your shoulder, the anger currently burning. Ideas are the faint copies you use when you remember, imagine or reason. The difference is not one of kind but of force: being scalded now and recalling it later are the same thing at two brightnesses, and the recollection is always the dimmer.

From this comes the first foundation stone: the copy principle — every simple idea is the copy of some prior simple impression. Imagination looks omnipotent but has only one trick, taking apart and recombining: a golden mountain is gold plus mountain. The real proof is negative. A person born blind has no idea of colour, a person born deaf none of sound — not because they cannot imagine hard enough, but because no original exists in them to be copied from.

The force of the principle lies in what Hume sharpens it into. Whenever a suspect philosophical term appears — substance, essence, self, necessity — ask one question: "from what impression is that idea derived?" If no impression can be produced, there is nothing behind the word but a run of agreeable syllables. Logical positivism would later lift this move wholesale and rename it the verifiability criterion of meaning. It changes how you read everything: you stop asking first whether a grand claim is true, and start asking what it is actually saying. (Hume honestly flags one counterexample: line up a graded series of blues with one shade missing, and a person who has never seen that shade can probably supply it from imagination. This is the "missing shade of blue" — he raises it, then declares it too small to revise the principle for. The crack has been there ever since; see the criticisms below.)

Hume's fork: there are only two kinds of true statement

He then splits everything knowable in two, with a cut known ever since as Hume's fork. One half is relations of ideas: geometry, algebra, arithmetic — "three times five equals half of thirty." These are settled by thought alone; their truth has nothing to do with whether anything exists anywhere. To deny one is to contradict yourself, so they are perfectly certain — at the price of telling you no new fact, only unpacking what the definitions already contained.

The other half is matters of fact: the sun will rise tomorrow, water boils at a hundred degrees, this bread will nourish me. Their signature is that the contrary is always conceivable and never contradictory. "The sun will not rise tomorrow" costs you no effort to imagine: absurd in experience, flawless in logic. So factual knowledge never reaches mathematical certainty; at best it is highly probable.

Hume's fork: each side has its virtue — and outside the two, he claims, there is nothing.

What makes the fork lethal is its exhaustiveness: no third category. Which is what licenses the famous verdict at the end of the book — pick up any volume and ask: does it contain abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number? No. Does it contain experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence? No. "Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion." Two centuries later the positivists were all but transcribing this passage as they swept out metaphysics. Your habits change accordingly: presented with any claim, you sort it on reflex — is this about definitions, or about the world? Blurring the two is the commonest bait-and-switch in argument.

Causation: nobody has ever seen a necessary connexion

Here the hardest part of the book begins. How, Hume asks, does any reasoning about fact get from what is present to the senses to what is not? There is only one bridge: cause and effect. You see a watch on the table and infer that someone was here; you hear a voice next door and believe someone is there. Remove causation and you cannot say a word about anything you have not personally witnessed.

So where does our knowledge of causes come from? Not from pure reasoning. Hume's illustration is beautiful: suppose Adam newly created, his rational faculties entirely perfect, looking at a pool of water — could he infer from its fluidity and transparency that it would drown him? He could not. It is not written in the appearance of water. The same holds for magnets attracting iron or gunpowder exploding: there is no link between cause and effect that can be read off the concepts, and the sharpest mind alive would not guess it on first acquaintance. We find these things obvious only because we have seen them so often.

Press further: what exactly do we observe even in experience? Hume's billiard balls — the white rolls across, strikes the red, the red flies off. Watch it ten thousand times and you will see exactly three things: the two are in contact (contiguity), one comes before the other (succession), and the pairing repeats without fail (constant conjunction). What you never once see is the rope supposedly tying them together — the necessary connexion. The hundredth viewing shows the eye no more than the first; the extra confidence is not coming from the eye.

Then where is it coming from? Hume turns his own scalpel on his own question: if "necessity" is an idea, it must have an impression — and that impression is not in the world outside, it is inside. Having met B after A often enough, the mind wears a groove: see A and the thought slides to B unbidden. That felt determination of the mind to make the transition is the original of which the idea of necessity is the copy. We take an internal compulsion of habit and project it outward as a girder of the world. Hence his two definitions of cause: first, an object followed by another, where all objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second; second, an object followed by another, whose appearance always conveys the thought to that other. The first describes a regularity in the world, the second a groove in your head — and Hume admits candidly that these are the only two we can give, and that they are not saying the same thing.

The problem of induction: how exactly is that step taken?

Put the two previous pieces together and you get the splinter Western philosophy has been unable to remove for 270 years. The chain: all reasoning about fact rests on cause and effect; all causal knowledge comes from experience; and experience can only report the past. Yet you spend every waking hour doing something experience cannot license — carrying past regularities into the future.

His way of putting it is disarmingly plain. "The bread I ate before nourished me" is unobjectionable. "This similar-looking bread will nourish me too" is a different statement — and what licenses the jump? To make it work you must first grant a major premise: that nature is uniform, that the future will resemble the past. So how is that established?

  • By logic? No. "Nature changes its habits tomorrow" contains no contradiction whatsoever; you can conceive it effortlessly. It is not a demonstrative truth.
  • By experience? Worse. The only evidence available is that nature has been uniform so far — and getting from that to "so it will continue" requires the very premise in question. An elegant circle: induction propped up by induction.

Schematic: Hume never says the step is wrong. He says it has no reason — and you take it every day regardless.

Notice how restrained the conclusion is: he never says induction is invalid, or should be abandoned, or that science is nonsense. He says the step has no rational ground and that we take it anyway, and must. That distinction is the key to reading him. Once installed, it changes how you watch every move from data to conclusion — tomorrow's share price, an extrapolated lab value, a model's behaviour outside its training distribution. You hear the question automatically: what entitles me to think the part I haven't seen resembles the part I have? Usually the answer is "it always has been so." That is no sin, but knowing whether you are running on habit or on proof decides how badly you are hurt when the world changes face.

Custom and belief: what actually holds us up

Having dismantled the foundation, Hume asks what keeps us upright. The answer is custom, or habit. After enough repetitions of A-then-B, the mind makes the leap with no reasoning involved at all. Hence the book's most quoted line: "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life."

He calls this a sceptical solution — an exquisite phrase meaning: I have not supplied the missing reason; I am only telling you what actually occupies that position. And he thinks this is good news. If an inference as load-bearing as "fire will still burn tomorrow" had been left to our stumbling reason, the species would be extinct. Nature settled it with an instinct: fast, reliable, and requiring no understanding on your part. There is something almost Darwinian in the temperament here: the human being is an animal first, and reason a thin rind grown on the animal, not the other way round.

Paired with this is his redefinition of belief. Imagining that it will rain tomorrow and believing that it will rain tomorrow involve the same idea — so what differs? Not the content, says Hume, but the manner: belief is that same idea held in a more vivid, lively, forcible and steady way. Belief is therefore a feeling, not the product of an inference, which is why you cannot order yourself to believe something — try sincerely believing, right now, that you are in Antarctica. It also explains why persuasion so often runs on repetition, vividness and proximity rather than on a tighter argument, anticipating by two centuries what cognitive psychology would call fluency and the sense that the familiar is true.

Miracles: proportion your belief to the evidence

Section X is the most explosive and most dangerous chapter in the book. Hume lays down a maxim that reads as common sense now and landed as a hammer then: "A wise man proportions his belief to the evidence." Not a switch between belief and disbelief, but a dial turned by the weight of evidence.

Then the famous scale. A miracle is by definition a violation of a law of nature; and laws of nature are precisely what an exceptionless experience establishes — so the evidence against any given miracle is automatically the strongest kind experience can furnish. On the other pan sits human testimony, and humans misremember, embroider, lie, and infect one another in the heat of a crowd. Hence his surgically precise test: no testimony suffices to establish a miracle unless its falsehood would be more miraculous than the fact it seeks to establish.

He adds four empirical supports: no miracle has ever been attested by enough witnesses of sufficient education, good sense and reputation to lose; people relish wonder, so the wilder the story the better it travels; miracles cluster among ignorant nations and remote ages; and rival religions all cite miracles, so their testimonies cancel out. The shift in your thinking is this: faced with an extraordinary claim, don't ask whether the speaker has a motive to lie — ask the balance question. Which is more astonishing, that they are mistaken or lying, or that the thing really happened? The modern slogan "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" is descended directly from this passage. (It is also the most heavily rebutted section in the book; see below.)

Liberty and necessity: a two-thousand-year quarrel he calls merely verbal

Section VIII handles free will in his characteristic way: first show that the two sides are not arguing about the same thing. Human conduct, he points out, is as regular as nature — otherwise history, politics and ordinary social life would be impossible. That you dare hand someone your purse, or expect a workman to come and collect his wages, rests on a stable conjunction of character, motive and action. And since "necessity" amounts to nothing more than constant conjunction plus that leap of the mind, human action has necessity too, and everybody assumes as much in practice.

On the other side, if "liberty" means acting free of any determination at all, the notion is incoherent: an action disconnected from your character and motives could not be imputed to you and is not what anyone wants freedom to mean. Liberty, he says, means one thing only: "a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will" — no chains on your wrists, no gun at your head; you can go if you choose. So liberty and necessity not only fail to conflict, they require each other: it is precisely because an action flows from your character that it is yours, and that praise or blame gets a grip. Posterity calls this compatibilism, still a leading position on free will today. Practical upshot: next time someone argues about whether free will exists, make both sides define "free" first — most of the deadlock evaporates on the spot.

Mitigated scepticism: what he actually wants you to do

The closing section disappoints everyone who has filed him under nihilism: he rejects extreme scepticism too. Pyrrhonism — the ancient school that suspends judgement on everything in the hope of tranquillity — cannot, on his view, be lived. Its sincerest adherent still dodges carts and still fears fire once he leaves the study. His cool observation: in these matters, nature is always too strong for principle.

What he wants is mitigated scepticism: let the cold water run over you once, washing off dogmatism and zeal, and keep two durable benefits — modesty (knowing how shallow the roots of your opinions are, and therefore not bullying those who disagree) and discipline (confining inquiry to subjects our faculties can actually reach), rather than taking a mind shaped for ordinary experience and setting it to prove the first cause of the universe and the final destiny of the soul. That is his real advice: not to stop believing, but to know how firmly you believe; not to halt, but to walk on carrying that knowledge.

The argument in outline

Compressed into a line, the book is a remarkably clean downhill run, each step using only the one before it:

  • Material: everything in the mind is impression or idea, ideas being copies. → You acquire a scalpel: which impression does this word answer to?
  • Division: only two knowable kinds — relations of ideas (certain but empty) and matters of fact (substantive but uncertain); and factual reasoning runs entirely on causation. → Nothing outside mathematics is necessary, and the whole weight now rests on "what licenses causation?"
  • Dismantling: causation is not read off by reason (Adam and the water), and experience yields only contiguity, succession and constant conjunction. → The girder called necessary connexion is not in the world.
  • The break: getting from past to future needs the plank "nature is uniform," which can neither be demonstrated (its contrary is conceivable) nor established by experience (circular). → The inductive step has no reason.
  • Relocation: what carries it is custom; the impression of necessity comes from the mind's trained transition. → Belief is therefore a feeling, not an inference.
  • Harvest and landing: the blade goes to work — miracles (proportion belief to evidence), liberty and necessity (compatibilism), providence and an afterlife (you may infer only a cause proportioned to the observed effect, never a grain more); and finally a refusal of total Pyrrhonian doubt in favour of mitigated scepticism: modesty, plus confining inquiry to what our faculties can reach. The rest, to the flames.

What has he actually established? Not that causation does not exist, nor that knowledge is impossible, but something more precise and more uncomfortable: the most ordinary, least deliberate class of reasoning we perform cannot be justified — and this does not stop it working, because justification was never what made it work. And by what means? Only two: a blade that demands an impression behind every idea, and one exhaustive interrogation of the past-to-future step, with both exits — logic and experience — closed.

Misreadings, criticisms, live disputes

  • Misreading 1: "Hume says there is no such thing as causation." He never says it, and he uses causal reasoning more energetically than almost anyone. What he denies is that we can find in experience, or prove by reason, the necessary connexion. There is also an unfinished scholarly war here: the "Old Hume" reading takes him for a regularity theorist — causation simply is constant conjunction and nothing more; the "New Hume" reading (Galen Strawson and others) takes him for a sceptical realist — real causal powers exist, we merely cannot form a clear idea of them. Both sides find text to quote; the question remains open.
  • Misreading 2: "The problem of induction means science is irrational." Hume idolised Newton and his lifelong ambition was to introduce the experimental method into moral subjects. He identified a gap in justification, not a stop-work order. The replies since take different postures: Popper simply accepted Hume and declared that science never used induction anyway, only conjecture and refutation (theories can be falsified, never confirmed); Bayesians recast the question as how prior probabilities get updated by evidence, at the cost of having to say where the prior came from; P. F. Strawson answered that demanding a justification of induction misfires — proceeding inductively is part of what "reasonable" means, like asking whether the law is legal.
  • Goodman digs the hole deeper. Nelson Goodman invented the predicate "grue," defined as observed to be green before some future time, or blue thereafter. Every emerald ever observed supports "all emeralds are green" and supports "all emeralds are grue" exactly as well — while the two predict opposite futures. The question escalates from "does induction have a justification?" to "given identical evidence, what entitles us to project one regularity rather than the other?"
  • The weak joints in the miracles argument. Define a miracle as a violation of natural law and then object that natural law rests on exceptionless experience, and you look uncomfortably close to begging the question — packing the conclusion into the definition. The more substantive attack comes from probability theory: Charles Babbage argued long ago that several independent and individually unreliable witnesses can, in concert, lift a very low prior; the contemporary philosopher of science John Earman devoted a whole book, Hume's Abject Failure, to arguing that the a priori half of this section is close to worthless in Bayesian terms. And the premise of "exceptionless experience" is itself exactly the kind of induction Hume had just taken apart.
  • The crack in the copy principle. The missing shade of blue is a counterexample Hume raised and waved away himself — if one simple idea can be supplied without an impression, the scalpel is chipped. Worse: which prong of the fork does the principle itself belong to? It is plainly no truth of definition; and if it is an empirical generalisation, it has no standing to pass sentence on anything else. That is the same boomerang later thrown at logical positivism: is the verifiability criterion itself verifiable?
  • Kant's counter-move. Kant granted the awakening and drew the opposite conclusion: causality is not read out of experience, nor is it merely a psychological habit — it is a formal condition the mind lays down in advance for any possible experience (a category). Hume was right to find no impression, because causation is not inside experience; it is what makes experience possible. The manoeuvre rescues universality and necessity, at the price of restricting us to appearances and barring the thing in itself.
  • One more thing, honestly recorded. In a footnote to his essay "Of National Characters," Hume wrote a flatly racist claim (suspecting those of African descent to be naturally inferior), which contradicts his own rule that conclusions must answer to evidence — prejudice mistaken for observation. In 2020 the University of Edinburgh renamed its David Hume Tower over it. This does not refute his arguments, but there is no need to cover for him while reading them.

The Hume beyond this book

Read only this one and you get a brilliant demolition expert in epistemology while missing the half he cared about most. Three additions are essential.

First, the is–ought gap. A very short passage in the Treatise notes that moral writers proceed for a while with "is" and then, imperceptibly, the copula becomes "ought" — and no set of statements about what is can yield a conclusion about what ought to be unless a premise that is already evaluative has been smuggled in. This crack, later dubbed Hume's guillotine, remains the first checkpoint for ethics and for every claim that science can tell us how to live.

Second, reason as the slave of the passions. The most notorious provocation in the same book: "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions." The argument hangs on the same blade as this one — reason can tell you what is true and how to reach an end, but it never generates ends; what drives action is always desire and feeling. He pushes it coldly to the limit: "'Tis not contrary to reason to prefer the destruction of the whole world to the scratching of my finger." The absurdity is the point: it shows that the charge of "irrationality" cannot attach to ends at all, only to means and to factual judgements.

Third, the self as a bundle, and morals grounded in sentiment. Turn the copy principle on the word "self" and Hume finds no constant impression of one — introspection only ever stumbles on some particular heat, cold, love, hatred, pain or pleasure. Hence the bundle theory: the self is a bundle of perceptions succeeding one another with inconceivable rapidity, a theatre with no stage (in an appendix he candidly admits the account still has problems he cannot solve — honest again). The conclusion stands strikingly close to the Buddhist doctrine of no-self. As for morality: examine a vicious act as often as you like and you will never find a fact called "vice," until you turn your attention back to the sentiment of disapproval in your own breast. Moral distinctions rest on sentiment and sympathy (here meaning the contagion and resonance of feeling between people, not pity). The book he most valued in later life was the one on that side of his work, the Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals; and his full assault on religion he left in the Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, which appeared only after his death.

Ten sentences

1. Everything in the mind is a copy taken from experience: when a grand word turns up, ask which impression it answers to — the ones that cannot produce an impression are usually empty.

2. Only two kinds of statement are knowable: the ones about definitions (certain, but telling you nothing new) and the ones about the world (substantive, but always possibly false); confusing the two is the commonest sleight of hand in argument.

3. Adam, however perfect his reason, could not see that water would drown him — causation is never read off the appearance of things, only learned by running into it often.

4. Watch ten thousand collisions and you will see contiguity, succession and unfailing repetition; the rope called necessary connexion you will not see once.

5. The impression of necessity is not in the world but in you: repetition wears a groove in the mind, and we project that groove outward as a structure of reality.

6. Nothing supports the step from past to future — logic cannot prove it (the contrary is conceivable) and experience cannot without circling. We take it daily all the same, without a reason.

7. "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life." Nature never entrusted life-and-death inference to our unreliable reasoning; it handed the job to an instinct — a defect, and a rescue.

8. Belief is not a better argument but the same idea held more vividly and steadily — which makes it a feeling, and is why you cannot command yourself to believe.

9. "A wise man proportions his belief to the evidence." Faced with an astonishing report, don't ask whether the teller has a motive to lie; weigh the scale — which is more astonishing, that they erred, or that it happened?

10. The quarrel over free will is largely verbal: liberty is not "undetermined by causes" but "acting or not acting as your will determines" — it is because the act flows from your character that it is yours. And since total doubt cannot be lived, what to keep is the mitigated kind: know how firmly you believe, then walk on.
