Influence

The Psychology of Persuasion · Robert B. Cialdini · first edition 1984

中文 →

In one sentence

No brain can analyse everything the day throws at it, so ours came equipped with a set of shortcut switches: hear the word "because" and wave it through, see that everyone else is doing it and follow, accept a small favour and feel indebted, hear "only three left" and reach for your wallet. This book takes the switches apart one by one, and its real subject is this: they usually save you, and everyone who persuades for a living knows how to flip them without giving you anything real. The bleakest line comes at the end — knowing all this does not make you immune.

Where it sits

Cialdini is a social psychologist at Arizona State University. What separates this 1984 book from ordinary psychology writing is that its raw material did not all come from the lab: he spent three years doing participant observation, answering job ads under an assumed name to train as a car salesman, a fundraiser, a telemarketer, an ad man — taking notes on the tactics that never make it into journals but are taught as common sense inside those industries. So it runs in both directions: lab-tested mechanisms on one side, an insider's operating manual on the other. He wrote it as self-defence, for people who keep finding they have agreed to things and only later wonder why — himself above all.

The central claims

  • People run "click, whirr" routines. Facing more than we can weigh, we develop programmes that fire a whole sequence of behaviour off a single cue. These shortcuts are right nearly all the time — and because they are right, we stopped checking them.
  • Influence professionals don't persuade; they trigger. They don't argue with you, they manufacture the cue — a small gift, a number, a uniform, a deadline — and let your programme run itself. You think you're judging. You're executing.
  • Seeing it doesn't stop it. These reactions fire before you notice, so the real defence isn't "try harder to be rational". It is learning to recognise the bodily signal of being pushed, and to counterattack in exactly one circumstance: when you discover the cue was counterfeit.

The core ideas, one at a time

Click, whirr: one cue, a whole programme

Cialdini opens with the fixed-action pattern from animal behaviour. A mother turkey's entire repertoire of care for her chicks hangs on a single trigger: the chick's "cheep-cheep". Push a stuffed polecat — her natural enemy — toward her with a recorder playing that sound inside, and she will gather it under her wing; switch the tape off and the same object is savaged. An intricate, generally excellent maternal programme, suspended from one noise.

We have them too. Ellen Langer's photocopier study is the cleanest demonstration. Cutting into a queue with "Excuse me, I have five pages, may I use the machine?" got about 60% compliance. Adding a real reason — "because I'm in a rush" — took it to 94%. Adding a reason that says nothing at all — "because I have to make some copies" — took it to 93%. What fired the switch was not the content of the reason but the shape of the word "because". But there is a second half everyone drops: when the request rose to twenty pages, the empty reason stopped working entirely, falling back to the no-reason rate. Raise the stakes and people switch out of autopilot. This isn't a story about humans being stupid; it's a story about humans rationing attention by cost.

Its close relative is the contrast principle: put your hand in cold water first and a bucket of lukewarm water feels hot; put it in hot water first and the same bucket feels cold. We perceive differences, not absolutes. Hence the iron rule of retail: sell the expensive item first. Sell a customer a $900 suit and the $90 sweater looks like nothing; reverse the order and the suit is terrifying. An estate agent shows you two overpriced wrecks so that the third, ordinary house glows. The insidious part is that it requires no lie at all — only a sequence.

Reciprocation: a debt has to be paid

Reciprocation is a rule found in every human society: if someone gives you something, you owe something back. Anthropologists argue it is precisely this rule that makes giving first safe, and so makes division of labour, trade and mutual aid possible — it is the starter motor of civilisation. Because it is that fundamental, violators get labelled moochers and ingrates, and the social pressure is heavy enough that we will overpay rather than stay in debt.

Dennis Regan quantified its force. In his experiment a confederate stepped out during a break and came back with two Cokes, handing one to the subject unasked. Later he asked the subject to buy raffle tickets. Subjects who had been given a Coke bought about twice as many — and how much they liked the man made no difference. That is the crucial part: reciprocation bypasses liking entirely. Someone you dislike can still extract money from you, provided he gives you something first.

Which is why free samples, free trials, the address labels that arrive with the charity letter and the flower pressed into your hand at the airport (the Hare Krishna classic) all work: the favour was uninvited, but the debt is real. The book's most haunting example: after the 1985 Mexico City earthquake, Ethiopia — then in the middle of its own famine — sent $5,000 in relief, because Mexico had sent aid to Ethiopia when Italy invaded in 1935. Fifty years and a famine had not cancelled that debt.

Reciprocation has a subtler edition: we trade not just favours but concessions. This is rejection-then-retreat, also known as door-in-the-face. Cialdini's own study: stop university students on the street and ask them to counsel juvenile delinquents two hours a week for two years — nobody agrees; then immediately retreat to "well, would you chaperone a group of them on a day trip to the zoo?" — and compliance runs about three times the rate for that request asked cold. Open impossibly high, then give ground, and your concession pulls one out of the other person — and because they feel they negotiated the outcome, they end up more satisfied and more likely to follow through. Its mirror image, foot-in-the-door, belongs to the next principle.

Commitment and consistency: we grow new reasons for what we've already done

We are under strong internal pressure to keep our actions, words and beliefs in line with each other. Inconsistent people are read as flaky and untrustworthy, and consistency is also a labour-saving device — decide once and you never have to think about it again. So the professional's aim is not to get you to agree to the big thing; it is to get you to take one small step and let you do the rest yourself.

The canonical evidence is Freedman and Fraser's study. Asked cold to install an enormous, ugly DRIVE CAREFULLY billboard on their front lawns, about 17% of suburban homeowners agreed. But if two weeks earlier someone had asked them to display a three-inch BE A SAFE DRIVER sticker in a window — a request so trivial almost nobody refused — compliance with the billboard request jumped to 76%. Why? Because after the sticker their self-image had shifted: I am the sort of person who cares about civic causes. The billboard wasn't a concession to a canvasser; it was loyalty to themselves.

Cialdini identifies four amplifiers that harden a commitment: it is active (you wrote it yourself rather than ticking a box), public (others saw), effortful (it cost you), and freely chosen (no obvious outside pressure). With all four, the commitment grows its own legs.

That explains a set of otherwise unrelated phenomena. Chinese-run POW camps in the Korean War extracted collaboration not through torture but through essay contests: write a short piece on how America is not perfect and win a small prize. Almost everyone wrote one, and because the prize was too trivial to count as a bribe, the prisoner had to explain his own behaviour as "I suppose I do think that" — and his views slid a little further with each essay. Fraternities, military academies and gangs whose initiations are more brutal reliably produce more loyal members, because "I suffered this much for it" demands an explanation, and the only dignified one is that it was worth it. The company that runs a "why I love this product" contest is working the identical mechanism.

The nakedest application is the lowball. A car dealer quotes a price too good to refuse; you test-drive, fill in the forms, imagine the neighbours, talk your partner round — and once everything is in place, the manager won't approve it, or an item was left off, and the price climbs back to normal. Most people buy anyway. The original price stopped being the reason long ago; you have since piled your own reasons on top of it. Remove the foundation and the building stands. The only defence is to ask, the moment the terms change: "if this had been the price at the start, would I have said yes?" If not, walk out.

Social proof: when unsure, watch what others do

Social proof means we decide what is correct by finding out what other people think is correct. Canned laughter is obnoxious, yet it works — audiences laugh longer and rate the material funnier, even knowing the laughter is a recording. The shortcut is sound: what most people do is usually the right thing to do.

Two conditions maximise its power: uncertainty (the situation is ambiguous and you don't know what to do) and similarity (the more the others resemble you, the more their behaviour tells you). Similarity is the practically useful half. To cut a household's electricity use, "save the planet" and "save money" both underperform "77% of the households on your street already do this"; a hotel card asking guests to reuse towels works better saying that most guests who stayed in this room reused theirs than making an environmental appeal (a field experiment by Goldstein, Cialdini and Griskevicius).

Uncertainty explains the book's most famous case — and the one most in need of correction: pluralistic ignorance. When something happens in front of a crowd, each bystander scans the others to judge whether it is really an emergency, and each is working hard to look composed. So everyone reads calm off everyone else's face, and the group manufactures a collective misjudgement. The more people present, the more diffused the responsibility and the less anyone moves. Hence the single most practical piece of advice in the book: in a real emergency, don't shout "somebody help" — point at one person and say, "You, sir, in the blue jacket: call an ambulance." Turn ambiguity into instruction and a crowd into one person.

Social proof also has a backfire mode that gets persistently overlooked. Cialdini's team ran a field study in Petrified Forest National Park, where signs warned that the forest was being destroyed because so many visitors take pieces of petrified wood. The intent was to shame; the message received was that everyone takes some. Theft along the stretches carrying that sign went up, not down. Every public-information campaign should have this tattooed somewhere: "look how widespread this problem is" and "you shouldn't do it" are two sentences that destroy each other, and the first one wins.

Liking: we say yes to people we like

The least surprising principle, but worth breaking into components, because every one of them can be cheaply faked:

  • Physical attractiveness. The halo effect silently converts good-looking into clever, kind and honest. Attractive defendants and job applicants fare measurably better — and we have no awareness of doing it.
  • Similarity. Shared accent, hometown, dress, hobbies, even a shared first name raise liking. Sales training openly teaches mirroring: copy the posture, the pace, the vocabulary.
  • Compliments. Praise works even when you know it is flattery and know the flatterer wants something. Joe Girard, the record-breaking car salesman, mailed 13,000 customers a card every month carrying one line: "I like you." Everybody knew it was printed. It still worked.
  • Contact and cooperation. Familiarity breeds liking — but only when the contact is pleasant. What actually dissolves hostility is cooperation toward a shared goal: Aronson's "jigsaw classroom", where the material is split so each student holds one piece and no one can succeed alone, measurably cooled the antagonism in newly desegregated classrooms. The police "good cop / bad cop" routine is the same principle put to worse use.
  • Association. Bearers of good news get a share of the glow and bearers of bad news get blamed — weather forecasters receive hate mail about rain. Cialdini's own research found that after a football win, students wore college insignia significantly more often and described the game as "we won"; after a loss, "they lost." We instinctively tether ourselves to winners and cut ourselves loose from losers. Celebrity endorsement and the car-plus-model advertisement are buying exactly this wire.

The commercial apex of liking is the Tupperware party: the person selling to you isn't the salesperson, it's your friend — she invited you into her home, fed you, and you know she profits from your order. You aren't buying storage containers, you are maintaining a relationship. The only defence that works in such a room is to force the two questions apart before paying: how much do I like this person, and, separately, is this thing worth the money?

Authority: obedience we were trained into

Deferring to authority is usually rational — doctors, pilots and engineers really do know more. The trouble is that what we defer to is often not the expert judgement but the symbols of authority.

Milgram's shock experiments are the centrepiece. Ordinary people, told they were in a study of learning, followed a lab-coated experimenter's instruction to raise the voltage on a "learner" in the next room, step by step, through protest, screams, banging on the wall and finally silence. Roughly two thirds went to the highest switch, the one labelled lethal. No threats, no incentives — just a calm voice saying "the experiment requires that you continue." (For the serious problems with this study, see the criticisms below; it is far less clean than the usual telling.)

More everyday are three symbols: titles, clothes, trappings. The book cites a medication-error case in which a physician abbreviated "place in right ear" as "place in R ear", and the nurse dutifully administered the drops into the patient's rectum. Absurd — yet nobody stopped to ask why an earache is treated from that end, because the price of asking is contradicting a doctor. Clothes do the same work: in early street studies, several times as many pedestrians followed a man across against the lights when he was dressed in a suit as when he wasn't; and when a car sits still after the light turns green, drivers behind honk markedly less often if it is a luxury car — they just sit there.

The defence isn't "distrust experts". It is to swap the question: don't ask whether this person is an authority, ask is this person genuinely an expert on this, and how honest is this person likely to be here? The actor in the white coat in the advert scores "looks like it" on the first and "is being paid" on the second. One counterintuitive refinement from Cialdini: a genuine expert who volunteers a small drawback first ("we're not the cheapest") becomes markedly more credible afterwards, because someone who surrenders unfavourable information doesn't look like someone managing you.

Scarcity: losing moves us more than gaining

Scarcity draws on two sources. One is the shortcut: hard-to-get things are usually better, so substituting rarity for a quality judgement is broadly sensible. The second runs deeper — reactance: Jack Brehm's theory holds that when a freedom is restricted or threatened, we feel a surge to reclaim it, and the restricted option becomes suddenly more desirable. What we want is not just the thing but the continued right to want it.

That accounts for a string of phenomena. The "no!" of the two-year-old and the rebellion of the teenager are peaks of reactance at the two moments when the sense of autonomy is newly forming. Parental opposition appears to intensify a romance (the so-called Romeo and Juliet effect — though that correlational study has not replicated robustly). When Dade County, Florida banned phosphate detergents in the 1970s, residents not only smuggled them in from out of state, they began rating phosphate detergents as better cleaners — a property they had not credited them with before the ban. The ban changed nothing about the detergent; it changed how people rated it. Likewise, evidence a judge rules inadmissible and instructs a jury to disregard tends to weigh more heavily, not less.

The most instructive study is the cookie-jar experiment by Worchel and colleagues. Subjects tasted a cookie and rated it: from a jar of ten, low ratings; from a jar of only two, higher. The second step is the clever one — one group was first given the jar of ten, which was then swapped for one holding two. Cookies that became scarce beat cookies that were always scarce. And ratings were highest of all when subjects were told the jar had been taken away because other people wanted them. Two rules fall out: (1) newly imposed scarcity bites harder than constant scarcity; (2) scarcity caused by competition bites hardest of all. That is the machinery under flash sales, "12 people are looking at this room right now", and the fever of an auction room — being deprived is harder to bear than never having had.

Scarcity applies to information too: the banned book is the one you want to read, the deleted post the one you want to see — and worse, people shift toward believing censored content before they have read a word of it. That point is more worth holding onto now than it was in 1984.

Unity: not "I like you" but "you are us"

In the 2021 expanded edition Cialdini added a seventh principle, unity. He insists it differs in kind from liking: liking is I like you, unity is you are us — shared family, region, ethnicity, faith, or something lived through or built together. It is stronger because it skips evaluation and goes straight to identity: persuading a member of "us" takes no argument, only a reminder of who they are. It is why furniture you assembled yourself is valued more highly, and why politicians labour to establish that they come from your town, hold your faith, ate the same dirt. It is the most seemingly unanswerable of the seven, which is exactly why it deserves the most suspicion — "we" is the cheapest prop in all of persuasion, and the most expensive to hand over.

The skeleton

The book runs mechanism → seven-item checklist → defence, but the thread that actually ties it together is this:

  • The premise: too much information, too little time, so humans must use shortcuts. Shortcuts key off single cues — and a cue can be stripped out and manufactured on its own. That is the crack the whole book lives in.
  • The seven shortcuts: reciprocation (I owe them), commitment and consistency (I already said so), social proof (everyone's doing it), liking (I like them), authority (they know), scarcity (it's running out), unity (they're one of us). Each corresponds to a decision rule that is genuinely useful in evolution and in society.
  • What the professional actually does: not create value, but create the cue — a free pen, a tiny commitment, a BESTSELLER sticker, a white coat, a countdown timer.
  • The defence: not to switch the shortcuts off (you can't, and you shouldn't want to) but to bolt on an authenticity check. Cialdini's criterion is sharp: once you find the cue was faked, you owe it no compliance at all — a counterfeit favour is not a favour, a counterfeit shortage is not a shortage, the debt is void and you can walk away without a twinge. And what alerts you is usually not a thought but a body: that queasy "something's off and I can't say what" feeling, and the regret afterwards.

Misreadings, criticisms, live disputes

  • The replication crisis (discount accordingly). This book comes out of social psychology's golden age, an era of small samples and flexible analysis; the large-scale replication movement after 2010 reshuffled the whole field. Treat the book's most vivid single experiments as instructive stories rather than settled facts. The findings repeated many times in the field with stable effect sizes — especially descriptive norm interventions like the hotel towels and household electricity — deserve much more confidence. The test is not how well a study is told but how often it has been independently repeated.
  • The Kitty Genovese details are mostly wrong. The 1964 New York Times account of 38 witnesses watching a murder and doing nothing has since been heavily corrected by historians and by the Times itself (2016): there were far fewer witnesses, most heard fragments and saw nothing, someone did call the police, and a neighbour went down and held her as she died. But keep two things apart: the story is wrong, while the bystander effect itself replicates well in the lab. Meanwhile a study analysing real CCTV footage of public conflicts found someone intervened in roughly nine cases out of ten — reality is considerably better than the laboratory.
  • Milgram is not as clean as the textbooks say. Working from the original archives, Gina Perry showed that the experimenter frequently departed from the script and pressed subjects more times than the protocol allowed; a substantial fraction of participants said afterwards they had suspected at the time that the shocks were fake; and obedience rates varied enormously across conditions, with the famous 65% being just one of them. It demonstrates the power of situations; it is not a precise measurement.
  • The effects are smaller than the anecdotes suggest. In meta-analysis, foot-in-the-door and door-in-the-face are real but modest, and heavily dependent on who asks, how long between asks, and how reasonable the request is. The occupational hazard of reading this book is believing you have acquired a set of switches that work on command. They tilt probabilities; they are not a remote control.
  • Spin-off claims like the "Werther effect" are contested. The research on suicide rates rising after media coverage has been argued over for decades on both effect size and causal inference. Don't file it as settled.
  • It was read as an attack manual. Cialdini wrote self-defence and the world took delivery of a sales bible — the book's great irony. He has since insisted on one ethical line: these principles may be detected and reported, never manufactured. If your product really is scarce, say so; if most people really do use it, say so. Faking social proof or inventing a deadline is not only dishonest but a bad trade: once the counterfeit is spotted, what breaks is trust, and trust is the capital every later persuasion is drawn against.
  • The most honest and most deflating point: knowing this doesn't stop it happening to you. These reactions fire before awareness, so "I've read Cialdini" confers no immunity. It gives you two things only: the ability to explain afterwards what just happened, and a sliver of room to stop in the moment — to notice the sensation of being carried along and say "I'll get back to you tomorrow." The most reliable defence was never intelligence; it's a rule: anything that pressures you to decide right now gets answered tomorrow.

Ten sentences

1. The mind runs click-whirr routines that fire whole sequences off a single cue; they're usually right, so we stopped checking — and the persuader's job is to manufacture the cue on its own.

2. Langer's photocopier study: the word "because" alone gets you waved through, with or without a real reason — but make the request bigger and people snap back to manual. We ration attention by cost.

3. Reciprocation is civilisation's starter motor and its strongest hook: one unasked-for Coke doubled raffle-ticket sales, and liking the giver made no difference at all.

4. Open with a request certain to be refused, then retreat — your concession extracts theirs, and because they feel they negotiated it, they're happier and likelier to deliver.

5. Professionals don't get you to agree to the big thing, only to take one small step: a three-inch sticker lifted compliance with a giant billboard from 17% to 76% — because after the step, you grow your own reasons.

6. Four things harden a commitment: active, public, effortful, freely chosen. Anywhere you're invited to say it aloud, write it down or declare it in front of others, a leg is being fitted.

7. Unsure, we watch others — while everyone watches everyone else stay calm, and the crowd manufactures "it must be nothing". In an emergency don't shout for help: point and say, "You, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance."

8. Social proof backfires: a sign lamenting how many visitors steal petrified wood raised theft. Saying how widespread a problem is means saying everyone does it.

9. Scarcity's force is not in the object but in the instant a freedom is removed: cookies that became scarce beat cookies that always were, especially when someone else took them — and banned information gains credibility before anyone reads it.

10. The honest conclusion: knowing all this, you still get caught. The book offers no immunity, only two things — recognising the bodily feeling of being pushed, and one rule: whatever demands a decision now gets answered tomorrow, and any favour or shortage you find was faked cancels the debt on the spot.
