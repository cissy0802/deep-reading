Organizational Culture and Leadership

Edgar H. Schein · 1985 (5th edition 2017, with Peter A. Schein)

中文 →

In One Sentence

An organization's culture isn't the values poster in the lobby or the employee handbook. It's the layer of basic assumptions a group picked up while solving its problems, assumptions that worked well enough to be taken as true and that nobody says out loud anymore. Culture decides what people notice, what they take for granted, and what never gets said at all, and a leader's most fundamental job is to create it, see it, and change it once it turns into a cage.

Where It Sits

Schein (1928–2023) was an organizational psychologist at MIT's Sloan School, and this book did more than any other to turn "organizational culture" into a serious academic idea. Along with Hofstede's work on national cultures, it is one of the two foundations of culture research: Hofstede compares dozens of countries side by side, while Schein digs straight down into one organization.

The Core Argument

  • Culture comes in layers, and the layer that actually does the work is the invisible one at the bottom: basic assumptions. The behavior you see and the slogans you hear sit on the surface. To understand an organization you have to dig down to the premises nobody debates anymore, or even thinks need debating.
  • Culture is learned. It's the residue of past success. When a way of doing things keeps working, it slides from "let's try this" to "this is simply how things are done." Culture isn't decoration somebody designed; it's a group's collective answer to the problem of survival.
  • Culture and leadership are two sides of the same coin. Founders and leaders impose their beliefs on a group, and if those beliefs bring success, they settle into culture. Once culture forms, it decides in turn what kind of leader the organization will accept. A leader who can't read the culture ends up being managed by it.

Put together: culture is a group's shared answer, now sunk below awareness, to the questions of how the world works, who we are, and how things should be done. It was once the reason for success, and sooner or later it becomes the resistance to change.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. What culture is: shared assumptions that are learned, taken for granted, and handed down

Schein's definition is long, but every word is doing work. Paraphrased: culture is a pattern of shared basic assumptions that a group learned as it solved its problems of external adaptation and internal integration, that has worked well enough to be considered valid, and that is therefore taught to new members as the correct way to perceive, think, and feel about those problems.

Four things stand out. First, culture belongs to a group, and it needs a shared history: a project team assembled last week has no culture, but a startup that has survived three near-death moments definitely does. Second, culture is learned, and what gets learned are solutions to two kinds of problems. External adaptation is how the group survives in its market and environment: what our mission is, how we set goals, what means we use, how we measure, how we correct course. Internal integration is how the group holds together: what language we share, who counts as one of us, how power and status are distributed, how close is close enough, which behavior gets rewarded and which gets punished. Third, it works well enough. Culture isn't the optimal answer; it's the answer that hasn't killed us yet. Fourth, it gets taught to newcomers, and taught as the right way, not just one way. Newcomers who don't comply get corrected or frozen out.

An analogy: culture is like the way you walk. You worked it out as a toddler, some gait kept you from falling, and it set. As an adult you never think about how you walk, until you hurt your leg or someone imitates you and you suddenly realize, "oh, so that's how I move." The defining feature of culture is that the people inside it are the last to see it.

Why it matters: the definition rules out the most popular corporate move, which is to write a few values and hang them on the wall. That's espoused values at best, and if it doesn't match what people actually get rewarded for, it hasn't touched the culture at all. It changes how you look at things: if you want to know a company's culture, ignore what it says. Watch what it does under pressure, in a dilemma, when no one is looking, and then ask what premise would make that behavior feel obvious.

2. Three levels: artifacts, espoused values, basic assumptions

This is the book's most famous model and its most quoted. Schein splits culture into three levels, like an iceberg: the deeper you go, the less visible, the harder to change, and the more it matters.

LevelWhat it isExamplesVisibility
ArtifactsEverything you see, hear, and feel when you walk inOpen-plan offices, dress, how meetings run, jargon, stories, ritualsEasy to see, hard to decode
Espoused beliefs and valuesWhat the organization says it believes and how it should behaveMission statements, values lists, strategic slogansEasy to say, not always done
Basic underlying assumptionsPremises taken for granted and no longer discussed"Truth comes out of argument." "The boss knows best." "People slack off unless watched."Invisible, yet they drive everything

The three levels of culture: the deeper the level, the more hidden it is and the more it drives behavior.

The trap with artifacts is that they're easy to see and hard to read. Walk into a company where everyone sits in open seating, wears T-shirts, and calls the CEO by first name, and it's tempting to conclude the place is egalitarian. But the same surface can come from completely different assumptions: real equality, or a founder who thinks cubicles waste money, or a culture where everyone keeps an eye on everyone else. Schein's warning: infer culture from artifacts alone and you will almost certainly project your own culture onto it.

Espoused values are the second level. They usually start as one leader's claim: "We should care more about customers." At first it's one person's belief, and the group goes along half-convinced. If it works, the belief goes through what Schein calls a cognitive transformation: from "someone thinks we should do this" to "we all know this is right," and eventually down to level three, where it becomes an assumption. Plenty of values never make that trip. They get written down to express an aspiration, or to cover for what actually happens. That gap between what's espoused and what's practiced is the most useful crack for diagnosing a culture. When a company says "we encourage risk-taking" and then fires the person who led a failed project, the real culture lives in that crack.

Basic assumptions are level three, the substance of culture itself. They're so deeply held that ideas violating them seem unthinkable, even absurd. Take a contrast the management theorist Douglas McGregor called Theory X and Theory Y. In one company it goes without saying that people only work hard when closely supervised; in another it goes equally without saying that people naturally want to do good work and management's job is to get out of the way. Their timekeeping, approval chains, and pay schemes will look nothing alike, and employees in both will feel their way is just obviously right.

Why it matters: the three levels give you a diagnostic order. Start with visible artifacts, compare them with espoused values, and wherever the two don't line up, dig for the assumption underneath. It changes how you see the world: when a reform keeps "failing in execution," the problem usually isn't execution. It has run into an assumption nobody has said out loud.

3. What the assumptions are about: truth, time, human nature, relationships

What, concretely, are basic assumptions about? Schein borrows from the anthropologists Kluckhohn and Strodtbeck, who studied "value orientations," and lists the deepest dimensions. Every organization has default answers on each:

  • Reality and truth: what counts as true? Whatever authority says, whatever the data says, or whatever survives argument?
  • Time: do we face the past, the present, or the future? Does "on time" mean to the minute or roughly? Do we do one thing at a time (monochronic time) or many things in parallel (polychronic time)?
  • Human nature: are people basically lazy or industrious? Fixed, or capable of growth?
  • Human relationships: individualist or collectivist? Should power be concentrated or shared?

Schein illustrates this with a now-classic contrast between two companies he consulted for, which sat at almost opposite ends of these dimensions.

The first was Digital Equipment Corporation (DEC), founded by Ken Olsen and at one point the second-largest computer company in America. DEC's core assumption: truth comes from conflict. An idea is right only after people have fought over it hard and it has survived the challenge. So DEC's meetings were loud, anyone could challenge anyone, and engineers were assumed to be smart, responsible adults who could make their own calls. Decisions waited until everyone had bought in. In the minicomputer era, that culture made DEC wildly creative.

The second was Ciba-Geigy, the Swiss chemical and pharmaceutical giant. Its core assumption: truth comes from scientific research and seniority. The higher your rank and the deeper your expertise, the more likely you are to be right. Meetings were quiet and formal, and juniors didn't challenge seniors in public. Schein tells a story that surprised even him. He sent a memo he thought was useful to several managers and asked them to pass it on to their peers. Nobody did. He later understood that here, sending a fellow manager information he hadn't asked for implied he didn't know his own business. It was an insult. Each person's area of responsibility was his turf. At DEC, the more open information was, the better; at Ciba-Geigy, information had an owner.

The real value of the contrast: neither culture was "wrong." Each was the winning answer its group had learned in its own environment. DEC's culture of argument fit a fast-moving technical market that rewarded trial and error; Ciba-Geigy's hierarchy and caution fit an industry with long research cycles where mistakes were enormously costly. It changes how you look at things: don't ask whether a culture is good. Ask whether it works in this environment, and whether it still does.

4. How leaders embed culture: watch what they attend to, not what they say

Where does culture come from? Schein's answer: mostly from founders and early leaders. A founder starts a company with beliefs about the world, about people, about what success takes. They hire people who think the same way and impose their methods on the team. If the company survives, everyone credits its success to those methods, and the beliefs settle into shared assumptions.

What makes Schein so useful is that he spells out the actual mechanisms leaders use to embed culture, in two groups. The first are the primary embedding mechanisms, the forces that really shape culture:

  • what leaders regularly pay attention to, measure, and control: the number they grill people on every week is the company's real priority;
  • how leaders react to critical incidents and crises: emotions run highest in a crisis, so people learn fastest then and remember longest;
  • how leaders allocate resources: where the budget goes is more honest than any strategy document;
  • deliberate role modeling, teaching, and coaching;
  • how leaders hand out rewards and status;
  • how leaders recruit, select, promote, and excommunicate: above all who gets fired, which announces to everyone what will not be tolerated.

The second group are secondary articulation and reinforcement mechanisms: organizational structure, systems and procedures, rituals, the design of physical space, the stories and legends people tell, formal mission statements. The key point is that these secondary mechanisms only reinforce culture when they line up with the primary ones. If the values handbook says "we invest in our people" while the boss only ever asks about quarterly revenue and cuts the training budget first in a downturn, employees will learn the second lesson every time.

Think of how children learn what matters. Not from their parents' lectures, but from when their parents actually get angry and when they're actually pleased. Organizations work the same way. Employees are a tribe of sharp-eyed anthropologists, decoding their leaders' attention day and night.

It changes how you look at things: if you want to change a culture, start not with the slogans but with your own calendar, the questions you ask, and your personnel decisions.

5. The life cycle of culture: from glue to cage

Schein stresses that culture plays very different roles at different stages of an organization's life.

Founding and early growth: culture is glue and identity. The founder's assumptions are the company's competitive edge, and employees take pride in being "not like the others." At this stage any challenge to the culture reads as betrayal.

Midlife: as the company grows and splits into divisions, regions, and functions, subcultures emerge. Schein singles out three occupational subcultures found almost everywhere. The operator culture believes work gets done through people and teamwork, and hates being tied down by rules. The engineering culture believes design and technology should remove human uncertainty; its ideal system is one that needs no people. The executive culture is centered on finance, sees people as costs, and answers to capital markets. Each makes sense on its own terms, and they routinely fail to understand each other. A lot of "execution problems" are really translation problems between these three.

Maturity and decline: when the environment shifts and the company keeps marching on the assumptions of its old success, culture flips from asset to liability. The painful part is that the very assumptions that made the company succeed are what keep it from seeing the new reality. DEC is the case Schein lived through. "Truth comes from argument" was a fountain of creativity on the technical frontier, but when the personal computer arrived and the market demanded fast, focused decisions, it turned into endless debate without resolution. The assumption that engineers were king also led the company to look down on marketing and low-end products. DEC was acquired by Compaq in 1998. Schein later wrote a whole book about it, DEC Is Dead, Long Live DEC (2003): DEC's culture failed commercially, but the people and ideas it produced spread across the entire industry.

It changes how you look at things: no culture is good or bad forever; there is only fit with the environment. A company is often in the most danger exactly when it is proudest of its culture.

6. Changing culture: survival anxiety has to exceed learning anxiety

Can culture change? Yes, but it's very hard, because changing a basic assumption means admitting that something we've always believed no longer holds, and that shakes people's sense of who they are. Schein uses the psychologist Kurt Lewin's model of unfreezing, changing, and refreezing: loosen the old equilibrium, learn the new way, then let the new way stabilize.

The sharpest part is his analysis of unfreezing in terms of two anxieties:

  • Survival anxiety: "If I don't change, I (or the company) will go under." This is what pushes change.
  • Learning anxiety: "If I do change, I may look incompetent, lose status, lose my identity, get pushed out of the group." This is what resists it.

Change happens when survival anxiety is greater than learning anxiety. Most leaders instinctively crank up survival anxiety and try to scare people: "Change now or there'll be layoffs!" Schein points out that this only makes people more anxious and more defensive, and they cling harder to the old ways. The right move is to lower learning anxiety by giving learners psychological safety: a clear vision, real training, time and room to practice, permission to make mistakes, coaches and role models, and a say in designing the change.

Schein also stresses that culture change is rarely a wholesale overhaul. You use the parts of the culture that still work to change the one assumption standing in your way.

It changes how you look at things: resistance to change isn't stupidity or laziness. It's reasonable self-protection. What you need to take apart isn't people's resistance; it's the fear that makes resistance feel necessary.

7. Humble diagnosis: you can't survey culture, you have to dig it up together

Schein was always skeptical of measuring culture with questionnaires. A survey can only capture what people are aware of and willing to say, which is level two, espoused values. Level three, basic assumptions, is exactly what people aren't aware of.

His alternative is clinical. An outside consultant works with a group of insiders: first explaining the three-level model, then listing as many artifacts as possible, then listing the company's espoused values, then hunting for where the two don't match and asking what assumption could explain the contradiction. Outsiders can see what insiders have stopped noticing; insiders can explain meanings outsiders can't read. Culture only becomes visible in the conversation between the two.

And the diagnosis has to aim at a concrete business problem. Analyzing culture just to "understand the culture" tends to be both boring and useless.

The Spine of the Argument

The whole book reduces to one clean chain:

To survive, a group has to solve problems of external adaptation and internal integration → leaders propose solutions → if a solution works, it moves from personal belief to group consensus and then sinks below awareness as a basic assumption → those assumptions make up the culture and are reinforced and passed to newcomers through leaders' attention, rewards, and personnel decisions → culture stabilizes the organization, reduces anxiety, and supplies meaning → but when the environment changes, yesterday's winning assumptions become blind spots → changing them requires leaders to see them and to drive new learning while lowering learning anxiety.

Inside that chain is the book's central insight: culture's power and its danger are the same thing. It spares people from having to rethink everything each time, and so it also stops them from rethinking. Culture exists to tame the anxiety of uncertainty, which is why challenging it means making people face the anxiety it had been holding down. What sets leaders apart is that they're among the few people in an organization who can, and should, step outside the culture and look at it. Schein went so far as to say (paraphrased) that the only thing of real importance leaders do is create and manage culture.

Common Misreadings & Criticism

Common misreadings

  • "Culture is values." Just the opposite. Values are only level two. However well written, if they clash with the basic assumptions, they're wallpaper.
  • "A strong culture means a good company." Schein never says so. Culture isn't good or bad; it fits its environment or it doesn't. Kotter and Heskett's research in Corporate Culture and Performance (1992) also found that "strong" culture alone didn't predict long-term results. Cultures that could adapt to change did.
  • "Culture eats strategy for breakfast." The line is usually credited to Drucker, but nobody has found it in his writing, and it isn't Schein's either. Schein's view is more careful: culture isn't strategy's enemy. It's the condition for strategy taking hold, and strategy itself grows out of culture.

The strongest criticisms

  • The blind spot of the "integration" view. In Cultures in Organizations: Three Perspectives (1992), the organizational scholar Joanne Martin argued that culture can be studied from three angles. The integration perspective sees culture as shared and consistent across the organization. The differentiation perspective sees several conflicting subcultures, with consensus only inside each. The fragmentation perspective sees meaning itself as ambiguous, shifting, and situational, with no stable consensus at all. Schein's emphasis on "shared" makes him the textbook case of the integration view. He does discuss subcultures, but he tends to treat ambiguity and conflict as a culture that hasn't formed yet rather than as what culture really looks like.
  • Leader-centrism. Critics argue Schein makes founders and leaders too central, as if culture were something leaders manufacture. In practice culture also grows from the bottom up, out of employees, industries, nations, and professions, and leaders may control it far less than Schein implies.
  • The power problem in "managing culture." Critical scholars such as the Swedish organization theorist Mats Alvesson and the British scholar Hugh Willmott argue that managing culture is itself a form of control: no longer just governing behavior, but trying to shape employees' thoughts, feelings, and identities. Willmott's 1993 paper borrowed Orwell for its title: "Strength Is Ignorance; Slavery Is Freedom." Gideon Kunda's ethnography Engineering Culture (1992) documented how an American high-tech firm used a "strong culture" as normative control. Employees asked to give themselves over completely also developed irony and distance to protect themselves. Schein's framework leans toward the manager's toolkit view and says little about this ethical layer.
  • Testability. If basic assumptions are unconscious, they're hard to measure objectively, and different consultants may dig up different "assumptions." That makes Schein's theory hard to test quantitatively. The quantitative school, such as Hofstede's organizational culture research or Cameron and Quinn's Competing Values Framework, went the other way: questionnaires that break culture into comparable dimensions, trading depth for comparability.

Schein Beyond This Book

Read only Organizational Culture and Leadership and it's easy to file Schein under "culture analyst." The real thread through his whole career was how to genuinely help a person or an organization. Culture was just the wall he kept running into while trying.

Coercive persuasion. Schein's career began with a study of American prisoners repatriated after the Korean War. In Coercive Persuasion (1961) he analyzed the "thought reform" in the camps. It relied on no mysterious brainwashing technique, but on cutting prisoners off from their old social support, keeping them in constant uncertainty and guilt, and then offering approval and belonging through a new group. The study showed him that a person's beliefs are deeply embedded in the group relationships they belong to; to change the beliefs, you first have to loosen that web. His later ideas about unfreezing and learning anxiety have their roots here.

Process consultation. In his Process Consultation books, Schein distinguished three ways of helping: the expert model, where the client knows the problem and buys your answer; the doctor–patient model, where the consultant diagnoses and prescribes; and the process consultation model, where the consultant helps clients see for themselves what is happening and find their own solution. The first two often fail: clients don't necessarily know what the real problem is, and they won't really take medicine an outsider prescribed. A helper's first job isn't to give answers; it's to build a relationship in which the problem can be seen.

Humble inquiry. In Humble Inquiry (2013), written late in his life, he distilled that stance into a way of talking. In cultures built on hierarchy and on telling, American management culture especially, learn to ask real questions, ones you don't already know the answer to, and stay curious about the other person. He saw this as the precondition for subordinates daring to deliver bad news.

Career anchors. Schein followed MIT Sloan graduates for years and found that everyone gradually develops a core they won't give up: technical or functional competence, general management, autonomy and independence, security and stability, entrepreneurial creativity, service or dedication to a cause, pure challenge, or lifestyle integration. A career anchor isn't what you're good at; it's what you refuse to let go of when you're forced to choose. It's really culture theory mirrored at the individual level: people have their own basic assumptions too, and they only find them through experience.

Taken together, Schein's full picture is this: for people and organizations alike, what really drives behavior are assumptions laid down by experience that their owners cannot see, and the art of helping is to use humble inquiry and a safe relationship so the other side can see those assumptions for themselves.

Crossovers

  • Philosophy · Wittgenstein's hinge propositions: In On Certainty, Wittgenstein argued that some propositions work like the hinges a door turns on. All our doubting and testing turns around them, but they themselves go untested, such as "the world existed before I was born." Basic assumptions are an organization's hinges. The difference: Wittgenstein's hinges are close to universal, while Schein's are learned by one group from particular successes and can be changed.
  • Neuroscience · priors in predictive processing: Predictive processing holds that the brain keeps explaining sensory input with prior expectations, so small mismatches get absorbed by the prediction. Basic assumptions act like an organization's priors: signals that don't fit get explained away, as when DEC dismissed the personal computer as a toy. The difference: the brain updates its priors continuously from prediction error, while organizations often wait for a crisis.
  • Buddhism · seeds and habit energy in Yogācāra: The Yogācāra school holds that every action plants a seed in the storehouse consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna). Seeds build up into habit energies (vāsanā), which shape how we see the world the next time we act. That is the same loop as successful practice settling into assumption and steering the next judgment, and it too runs below awareness. The difference: Buddhism aims to see through and extinguish every habit energy, while Schein only wants to spot the one assumption blocking today's problem and swap in a better one.
  • Evolution · Crick's frozen accident: In 1968 Francis Crick proposed that the genetic code's specific mapping may have been an early accident. Once all life depended on it, any change was fatal, so it "froze." A founder's assumptions work the same way: they may not have been optimal, but the whole organization has grown around them.
  • Distributed systems · protocol ossification: Huge numbers of middleboxes on the internet, such as firewalls and NATs, assume TCP packets "should" look a certain way and drop new extensions. No standard ever wrote those defaults down, yet they locked evolution in place, and QUIC sidesteps them by running encrypted over UDP. Unspoken assumptions harden into a cage.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. Culture is a set of shared basic assumptions that a group learns, takes as true, and teaches to newcomers.

2. Culture has three levels: artifacts are visible but hard to read, values are easy to say but not always lived, and basic assumptions are invisible but decide everything.

3. To read a culture, find the crack where what's espoused and what's done don't match, then ask what assumption explains it.

4. Culture is sediment from past success: a method that keeps working slides from "let's try it" to "that's just how it's done."

5. Employees don't listen to what leaders say; they watch what leaders attend to, reward, and fire people for.

6. Two opposite cultures, DEC's argument and Ciba-Geigy's hierarchy, can both be right. Culture has fit, not virtue.

7. The assumptions that made a company succeed are often the very ones that keep it from seeing a new reality.

8. Change requires survival anxiety to exceed learning anxiety, and the way there is to lower learning anxiety, not to turn up the fear.

9. Culture change isn't a teardown: use the parts of the culture that still work to remove the one assumption in the way.

10. Culture and leadership are two sides of one coin: leaders who can't read their culture will be managed by it.
