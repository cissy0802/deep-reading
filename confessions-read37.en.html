Confessions

Confessiones · Augustine of Hippo · c. AD 397–400

中文 →

In one sentence

A bishop in his forties writes down the theft, the lust, the vanity and the intellectual dead ends of his first thirty years and hands the account to God — but what he actually accomplishes is the invention of a way of writing in which the inner life can be narrated, interrogated, and tried like a case at law: for the first time a person is written not as a role in a fate or a city, but as a depth that is opaque even to itself. And he asks two questions that have not stopped being asked in the sixteen centuries since — why can't I do the thing I want to do? and what, exactly, is time?

Where it sits

Augustine (354–430) was born in Roman North Africa and taught rhetoric at Carthage, Rome and Milan — the career track to power in his day. He converted to Christianity at thirty-two, became bishop of Hippo in his early forties, and died at seventy-six with the Vandals besieging the city. Confessions was written in his first years as bishop, about the man he had been a decade earlier. He is the figure who welded Greek philosophy — the Platonic line above all — to Christian faith; a thousand years of Western thought, and later the Reformation of Luther and Calvin, were still building on the foundation he laid.

The core claims

  • Human restlessness has a direction. Desire itself is not the flaw; the flaw is that all of it points at things too small to hold it. "You made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you" (Book 1) — the book's thesis and its diagnosis in one line.
  • Evil is not a thing; it is a hole. There is no entity called Evil at war with Good; evil is the absence of good, a will turning from a higher good to a lower one. This dismantles the good-versus-evil dualism he had believed for nine years.
  • The will fights itself, and cannot cure itself. "I command my hand and it moves; I command my will, and it refuses" — the sharpest psychological stroke in the book, and the root of his doctrine of grace.
  • Time is not in the world; it is in the mind. The past is gone, the future is not yet, the present has no thickness — so what are we measuring? His answer (time as a "stretching of the mind") is the book's purest philosophical achievement.

The core concepts, one by one

The word "confession" misleads you from the first page

The Latin confessio carries three senses, and English keeps only the narrowest. It is at once confession of sin (admitting what I did), confession of praise (admitting what God is), and confession of faith (admitting what I believe). So this is not a charge sheet but three acts happening in the same sentence: saying how bad I am is saying how good he is, because only by his goodness can I see my badness at all.

More important is the person it is written in. From first word to last the book addresses "you," and the addressee is God, not the reader. Reading Confessions means eavesdropping on a prayer. That is not a stylistic flourish; it sets the grain of the whole book — since the listener already knows everything, the author has no room to lie and no motive to plead his case. Every later kind of writing that digs inward is a secular version of this form; the difference is that Rousseau's Confessions is addressed to human beings, and so it argues for the defence on every page.

The restless heart (cor inquietum): the problem is not desire but misdirection

The judgment falls on page one: a human being comes with an appetite that will not switch off, and that appetite is how we were made. What goes wrong is never "you want," but "you have aimed it somewhere else."

His own first half-life demonstrates the point. Sex: he lived fifteen years with a woman and had a son by her, Adeodatus ("given by God"), then sent her back to Africa for an advantageous engagement — and immediately took someone else to fill the gap. He records it coldly: the wound did not heal, it scabbed over and festered. Reputation: he made his living teaching people to speak beautifully without needing to speak truly, and was proud of it; he remembers weeping as a schoolboy over Dido's suicide while feeling nothing at all about the death of his own soul — "I wept for Dido's death, and not for my own." Knowledge: at eighteen or nineteen Cicero's Hortensius (a lost exhortation to philosophy) set him alight, and from then on what he wanted was wisdom itself, not any particular book about it.

The modern usefulness of this diagnosis is enormous: it reclassifies "I am never satisfied" from a moral failing into a structural fact. You change jobs, cities, partners, goals; each time you think this is the one, and each time the same emptiness grows back. Augustine's reply is that you are not ungrateful — you are using finite things to fill a hole cut to an infinite size. You need not accept his answer (he calls that infinite God), but anyone who has lain awake asking "what am I actually chasing?" recognises the mechanism.

The pears: why a trivial bit of mischief deserves this much attention

Book 2 contains the book's most famous scene and the one most often dismissed as overwrought. At sixteen he and a gang raided a neighbour's pear tree at night — the pears were sour and unappetising; they stripped the tree, tasted a few, and threw the rest to the pigs.

He will not let it go, because the episode strips away every ordinary motive for a crime. Hunger? He had better pears at home. Greed? He had no intention of eating them. Precisely because there was no intelligible benefit, the act of wrongdoing is left standing alone and exposed — what I loved was not the fruit but the breaking, the fact that I had ruined something.

He offers two explanations, both sharp. The first is social: "alone I would never have done it." Wrongdoing here is a pleasure of complicity — a group doing together what none of them would do singly, the shared laughter being the whole reward. Anyone who has watched a pile-on online or in an office knows this one. The second cuts deeper: sin is a botched imitation of God's freedom. Every sin counterfeits some divine attribute — the lust for power counterfeits omnipotence, curiosity counterfeits omniscience, sloth counterfeits rest, and rule-breaking counterfeits being subject to nothing. What people want is the sensation of having the last word, even at the cost of wrecking themselves. So the deepest sin is not appetite but pride (superbia): not wanting bad things, but wanting to be the one who decides what "good" means.

Evil as privation (privatio boni): not defeating the darkness — revoking its status as a thing

To see how ruthless this move is, you need to know what it dismantles. Manichaeism was a fashionable dualist religion: two equally real first principles, Light and Darkness, locked in eternal war, the good in you being fragments of the Light and the evil a contamination of dark matter. Augustine was a Manichaean "hearer" (lay adherent) for nine years, because the story is so convenient — it lets you do wrong and stay calm about it: that wasn't me, that was the darkness in me.

He got out in two steps. Empirically: Faustus of Milevis, the great Manichaean teacher, finally arrived, and Augustine came loaded with astronomy questions — the Manichaean cosmology did not match the astronomy he had read — only to find a superb speaker with almost no learning, who frankly admitted he could not answer. Half his faith collapsed on the spot. Philosophically: he read "the books of the Platonists" (Neoplatonism, chiefly Plotinus in Latin translation) and found a new solution — whatever exists is, insofar as it exists, good; therefore "evil" is not an existing thing at all, but a gap. His analogy is medical: disease is not a substance added to a body but the absence of health; darkness is not a kind of light but the lack of it; the hole in a cloth is not made of cloth, because a hole is not a thing.

This accomplishes two things at once. ① It clears God (evil is not something he made, because evil is not a something). ② It puts the entire bill for wrongdoing back on the will — with no external dark force pushing you, evil can only come from a free will's own turning: away from a higher good (truth, other people) toward a lower one (pleasure, control). Note the precision of the definition: doing wrong is not choosing "the bad," it is choosing a smaller good and treating it as the highest one. The boy in the orchard was chasing a distorted good too — the taste of freedom, his friends' laughter. This is nearly the template of modern criminal psychology: nobody wants evil for its own sake; people just promote a secondary good to first place.

The divided will: the most modern chapter in the book

Book 8 is the heart of Confessions, and the first time in Western psychology that anyone dissected "I know exactly what to do and still don't do it" all the way down. The opening move is a superb observation: the mind commands the hand and the hand obeys instantly; the mind commands itself, and meets refusal. The same "I" is better at directing a body than at directing itself. His conclusion is that this is not two wills, one good and one evil, at war (that would be Manichaeism again) but that a single will is itself incomplete: it does not will wholly. "It is the same mind that commands and that is commanded; it commands because it wills, and it does not obey because it does not wholly will." He names the state non plena voluntas — a will that is not full.

And here falls the most honest and funniest prayer in the book: "Grant me chastity and continence, but not yet." You know precisely how that feels: I'll quit smoking tomorrow, start training next week, resign once this project ships. You do want the change — you want a future self to have it, while the present self is spared the bill.

Better still is the mechanism of addiction he lays out, a four-step staircase (Book 8, paraphrased): a perverse will produces lust; lust obeyed becomes habit; habit unresisted becomes necessity.

How freedom locks itself down one link at a time: at every step you still had a choice; by the last one you don't — and you forged every link yourself.

His image is exactly that: a chain. The enemy held my will, and out of my will made a chain and bound me with it. What matters is that the chain is made entirely of my own freedom — nobody tied you up; you walked that road willingly every day until the road became the only one you know how to walk. This beats most later theories of willpower: the danger of a habit is not that it forces you, but that it abolishes the question of whether to do it at all.

The break comes in a Milan garden: at thirty-two, weeping under a tree, he hears a child's voice on the other side of the wall singing over and over, "take up and read, take up and read (tolle lege, tolle lege)." He goes back, opens Paul's letters, and his eye falls on the end of Romans 13 — not in revelling and drunkenness, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ and make no provision for the flesh. The instant he finished reading, he says, it was as if a settling light poured into his heart and every shadow of doubt dispersed. Notice that the point of his account is not "I figured it out" but "I no longer had to figure it out myself": the barrier he had spent a whole book failing to will his way over collapsed the moment he stopped trying to will his way over it.

Grace (gratia): one prayer that lit a thousand years of theological civil war

Follow the previous section out and the conclusion is nearly forced: if what is sick is willing itself, it cannot be cured by willing harder — a hand cannot twist its own joint. The cure has to come from outside, and that is grace: not a reward, not earned, given for nothing. In Book 10 he writes the prayer that would detonate everything: "Give what you command, and command what you will (da quod iubes et iube quod vis)." If you want chastity of me, give me chastity first, and then I can. The bomb inside it: a human being lacks even the power to become better.

By Augustine's own account, the British monk Pelagius, hearing that line quoted in Rome, could not bear it and nearly came to blows over it. Pelagius' position is a very attractive one: if God commands you to be good, that proves you can be; otherwise the command would be unjust. The Pelagian controversy is one of the most consequential arguments in Western thought, because the real question is: can a person make himself better? Augustine won (Pelagianism was condemned as heresy), and what he won with — original sin, grace, predestination — became the gunpowder of Luther, Calvin and Protestantism at large.

How it changes the way you see things: strip out the theological vocabulary and the argument is still exactly where it was. "You can do it if you want it badly enough" is Pelagian. "Willpower is a finite resource; rely on environment and institutions, not resolve" is Augustinian. Where contemporary behavioural science has ended up — don't lean on willpower, redesign the environment — is structurally on Augustine's side: rescue has to come from outside the will. The only difference is that he called the outside God, and we call it defaults, commitment devices and good institutional design.

Memory (memoria): the discovery that you contain a space too large to contain you

Book 10 is the hinge. The story is told; he turns and asks who he is now — and to find the present self he can only go into memory. Hence the famous exclamation: memory as "wide fields and vast palaces," where I can summon one image at will while others crowd in unbidden and have to be brushed aside before I find what I wanted.

The finding has three layers, each deeper. First: memory holds more than pictures — mathematical truths, the rules of music, abstract concepts, things that never entered through eye or ear, are in there too. Second: it holds emotions themselves — I can recall sadness without being sad, the way a stomach can recall a taste without eating. Third, and dizzying: memory holds forgetting. How can I remember what I have forgotten? If it truly is not in memory, I could not even say "I forgot"; but if it is there, in what sense have I forgotten it?

Down that road he arrives at the famous line: "I have become a question to myself" (quaestio mihi factus sum). Before him, "know thyself" meant identifying your place in the order of the cosmos; after him, the self is a depth to be excavated, one that may contain things you do not know about yourself. Freud's remark fifteen centuries later — that we are not master in our own house — is a lineal descendant. And the loveliest turn in the book comes here: he searches memory everywhere for God and cannot find him, then realises God is not an exhibit stored in memory but the thing that makes memory possible. Out of that come the two most beautiful sentences in the book: "more inward to me than my innermost self and higher than my highest" — go all the way in and you find that the innermost thing is not you; and "Late have I loved you, beauty so old and so new! You were within me, and I was outside, and there I searched for you."

Time: Book 11, the purest philosophy in the book

Then comes the book that even an atheist like Russell conceded was a genuine philosophical contribution. It starts from a needling question: what was God doing before he made heaven and earth? A joke answer was in circulation — preparing hell for people who ask such questions — and Augustine explicitly refuses to use it. His serious answer forces out the whole book: "before" has no meaning here, because time itself was created along with the world. Asking what came before creation is like asking what lies north of the North Pole. Eternity is not a very, very long time; it is a mode of being with no before and after, all of it present at once — God does not live long, he does not undergo one-thing-after-another.

Then he turns the blade on time itself: "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I wish to explain it to someone who asks, I do not know." Where is the difficulty? The past no longer exists; the future does not yet; and if the present had any length it could be divided into a past part and a future part, until nothing is left but a point with no thickness. Two of the three do not exist and the third has no duration — so when we say "that piece of music was long," what are we measuring? His solution moves time out of the world and into the mind: strictly speaking there are not three times but three presents.

All three exist only in the mind, and all three are present-tense — what you measure is never the event but the impression it leaves in you.

His proof case is startlingly plain and completely decisive: recite a poem you know by heart (his example is a psalm). Before you begin, the whole thing sits in expectation; with each syllable a piece shifts from expectation into memory; attention is the pass it travels through. Halfway, expectation is shrinking and memory lengthening, while the whole remains intact in you the entire time. So what you measure is never the poem, but the stretched-out impression of it in your mind. He names the state distentio animi. The pun is exquisite: it means both a stretching-out (time is the length the mind is drawn to) and a being-scattered — we live in time precisely because we are dispersed, unfinished; eternity is being drawn out no longer, gathered wholly into the present.

Why this book changed everything after it: it converts time from an objective quantity (marks on a clock) into an achievement of consciousness. Husserl on internal time-consciousness, Heidegger on the temporality of Dasein, Ricœur's Time and Narrative all begin by going back to Book 11. That you can today take for granted that memory rewrites itself, expectation distorts, and time's speed depends on attention, is because Augustine in AD 400 put time inside the mind rather than in the cosmos.

The argument in outline

The thirteen books look like two books stapled together (nine of autobiography, four of philosophy and Genesis), and readers complain that the second half wanders off. In fact it is a single line:

  • Books 1–9 | how a soul scatters and returns: infant appetite → the pear theft (motiveless wrongdoing) → lust and rhetorical vanity in Carthage → nine Manichaean years (outsourcing evil to matter) → the career in Rome and Milan → Ambrose's preaching, which teaches him to read the Old Testament allegorically and breaks the deadlock of literalism → Neoplatonism, which hands him the key of evil-as-privation but no strength to use it → "take up and read" in the garden → baptism → the conversation at the harbour of Ostia, standing at a window with his mother Monica shortly before her death.
  • Book 10 | who am I now: the turn into memory — the self as a space that holds the world but cannot hold itself; "I have become a question to myself"; plus a brutally honest audit of which appetites — sex, food, curiosity, the pleasure of being praised — are still biting him.
  • Books 11–13 | time and creation: if the past exists only as memory, he must first settle what time is; and since time arrived with creation, he turns to the opening line of Genesis and reads creation, time and the heart's homecoming as one and the same event.

So the whole book argues one thing: how a scattered person (distentio) is gathered. The narrative demonstrates the scattering, Book 10 audits its present state, and Books 11–13 explain its metaphysical root and point to the direction of gathering. See that and the complaint about the last four books evaporates: they are not an appendix, they are the explanation of the first nine.

Misreadings & honest criticism

  • Misreading one: treating it as autobiography. It is a prayer, not a memoir; it is indifferent to documentary completeness (he barely explains how he became a bishop) and interested only in what happened to this soul. The French scholar Pierre Courcelle argued in 1950 that the garden scene bears clear marks of literary construction and need not be verbatim reportage. That is not to call him a liar — he is retelling, a decade later and with the eyes of a man who has worked it out, the story of a man who had not — but readers should not mistake it for footage.
  • Misreading two: that he loathes the body. What he loathes is losing control; his target is "I can't even govern myself," not flesh as such, and on his own metaphysics the body is created and therefore good. But that is the limit of the defence: his later linking of original sin to sexual generation left the West with a very deep bodily shame.
  • The strongest opposing case: Julian of Eclanum. This Pelagian bishop fought the elderly Augustine for years, and his charges are savage: you never actually left Manichaeism — you merely renamed "dark matter" as "original sin," so that people are still born carrying a badness they did not do; making an infant guilty because of Adam makes God unjust; and calling marriage and childbearing the conveyor belt of sin slanders what God himself made. The blow has never been fully parried.
  • The woman has no name. The partner he lived with for fifteen years and had a child by is never named in the book; her return to Africa and her vow never to marry again occupy a handful of lines, and even those lines keep the camera on Augustine's own wound. A man who excavated his interior life this far shows almost no curiosity about the actual person beside him — the most glaring blind spot in the book.
  • On historical consequences: Elaine Pagels, in Adam, Eve, and the Serpent, argues that Augustine's reading of the Fall rewrote what had been an early Christian story about freedom into a story about inherited guilt and necessary authority, shaping Western views of sex, will and the legitimacy of rule ever since — contested, but worth knowing. Nietzsche, for his part, read Confessions with disgust, finding Augustine's psychology vulgar and his self-abasing piety a specimen of slave morality (paraphrased) — amusingly, what he attacks is what he inherited most: reducing morality to psychological motive is a habit Augustine started. One last caution: many historians of philosophy (Russell is the type) will grant only Book 11 the status of "real philosophy," but that analysis cannot be cleanly detached from the theology — it was forced out precisely by the question of how an eternal being creates time.

The Augustine outside this book

Read only Confessions and you get the introspective Augustine while missing the one who shaped Western politics and its picture of human nature. Three additions:

① The City of God (413–426): two cities. The occasion was the sack of Rome by the Goths in 410, which pagans blamed on the empire's conversion to Christianity. Augustine's reply redraws all of history: humanity has always belonged to two interwoven "cities" — the earthly city built by "love of self to the contempt of God," the City of God by "love of God to the contempt of self" (paraphrased). These are not code for church and state but two orientations of love, permanently mixed in this world and indistinguishable to the eye. The political consequence is enormous: every worldly empire, Christian ones included, is stripped of sanctity — the legitimate business of a state is a limited peace, not salvation. He adds a line with a barb in it: without justice, what are kingdoms but gangs of robbers? — followed by the story of the pirate before Alexander the Great: I raid with one small ship and am called a bandit; you raid with a whole fleet and are called an emperor (paraphrased).

② On the Trinity: the mind as an image of God. He offers a far-reaching analogy: the mind contains a three-in-one structure — memory (memoria), understanding (intellegentia), will/love (voluntas) — mutually containing, inseparable, and yet not three things. The by-product outlived the analogy: will and love are raised to the same rank as cognition. In the Greek tradition the core of a person is reason; after Augustine, who you are depends on what you love.

③ The anti-Pelagian works: original sin and predestination. Late in life he pushed the private experience of Book 8 into systematic doctrine: after Adam human nature is damaged; the will survives but cannot save itself; salvation rests on God's free election (predestination), not on merit. This is his heaviest, most contested and most consequential stroke — the direct source of Luther's justification by faith and the template for Calvin's predestination — and it leaves a question that still stings: if salvation is already determined, what is human effort for? Without this piece you meet only a candid self-examiner and miss the thinker who wrote "you cannot do it yourself" into the base coat of Western Christianity.

The book in ten sentences

① "You made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you." Desire is not the disease; misdirection is — you keep filling an infinite-sized hole with finite things.

② "Confession" is three acts at once — of sin, of praise, of faith — and the whole book is a prayer addressed to God, which is why its author has no room to lie and no motive to plead.

③ The pear theft is worth dwelling on precisely because there was nothing in it: strip out every motive and what remains is love of the transgression itself — the deepest sin is not appetite but pride, wanting to be the one who defines "good."

④ "Alone I would never have done it" — wrongdoing is often a pleasure of complicity: a group doing together what none of them would do singly.

⑤ Evil is not a thing but a hole in the good, as darkness is not a kind of light and a hole is not made of cloth. So the bill goes back to the will — nobody wants evil for its own sake; people just promote a smaller good to first place.

⑥ "Grant me chastity and continence, but not yet." The mind commands the hand and it moves; it commands itself and meets refusal, because it does not wholly will.

⑦ A perverse will produces lust, lust obeyed becomes habit, habit unresisted becomes necessity (paraphrased): every link in that chain was forged out of your own freedom.

⑧ If what is sick is willing itself, the will cannot cure itself — "give what you command." In today's terms: stop betting on resolve; change the environment and the institutions. Rescue must come from outside the will.

⑨ "I have become a question to myself." The self stops being a position in the cosmos and becomes a depth you cannot see the bottom of — "more inward to me than my innermost self."

⑩ "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I wish to explain it, I do not know." There are only three presents — memory, attention, expectation; what you measure is never the event but its impression in you. Time is the stretching of the mind (distentio animi): we live in time because we are scattered.
