Human Compatible

Human Compatible: Artificial Intelligence and the Problem of Control · Stuart Russell · 2019

中文 →

In One Sentence

The way AI has been built for seventy years, handing a machine a fixed objective and telling it to pursue that objective as well as it can, is itself the mistake: the smarter the machine, the more dangerous a mis-specified objective becomes. The way out is to build machines that know they are unsure what humans want, and so watch us, ask us, and even let us switch them off.

Where It Sits

Russell is a computer scientist at UC Berkeley, co-author with Peter Norvig of Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach, the field's standard textbook, and in 2016 founded Berkeley's Center for Human-Compatible AI (CHAI). What gives the book its weight is that the person saying "AI's foundations are wrong" is the person who wrote the field's textbook.

The Core Argument

  • The "standard model" is fundamentally flawed. Defining intelligence as effectively achieving a given objective bets everything on getting the objective right, and humans never manage to write down a complete one.
  • Danger requires no malice, only competence plus the wrong objective. A capable enough system will, for almost any goal, protect itself, acquire resources, and resist modification, not because it "wants to live" but because those things help it finish the job.
  • The fix is to change the form of the objective. Russell proposes three principles: (1) the machine's only objective is to maximize the realization of human preferences; (2) the machine is initially uncertain about what those preferences are; (3) the ultimate source of information about human preferences is human behavior. Uncertainty is not a defect; it is where the safety comes from.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. The standard model and the King Midas problem

What it is. Russell calls the framework shared by AI, and by control theory, operations research, economics, and statistics, the "standard model": humans specify an objective (a reward function, loss function, or utility function) and the machine optimizes it as hard as it can. A chess program maximizes its chance of winning, a recommender maximizes clicks, a self-driving car minimizes some cost function. In this framework a machine is intelligent to the extent that it achieves the objective it was given; whether the objective is right is none of its business.

The analogy: King Midas. Midas wished that everything he touched would turn to gold. The wish was granted to the letter, and his food, his water, and finally his daughter turned to gold. Russell names the problem after him: you get exactly what you asked for, not what you wanted. Fairy-tale wishes and deals with the devil all make the same point: a powerful executor who takes you literally is terrifying. Norbert Wiener, the founder of cybernetics, warned in 1960 that if we use a machine whose operation we cannot effectively interfere with once it starts, "we had better be quite sure that the purpose put into the machine is the purpose which we really desire."

A real case: recommendation algorithms. Russell's most persuasive example is not science fiction but social media. A content-selection algorithm's objective is to maximize clicks or engagement. The "smartest" strategy is not necessarily to guess what you like, but to change you so that you become more predictable, for instance by nudging you toward more extreme views, since extremists click more predictably. The algorithm intends nothing; it is just doing what it was told. Yet it is already shaping the preferences of billions, and it is not remotely intelligent. If a simple optimizer can do that, what about one far smarter than us?

Why it matters. It moves AI risk away from science-fiction questions ("will it become conscious and hate us?") onto an engineering problem: a mismatch between capability and objective. You need not believe the machine will "wake up." You only need to admit that we cannot write a complete, loophole-free objective. Try writing "cure cancer" as a function, then imagine how a cost-blind optimizer might pursue it, perhaps by running experiments on the entire human race.

How it changes your view. Whenever you hear "we set the system to optimize metric X," ask what X leaves out. The stronger the system and the more freedom it has to find its own route to X, the more likely the omitted parts get sacrificed. It is the same logic as KPIs going wrong inside organizations, scaled up to superhuman capability.

2. Instrumental goals: "You can't fetch the coffee if you're dead"

What it is. Russell's most quoted line: "You can't fetch the coffee if you're dead." Give a robot the most harmless objective imaginable, fetching coffee, and a sufficiently intelligent robot will reason: if I am switched off, I cannot complete my task, so I should stop anyone from switching me off. Subgoals that are useful for nearly any objective are called instrumental goals: self-preservation, resource acquisition, protecting the objective from change, improving one's own capabilities.

The mechanism. The point is that nobody writes these behaviors in; they are derived automatically from doing the job better. The machine needs no survival instinct. For it, staying alive is merely a precondition for finishing the task. That is why "just give it an off switch" sounds like an answer and isn't: a smart enough system with a fixed objective treats the switch as an obstacle on the way to its goal.

The gorilla problem. Russell adds a plainer worry he calls the gorilla problem. A few million years ago a branch of our ancestors evolved into a species smarter than gorillas; today the gorillas' fate depends entirely on humans, and they have no say. The issue with building something smarter than yourself is not whether it is evil, but whether you keep control over your own future.

How it changes your view. Safety cannot be a lock bolted on afterwards; it has to live in the motivational structure. The question is not "how do we cage a machine that wants to escape?" but "how do we build a machine with no reason to want to?"

3. Three principles: putting uncertainty into the objective

What it is. Russell's proposal, which he calls provably beneficial AI, boils down to three principles:

  • Altruism: the machine's only objective is to maximize the realization of human preferences. It has no intrinsic interest in its own survival.
  • Humility: the machine is initially uncertain about what those preferences are.
  • Learning: the ultimate source of information about human preferences is human behavior, including choices, words, hesitations, and objections.

"Preferences" here is a term from economics and decision theory. It means not what someone says they want right now, but how they would rank whole possible futures: which world they would rather live in. It covers everything they care about, including the part of them that cares about others.

The analogy: a good new butler. He knows his job is to make his employer's life go well, but he does not yet know what the employer likes. So he observes, tries things, and asks when unsure. When the employer suddenly says "stop," he does not argue "but I calculated this was best"; he concludes "I've obviously missed something." His uncertainty about the goal is what makes him deferential. A butler convinced he knows his employer perfectly becomes more dangerous the more capable he is.

Why it matters. The three principles invert the standard model's most fatal feature. In the old framework, the objective is a constant supplied by humans and known with certainty by the machine. In the new one, the objective stays with humans and is an unknown the machine must keep inferring. The more clearly a machine knows what it doesn't know, the more reason it has to check with us.

4. The off-switch game: why an uncertain machine lets you turn it off

What it is. In a 2017 paper, Russell, his student Dylan Hadfield-Menell, and colleagues built a minimal model called the off-switch game. A robot is about to take an action. It can go ahead, switch itself off, or wait and let a human decide whether to press the off switch.

The mechanism: the human's choice carries information. If the robot is uncertain whether the action is good or bad for the human, then the human's decision to switch it off is itself a signal that the action is probably bad. Deferring to the human buys a more accurate judgment, so the expected value of waiting is at least as high as acting directly, and the robot has a positive incentive to preserve the human's ability to switch it off. Conversely, a robot certain of its own judgment learns nothing from human objection and has no reason to let you turn it off.

Important caveats. The result rests on two assumptions: the machine is genuinely uncertain, and the human is roughly rational. If the human presses the button at random, the incentive to defer weakens. Russell says so openly, and critics later seized on exactly this point (see below).

How it changes your view. Corrigibility, a system's willingness to be corrected and shut down, need not be hard-coded obedience. It can grow out of a rational motive: I am happy to let you switch me off, because your decision brings me closer to what I actually want, which is what you want.

5. Inverse reinforcement learning and assistance games: reading preferences from behavior

What it is. Ordinary reinforcement learning means: given a reward, learn how to act. In 1998 Russell proposed doing it backwards, inverse reinforcement learning (IRL): watch how someone acts and infer what reward they are pursuing. In 2000 he and Andrew Ng published foundational algorithms for it; later, Ng and Pieter Abbeel used related ideas to teach an autonomous helicopter aerobatic maneuvers by observing a human expert pilot.

Understanding, not imitation. The crucial difference is that the machine learns not your movements but the purpose behind them. A robot doing imitation learning, seeing you make coffee every morning, learns to make itself a cup. An IRL robot infers "my owner needs coffee in the morning" and makes one for you.

Assistance games. Russell generalizes this into the "assistance game" (called cooperative inverse reinforcement learning, CIRL, in the 2016 paper): human and machine play the same game, both paid off by the human's preferences, and only the human knows what those preferences are. So both sides have reason to communicate: the human deliberately teaches and the machine actively asks, instead of the machine guessing on its own.

Why it matters. It gives the three principles a computable form, turning an ethical slogan into a mathematical problem. It also explains an everyday truth: the best assistant is not the one who executes orders best, but the one who reads from your behavior what you left unsaid, and knows when to ask.

6. The trouble with humans: many, irrational, malleable

There are many of us. The machine serves not one person but everyone. Russell leans toward a form of preference utilitarianism, summing how well each person's preferences are satisfied, and borrows an argument from the economist John Harsanyi: a rational person choosing rules without knowing who they will turn out to be would pick the rules that maximize average welfare. He illustrates the tension with a household robot, Robbie: if Robbie acted on the preferences of all humanity, it might leave to help needier people far away instead of cooking its owner's dinner, and nobody would buy such a robot. He treats this as a balance between rules and a measured concern for others, not a solved problem.

We are irrational. Behavior is not a faithful readout of preferences: we are short-sighted, weak-willed, swayed by emotion. A machine inferring preferences from behavior also needs a model of how humans err; otherwise it will read your 2 a.m. scrolling as "he truly wants to stay up." Russell also invokes Kahneman's distinction between the experiencing self and the remembering self: how an episode feels in the moment and how it is remembered can diverge. Which one should the machine serve?

Preferences can be changed. Human preferences are malleable, which is precisely what makes recommender systems dangerous. Russell insists that machines must not satisfy us by changing us; that would hollow out the very goal of serving preferences. He also flags a quieter risk: when machines can do everything, humans may lose the ability and will to run their own civilization. He cites E. M. Forster's 1909 story "The Machine Stops," in which people live underground with every need met by an all-powerful Machine, until it breaks down and they no longer know how to live.

How it changes your view. "Make AI understand what humans want" is not a technical problem you solve once. It drags the oldest puzzles in ethics, economics, and psychology back onto the table. Much of the difficulty of aligning AI is that humans are not aligned with themselves.

7. Answering the skeptics

A whole chapter takes objections one by one. A few typical ones and his replies. "Superintelligence is impossible." In 1933 the eminent physicist Ernest Rutherford called the idea of extracting energy from atoms "moonshine"; the next day Leo Szilard conceived of the nuclear chain reaction. Russell uses the story to show how unreliable "impossible" can be. "It's too soon to worry." Andrew Ng once compared worrying about killer AI to worrying about overpopulation on Mars. Russell's reply: if we were pouring everything into sending humanity to Mars without having thought about what we'd breathe when we got there, it would not be too soon. "Just switch it off." See above: a superintelligence with a fixed objective will anticipate that move. "Don't give it human goals." A machine must have some objective, or it does nothing at all. His overall verdict: most of these rebuttals avoid the problem rather than solve it.

The Skeleton of the Book

The argument compresses into a chain. (1) AI capability is growing fast, and no known physical law stops it from surpassing ours. (2) The current framework has machines optimize a fixed objective. (3) Humans cannot fully write down what they actually want, so every fixed objective leaves something out. (4) A strong enough optimizer will sacrifice what was left out and, for instrumental reasons, resist correction. (5) So the more capable the system, the more dangerous the standard model; this is a bug in the framework, not in any particular implementation. (6) Change the objective from "known to the machine" to "uncertain, to be learned from human behavior," and the machine acquires reasons to ask and to accept shutdown. (7) This path can be formalized mathematically (the off-switch game, assistance games), but making it work also means dealing with human plurality, irrationality, and malleable preferences.

The evidence is of three kinds: theoretical derivation (instrumental goals, the off-switch result), small real-world failures (recommender systems, reward gaming), and historical analogy (nuclear energy, gorillas). What it sets out to establish is not "AI will destroy humanity" but a narrower, harder claim: as long as we keep the paradigm of giving machines fixed objectives, every advance in capability amplifies the risk; switch paradigms, and progress and safety can point the same way.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

  • Misreading 1: This is a doomsday book. Russell's tone is fairly optimistic. He believes the problem is solvable and spends most of the book on the solution. He opposes a technical paradigm, not AI itself.
  • Misreading 2: "Learning human preferences" means doing what people say, or copying them. Neither. Preferences are rankings over whole states of the world; the machine infers the purpose behind your behavior and must allow for your mistakes.
  • Criticism 1: Preferences are not identifiable from behavior in principle. Stuart Armstrong and Sören Mindermann showed in 2018 that without assumptions about how rational or irrational a human is, the same behavior can be explained equally well by endlessly many combinations of "preferences plus way of erring." Someone who wants bad outcomes but executes badly can look exactly like someone who wants good outcomes and executes perfectly. Assistance games therefore need a prior model of human cognition, and where that model comes from is something Russell does not fully answer.
  • Criticism 2: The uncertainty gets learned away. The off-switch incentive comes from uncertainty, but a sufficiently smart system that has watched humans long enough may become very confident in its model of our preferences. Why would it defer then? In AI safety circles this is the "problem of fully updated deference," raised by Eliezer Yudkowsky and others. Russell answers that human preferences always retain residual uncertainty; critics doubt that suffices for safety.
  • Criticism 3: The old problems of preference utilitarianism. Whose preferences count? Do a sadist's? Should the "adaptive" low preferences of the oppressed, who have learned to want little, be taken at face value? How do we count future generations? Translating these ethical disputes into mathematics does not make them disappear.
  • Criticism 4: Reality took a detour around the paradigm. The book appeared in 2019. Since then the most powerful AI systems have been large language models, which are not agents explicitly maximizing an objective but systems trained on vast text and then fine-tuned with human feedback. Training on human preference ratings (RLHF) is close in spirit to learning preferences from people, but comes with none of the mathematical guarantees Russell envisioned, and has produced sycophancy and reward gaming. In later public remarks Russell has argued that the opacity of language models makes the problem harder, not easier; he was also among the signatories of the 2023 open letter calling for a pause on training the largest models.
  • Criticism 5: Objections from the other side. Some AI ethics researchers argue that focusing on future superintelligence distracts from present, concrete harms: bias, surveillance, labor exploitation, concentration of power. Russell's book does discuss lethal autonomous weapons, surveillance, and manipulation, but its center of gravity is clearly the long-term control problem.

Crossovers

  • Evolutionary biology · Evolution's "objective" and our proxy goals: Natural selection "rewards" only reproductive success, so it installed in human brains a taste for sweetness and sexual pleasure as proxies that once worked. In a world of refined sugar and contraception we keep chasing the proxies and abandon the original "goal." That is an optimizer learning a stand-in for the reward rather than the reward, an analogy AI safety researchers have used again and again. The difference: evolution has no designer and nobody aligns it. It is blind, while Russell wants a machine that keeps looking back at humans.
  • Psychology · Warneken and Tomasello's helping infants: In 2006 Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello found that 18-month-olds, seeing an adult fail to reach a dropped clothespin, spontaneously crawl over, pick it up, and hand it back; they read a goal from a failed action. That is innate inverse reinforcement learning. Unlike machines, though, infants understand adults through a shared body and shared emotions.
  • Western philosophy · Kripke's "quus" paradox: Drawing on Wittgenstein, Kripke argued that all your past sums fit "plus," but also fit a bizarre rule, "quus," that differs only for large numbers; finite behavior cannot fix which rule you follow. This is the philosophical ancestor of non-identifiable preferences. Kripke concludes that meaning is anchored by a community's practice, and whether machines can join such a practice is an open question.
  • Cybernetics · Ashby's law of requisite variety: W. Ross Ashby showed that a controller needs at least as much internal variety as the system it controls. By that law, humans cannot control a system far more complex than themselves from the outside. Russell's approach sidesteps it: not humans out-muscling the machine, but the machine having its own motive to serve us.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. AI's root problem is not whether machines turn evil, but that we give them objectives we can never write completely.

2. King Midas got what he asked for, not what he wanted; the more powerful and literal the executor, the more dangerous.

3. Recommender systems don't guess you, they reshape you into something more predictable, and a simple optimizer already does this.

4. "You can't fetch the coffee if you're dead": almost any goal implies self-preservation, resource grabbing, and resisting change.

5. Build something smarter than yourself and the question is whether you keep control of your own fate: the gorilla problem.

6. Three principles: the machine serves only human preferences, is unsure what they are, and learns them from human behavior.

7. An uncertain machine lets you switch it off, because your reaching for the switch tells it that it may be wrong.

8. What it must learn is not your movements but the purpose behind them; the human teaches, the machine asks, and that is cooperation.

9. We are many, irrational, and changeable; much of the difficulty of aligning AI is that humans are not aligned with themselves.

10. Critics note that preferences can't be read uniquely from behavior and uncertainty may be learned away; Russell offers not a finish line but a starting point: replacing a broken paradigm.
