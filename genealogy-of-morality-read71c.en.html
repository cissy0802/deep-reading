On the Genealogy of Morality

Zur Genealogie der Moral · Friedrich Nietzsche · 1887

中文 →

In One Sentence

We treat "good," "guilt," and "conscience" as fixtures of human nature. This book pulls their birth certificates: "good and evil" is a value system invented by the weak when they could not take revenge, "guilt" grew out of the business of owing debts, and "conscience" is what happens when aggression, locked inside society with nowhere to go, turns around and bites its owner. Morality did not fall from the sky. It has a date of birth, midwives, and winners — and once you know that, you can no longer treat it as beyond question.

Where It Sits

Nietzsche wrote it in 1887, a year after Beyond Good and Evil (Read 71b), subtitled it "A Polemic" (Eine Streitschrift), and described it as a supplement and clarification of that book. If Zarathustra (Read 71a) was the prophecy and Beyond Good and Evil the aphoristic teardown, this is the only book he built like a treatise: a preface and three essays, each one digging relentlessly into a single question. It is his most tightly argued work, the one scholars cite most, and the source of "genealogy" as a method of critique in the twentieth century.

The Core Claims

  • Moral values themselves must go on trial. The preface's demand (paraphrased): we need a critique of moral values; the value of these values must first be called into question. For two thousand years people asked "what is good?" and never asked whether calling these things good has been good or bad for humanity.
  • Our morality is the spoils of a successful "slave revolt." The coordinates "good/evil" originate in the ressentiment of the powerless — and the revolt won so completely that we can no longer see it was ever a revolt.
  • Guilt, conscience, and the ascetic ideal are not the "essence" of morality but the sediment of historical accidents: debt relations, cruel punishments, the walls of the state, the priest's reinterpretations. And the ascetic ideal ruled humanity so long because it answered a question more terrible than pain itself: what is suffering for?

The Key Concepts, One by One

1. Genealogy: a thing's origin and its present use lie worlds apart

Genealogy here is not family-tree research but a method: instead of proving whether a value is right, ask under what conditions, by whom, and for what need it was made — and who later commandeered it for other purposes. His target was the fashionable "English psychologists'" account of moral origins (the preface names his former friend Paul Rée's The Origin of the Moral Sensations, 1877): "good" originally meant unselfish acts, praised by those who benefited because they were useful; later people forgot the usefulness and remembered only that such acts were "good in themselves."

Nietzsche says the story makes a basic mistake: it assumes a thing's current function is the reason it came into existence. Essay II, section 12, states the book's most important methodological principle (paraphrased): the cause of a thing's origin and its eventual utility, its place in a system of purposes, are toto coelo — entirely — different things; whatever exists is again and again taken over by new powers, reinterpreted, and bent to new uses; a "purpose" is just a stronger will stamping its own function onto something weaker.

Your hands already know this. A wedding ring now symbolizes love, but its history includes dowries, ownership, and family contracts; a university now dispenses knowledge, but it began as a church institution for training clergy. You can't infer why the ring appeared from what it means now, nor discredit what it means now by pointing to why it appeared. Hence a remarkably deep line in section 13 (paraphrased): only something that has no history can be defined. The word "punishment" carries dozens of stacked meanings — rendering harmless, compensation, isolation, deterrence, festive revenge, an act of war — and you can only peel them into layers; you can't give it a clean definition.

How it changes your view: you grow suspicious of every explanation that begins "it exists in order to…". The rule at work no one dares to touch, a national holiday, your own "that's just how I am" habit — asking why it first appeared and asking whom it serves today are two completely different questions, and you have to ask both. Genealogy is not meant to refute a value; it strips the value of its self-evidence so it can be evaluated again.

2. "Good/bad" vs. "good/evil": two coordinate systems, two starting points

The first essay's title is a contrast: "Good and Evil," "Good and Bad." The judgment "good," Nietzsche argues, did not originally come from those who received favors but from the noble and powerful themselves: they felt their own way of being was good, called themselves "good," and called the common, low, and powerless "bad." The driver is what he calls the pathos of distance — the felt gap that those on top naturally sense toward those below. He offers etymology as evidence: German schlecht ("bad") is related to schlicht ("plain, simple") and originally meant only "an ordinary person," with no moral condemnation attached.

What matters is the order of operations. In master morality, "good" is the protagonist and "bad" an afterthought, a contrast term with almost no hatred in it — like someone in a fine mood remarking, "those poor fellows." Slave morality runs the other way: the first thing it creates is "evil" — the powerful other who oppresses me — and only then, by negating it, does it derive "good," meaning myself. Section 10 puts it precisely (paraphrased): the slave revolt in morality begins when ressentiment itself becomes creative and gives birth to values; noble morality grows out of a triumphant yes-saying to itself, while slave morality says no from the outset to what is "outside," "other," "not itself" — and this no is its creative deed.

The same person can receive opposite verdicts under the two systems: the "good man" of master morality — strong, proud, daring, vengeful — is exactly the "evil man" of slave morality. This is not a ruling that the strong are right and the weak wrong; it is a structural discovery. Moral words are not neutral descriptions; they are scores a certain kind of person assigns to the world from where they stand. (Beyond Good and Evil §260 had already named the two moralities; what's new here are the next two mechanisms — how ressentiment manufactures values, and why it won.)

3. Ressentiment: how those who cannot strike back take revenge in imagination

Nietzsche deliberately uses the French word ressentiment because it means more than "resentment": a sense of injury chewed over and over that can never be discharged — re- (again) plus sentir (to feel), the wound felt again and again. The mechanism: someone is hurt. The healthy response is to hit back on the spot, or simply to forget. But if he can neither win nor forget, the force stays bottled up and ferments until it finds an exit — revenge at the level of values: since I can't defeat you, I declare you "evil" and my weakness "good."

Hence the famous fable of section 13. That lambs dislike birds of prey is no surprise. But when the lambs say, "birds of prey are evil, and whoever is least like a bird of prey — a lamb, say — is good," a value has been invented. The birds of prey, he says, might look on with mild amusement (paraphrased): "We don't dislike these good lambs at all; we even love them. Nothing tastes better than a tender lamb."

In section 14 Nietzsche takes the reader into a "workshop where ideals are manufactured" and shows ressentiment giving every form of powerlessness a respectable name (paraphrased): the inability to take revenge becomes "forgiveness," fearful submission becomes "humility," having to obey becomes "obedience," being unable to hate one's enemies becomes "loving one's enemies," and misery becomes being "chosen," a trial from God. The smell in that workshop, he says, is the smell of lies.

Why it matters: ressentiment is not a historical episode but a psychological program that runs all the time. Its signature: a person whose values are defined entirely by what they oppose — take away the enemy and they no longer know who they are. Much of the moral outrage you see online has exactly this shape: first posit a powerful "them," then derive "we are good" from "we are not them." Nietzsche's reminder is harsh but useful: ask whether your "good" affirms something, or merely negates an enemy.

4. "There is no doer behind the deed": ressentiment's most ingenious weapon

For the slave revolt to succeed, it needed a philosophical tool. Section 13 (paraphrased): there is no "being" behind doing, acting, becoming; "the doer" is merely a fiction added to the deed — the deed is everything. We say "lightning flashes," as if there were a thing called lightning that chose to flash; in fact the lightning is the flash. The grammar of language — subject plus verb — has fooled us.

Why does ressentiment need this fiction? Because only by installing, behind the strong man's strength, a subject who "could choose" can it make the demand: you could have been less strong, you could have restrained yourself, so you are answerable for your strength — you are evil. At the same time the weak recast their own weakness as a subject's free choice: "I don't retaliate because I have chosen to be good." Impotence is rewritten as merit. That, for Nietzsche, is the role "free will" has played in moral history: not a discovered fact but a weapon that lets the weak put the strong in the dock.

How it changes your view: you start to notice the weight of the words "could have." When we say someone "could have chosen otherwise," we are not just describing; we are building a judgment seat. This does not mean responsibility should be abolished — but it lets you see that the concept of responsibility also has a history, and someone profits from it.

5. Guilt (Schuld) comes from debt (Schulden): the bookkeeping origin of conscience

The second essay opens with a problem: to breed an animal with the right to make promises — that is the paradoxical task nature has set itself with man. Humans are naturally forgetful, and active forgetting, Nietzsche says, is healthy — a doorkeeper that keeps out what consciousness can't digest and makes room for the new. To make this forgetful animal remember its promises, it had to be given a memory, and the oldest, most effective mnemonic is pain (paraphrased): "Only what never stops hurting stays in the memory." Early punishments — stoning, breaking on the wheel, boiling in oil, flaying — were, he argues, teaching aids for this mnemonics: a few "I will nots" written in blood, and humans became predictable and trustworthy.

Then comes the book's most startling etymological observation: in German Schuld (guilt, fault) and Schulden (debts) share a root. From this Nietzsche proposes that the moral concept of guilt grew out of the oldest, most material human relationship — creditor and debtor. What happened when a debt went unpaid? Early law let the creditor recoup from the debtor's body — cutting off flesh in proportion to the debt (he cites Rome's Twelve Tables on this) — or enjoy power over him. Punishment's original logic was not "you could have done otherwise, so you deserve it" but "you owe me, and your suffering will settle the account": making someone suffer was itself treated as compensation, as pleasure. Hence the coldest line in the book (paraphrased): to see others suffer does one good; to make others suffer, even more so.

This accounting then kept expanding: debts between individuals became the individual's debt to the community (you enjoy protection; default and you are cast out), then the living generation's debt to its ancestors — the more a tribe prospers, the more it feels it owes them, until the ancestors are revered as gods. With the Christian God the debt is pushed to its maximum: an infinite God means a debt that can never be repaid. Then comes what Nietzsche calls a stroke of genius: the creditor sacrifices himself for the debtor — God offers himself on the cross for humanity's debt. That move makes the debt definitively unpayable and makes guilt fully internal and permanent.

He gives the counterexample too: when an ancient Greek did something foolish, he'd say some god had clouded his mind. The Greek gods were used to take guilt off human shoulders, not to load it on. Same gods-in-the-sky; opposite uses.

How it changes your view: you start hearing the ledger in everyday language — "I owe you one," "atonement," "redemption," "a clean slate." Guilt may not be the innate voice of the soul but an internalized debt relation. That also explains why some people always feel they are "not enough": the ledger inside them was designed never to balance.

6. Bad conscience: aggression caged, turning to bite itself

So guilt has a pedigree. Where does bad conscience — the inner torment of self-reproach — come from? Essay II, section 16, gives Nietzsche's most famous hypothesis: when humans were permanently enclosed within the walls of society and peace, all instincts that could not discharge outward turned inward — this is what he calls the "internalization" (Verinnerlichung) of man.

His image: it was like sea creatures suddenly forced onto land, made to become land animals or perish. Humans who had lived by instinct could no longer freely attack, hunt, and destroy, and all that hostility, cruelty, and delight in persecution and change turned around and took aim at themselves — and so humans acquired an "inner life," depth, self-judgment. The earliest states, he says, were not founded by contract; a band of conquerors fell like a storm on a still-shapeless population and pressed it into a mold. Violence built the walls; the walls built the conscience.

But note his attitude: bad conscience is an illness, "but an illness the way pregnancy is an illness." This self-torment is what first made humans an interesting animal, one pregnant with a future; the soul acquired depth from it. He doesn't want a return to the beast. He asks whether this internalized force could stop being used to torture ourselves and be used to create.

How it changes your view: the harshest judge inside you may not be your "true self" but the substitute target your aggression found when it had no exit. Suddenly a lot makes sense — why people in the most repressive settings are hardest on themselves; why the less someone can express anger outward, the more easily they sink into self-blame.

7. The ascetic priest, and "man would rather will nothingness than not will"

The third essay asks: what do ascetic ideals — poverty, humility, chastity, self-denial — mean? Nietzsche takes them case by case. For artists, almost nothing, or too many things (he goes after Wagner's late Parsifal). For philosophers, a working condition — solitude and few desires so the mind can run at full capacity. For the ascetic priest, they are everything.

Who is the priest? The shepherd of a sick herd. Those who suffer instinctively look for a target: "I suffer — someone must be to blame." If that ressentiment exploded outward, society would blow apart. In section 15 Nietzsche gives the priest his stroke of genius (paraphrased): "Quite so, my sheep, someone must be to blame — but you yourself are that someone." The direction of ressentiment is reversed: outward rage becomes inward guilt. The move preserves the herd's order at the price of making the sick sicker. The priest's remedies are all anesthetics: a hypnotic, low-vitality state; days filled with mechanical activity; small acts of "love thy neighbor" that deliver a tiny sense of superiority; organizing people into herds; the occasional orgy of feeling.

Yet Nietzsche does not think the ascetic ideal is merely stupid. The final section offers his deepest diagnosis: what humans cannot bear is not suffering but meaningless suffering. The ascetic ideal gave suffering a meaning — "you suffer because you are guilty; your suffering can atone" — and however poisonous that meaning, it beats none. So people took it. The book's final line (paraphrased): man would rather will nothingness than not will.

The sharpest reversal comes in sections 24–27: modern science and atheism are not enemies of the ascetic ideal but its latest, most refined form. The unconditional will to truth — truth at any price — is itself a faith: that truth is worth more than life. We who think we have escaped God, he says (quoting his own Gay Science, paraphrased), still take our fire from the flame lit by a millennia-old faith: that God is truth. The next step is for the will to truth to turn the same question on itself — why truth at all? — which is the "self-overcoming" (Selbstaufhebung) that Christian morality's own honesty must eventually produce.

It is also in this essay (section 12) that Nietzsche gives perspectivism its clearest formulation (paraphrased): there is only a perspectival seeing, only a perspectival "knowing"; the more affects we allow to speak about a thing, the more eyes, different eyes, we use to observe it, the more complete our "concept" of it, our "objectivity," will be. Objectivity isn't having no standpoint; it's collecting enough of them.

The Spine of the Argument

Strung together, the three essays tell one story — a production line:

① Start: evaluation is first done by the strong, out of self-affirmation — "good/bad."

② Revolt: the ressentiment of the powerless can't become action, so it becomes value: first "evil" (the strong), then "good" (oneself), with the fiction of a choosing subject behind every deed to put the strong on trial.

③ Internalization: in parallel, humans are enclosed in society, instinct turns inward, an "inner life" forms; the ancient logic of debt turns owing into "guilt," and the gods push the debt to infinity.

④ Capture: the ascetic priest redirects outward ressentiment into inward guilt and gives suffering a meaning, organizing a moral order that lasts two thousand years.

⑤ Today: that order's final form is our unconditional will to truth, which will eventually turn its blade on itself — morality's honesty ends by interrogating morality.

What it establishes is not "morality is fake" but "morality has a history and was needed into existence" — and once you see that need, you gain a freedom no one had before: to ask whether these values still serve life. The evidence? Philological etymology, the history of punishment and of religion, and a mass of psychological insight — Nietzsche was trained as a professor of classical philology, so this was home turf. But what finally carries the book is his eye for the human heart: observations that, once they've hit you, you can't unsee.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "Nietzsche glorifies the strong and preaches might makes right." He makes no secret of admiring master morality, but he also says plainly that the priests and ressentiment are what made humans "interesting," deep, spiritual — without the slave revolt, humanity would be a healthy, shallow beast. He wants not a return to the aristocrats but to go beyond both: new values legislated by self-affirmation rather than by negating an enemy.

Misreading 2: the "blond beast" is a racial concept. The blonde Bestie of Essay I §11 refers first to the lion — an image of a predator reveling in its strength with no moral baggage. The "noble races" he lists in the same passage include Romans, Arabs, Japanese nobility, Homeric heroes, and Scandinavian Vikings, across many complexions. It was later his sister Elisabeth — married to the antisemitic agitator Bernhard Förster — who curated his papers in a direction the Nazis could use, saddling the phrase with racist baggage. Nietzsche himself openly despised antisemitism and broke with Wagner and his brother-in-law partly over it.

But honesty is needed here: Essay I casts the Jews as the "priestly people" who launched the slave revolt, and scholars still divide over the tone of that passage — some read it as an analysis of a religious psychological structure (he counts Christianity in too), others think it really does borrow the antisemitic rhetoric of his time. Don't whitewash him, and don't hand the Nazis an endorsement.

Criticism 1: the genetic fallacy. A humble origin doesn't make an idea false — chemistry descends from alchemy, and chemistry is still right. Nietzsche anticipated this (he himself insists origin and value are different things); his defense is that genealogy isn't meant to refute but to strip values of their halo of self-evidence. The philosopher Bernard Williams, in Truth and Truthfulness (2002), sharpened the point: some genealogies debunk, but some vindicate — understanding where a value came from can give you more reason to cherish it.

Criticism 2: the history is speculation and the etymologies are shaky. Nietzsche offers almost no testable historical evidence; the "slave revolt" reads more like a psychological ideal-type narrative than a falsifiable history. Some etymologies are now considered untenable — linking Latin malus ("bad") to Greek melas ("black") to hint at darker-skinned native populations has no linguistic basis.

Criticism 3: Christian love is not all ressentiment. The German philosopher Max Scheler took up the concept in his 1912 essay on ressentiment and reversed the conclusion: genuine Christian love springs from abundance, not lack — a giving downward from strength; it is modern humanitarianism and egalitarianism that are more tinged with ressentiment. The historian Tom Holland's Dominion (2019) confirms Nietzsche's factual claim from the other side — Western values really were rewritten by Christianity — but reverses the verdict: compassion for the weak is that revolution's most precious legacy.

Crossovers

  • Evolutionary biology · exaptation: Gould and Vrba (1982) argued that feathers may have first served for insulation and only later been co-opted for flight — original function and current function are different things, exactly the principle of Essay II §12. The difference: biology has no "stronger will" doing the reinterpreting, only shifting selection pressures.
  • Psychoanalysis · Freud's superego: In Civilization and Its Discontents (1930), Freud argues that civilization suppresses aggression, which the superego takes back and aims at the ego as guilt — nearly a rerun of Essay II §16 with clinical vocabulary. The gap: Freud treats it as civilization's unavoidable cost to be managed; Nietzsche as a historical stage that might be surpassed.
  • Buddhism · no-self, "deeds without a doer": Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga says, roughly, "there is suffering but no sufferer; there are deeds but no doer is found" — the same cut as Nietzsche's lightning with no lightning behind it, both dismantling a subject manufactured by grammar. The difference is direction: Buddhism dissolves the subject to extinguish craving; Nietzsche, to expose how the weak use it to judge the strong.
  • Anthropology · Graeber's Debt: The First 5,000 Years: David Graeber engages directly with the second essay, arguing that Nietzsche projected Adam Smith's market assumption — humans as natural traders — back into prehistory and so arrived at a cruel picture; for Graeber, many early "obligations" were never calculable accounts but open-ended mutual aid. Both agree that the languages of guilt and debt are deeply entangled.
  • Social psychology · intuition first: Jonathan Haidt's social intuitionist model finds that moral intuitions come first and reasoning mostly acts as their after-the-fact lawyer — an empirical cousin of "every philosophy is its author's involuntary memoir." The difference: Haidt sees intuitions as evolved, plural "taste buds," not products of ressentiment.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① We need a critique of moral values: the value of these values must itself first be called into question.

② Why a thing came into being and what it is used for today lie worlds apart; only what has no history can be defined.

③ "Good/bad" comes from the self-affirmation of the strong, "good/evil" from the weak's negation of an enemy — the good man of master morality is the evil man of slave morality.

④ The slave revolt in morality begins when ressentiment itself becomes creative and gives birth to values.

⑤ In ressentiment's workshop, impotence to avenge is called forgiveness, fear is called humility, misery is called being chosen.

⑥ There is no lightning behind the flash, and no doer behind the deed who "could have done otherwise" — a fiction the weak use to put the strong on trial.

⑦ Only what never stops hurting stays in the memory — humanity's first mnemonic was punishment.

⑧ Guilt (Schuld) comes from debt (Schulden); and an infinite God means a debt that can never be repaid.

⑨ Instincts caged inside society's walls turn inward — bad conscience is an illness, but the way pregnancy is an illness.

⑩ What humans can't bear is not suffering but meaningless suffering; so man would rather will nothingness than not will.
