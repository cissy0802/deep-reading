When Things Fall Apart

When Things Fall Apart: Heart Advice for Difficult Times · Pema Chödrön · 1997

中文 →

In One Sentence

When life caves in, our instinct is to grab something solid or find a way out; Chödrön says don't run, stay with the unease, because there was never any solid ground under you, and the collapse has simply let you see it. What she offers is not a way to make things turn out well but a set of practices for not tormenting yourself when they don't: unconditional friendliness toward yourself (maitri), not chasing the "eight worldly dharmas," giving up the hope that it will all work out, and tonglen, the practice of breathing in others' pain and breathing out relief.

Where It Sits

Chödrön is an American-born Tibetan Buddhist nun, a student of Chögyam Trungpa, the Tibetan teacher who founded the Shambhala community, and for many years the resident teacher at Gampo Abbey in Nova Scotia, Canada. The book was edited from talks she gave there, and it is the classic attempt to put Tibetan Buddhist "heart instruction" into words ordinary Westerners can use.

The Core Claims

  • Suffering comes less from things going wrong than from our frantic search for ground that can't go wrong. Impermanence is simply a fact: relationships, health, identities and plans come together and fall apart, then come together and fall apart again. Most of our pain comes from refusing this and trying to nail down what is moving.
  • The way through is not escaping discomfort but learning to stay with it. Collapse, fear, embarrassment and heartbreak are the best teachers precisely because they strip off the shell we normally use to protect ourselves. Each time we bolt, we miss a chance to see clearly, and the same thing comes back next time.
  • What makes staying possible is unconditional friendliness toward yourself, and the compassion for others that grows out of it. This isn't a reward for becoming a better person; it is an attitude you can practice right now. Taken far enough, your pain and other people's pain begin to merge, and practice shifts from "how do I feel better" to "how do I stand with everyone who is hurting."

The Key Concepts, One by One

1. Groundlessness: there was never a floor

What it is. The book's keynote is a single word: groundlessness, the condition of having no footing in life that can be relied on forever. Buddhism teaches impermanence: anything that arises from causes and conditions will change and come apart. Chödrön brings that abstract doctrine down into something you can feel in your body: the sensation of the floor dropping away is not an accident; it is how things actually are, and ordinarily we just cover it over with habit and plans.

Her own case. She describes one collapse in her own life: on an ordinary day her marriage suddenly ended when her husband told her he was seeing someone else (paraphrased). She remembers the sky being huge, the river making its sound, steam rising from her tea; everything carried on as usual except her world. She says it was in that state of having absolutely nothing to hold on to that she really began to practice (paraphrased). The title comes from this kind of experience: things falling apart is a kind of test and also a kind of healing. One often-quoted passage says, in substance, that we think the point is to pass the test or overcome the problem, but things never really get solved; they come together and fall apart, then come together again, and healing comes from letting there be room for all of it.

Why it matters. Most self-help promises that if you do it right, life will steady. Chödrön starts from the opposite premise: stability is an illusion, and the harder you insist on it, the harder each wobble knocks you down. She is not trying to help you find the floor again; she wants you to learn to relax without one. Think of learning to swim: the beginner thrashes about trying to touch bottom and sinks; the swimmer doesn't need a bottom because he has stopped fighting the water.

How it changes your view. The next time a plan collapses or a relationship cracks, you can skip "how do I fix this right now" and first ask, "am I scrambling for a floor?" Admitting the floor isn't there often loosens the anxiety, because you've stopped arguing with the facts.

2. Staying put: don't run, don't fill, don't explain

What it is. If there is no ground, what then? Her answer is plain to the point of seeming dull: stay. When discomfort comes up, don't rush to drive it away, fill it with something else, or spin a story to explain it; just keep it company for a while. She calls this intimacy with fear, which is also the title of the book's first chapter.

The mechanism: how we run. She notices that when people feel bad they reach, almost by reflex, for some comfort: food, the phone, a drink, someone to complain to, an endless argument in their heads. None of these is necessarily bad in itself; the problem is the job they do. They make sure we never actually touch the discomfort, so it is never seen clearly, and next time it pushes us around exactly as before. She writes that only to the extent we expose ourselves over and over to annihilation can what is indestructible in us be found (paraphrased).

How to practice: sitting and the label "thinking." The basic meditation she teaches comes from Trungpa's lineage: sit upright with eyes open, attention resting lightly on the out-breath; whenever you notice you've drifted into thought, say "thinking" to yourself without judging the thought, and come back to the breath. The label pulls you out of the story and back to what the body is feeling now. She insists the touch be light; an image she often uses for this kind of practice is a feather touching a soap bubble (paraphrased). The aim is not to defeat thoughts but to stop wrestling with them. In this small daily drill you learn a real capacity: seeing an urge arise without instantly obeying it.

"Three methods for working with chaos." The book sets out three approaches for chaotic times (paraphrased): first, no more struggle: stop fighting the situation and let the mind settle enough to see it; second, using poison as medicine: treat your own pain as the raw material for compassion, which in practice means tonglen, described below; third, regarding whatever arises as the expression of awakened energy: stop sorting emotions into acceptable and unacceptable, and recognize that each carries a seed of clarity.

How it changes your view. You begin to treat discomfort as information rather than malfunction. When anxiety arrives, your first move can shift from "how do I make it stop" to "all right, it's here; let me stay a moment and see what it actually is." That small gap is where freedom starts.

3. Maitri: making friends with yourself

What it is. Maitri is a Sanskrit word usually rendered as loving-kindness, the wish for others to be happy. Chödrön gives it a crucial turn: maitri begins as unconditional friendliness toward yourself. Not liking yourself once you've fixed your bad habits and improved, but right now, with all the mess and pettiness, holding a gentle, non-judging attitude toward who you are.

Why it has to come first. "Stay with discomfort" can sound like gritting your teeth. She warns that if you rely on grit, practice turns into one more form of self-attack: "I drifted off again, I'm hopeless," "I'm still angry, I'm terrible at this." That harshness only makes you want to run. Only when you are friendly enough toward yourself do you dare to look at yourself honestly, the way a child admits a mistake only when he isn't afraid of being scolded. The way of practicing is itself the goal: approaching your own pain gently is already the practice of compassion.

An example. She describes a common experience among practitioners (paraphrased): people come to Gampo Abbey for retreat expecting to grow calm within a few weeks, and the first long stretch of silence shows them a mind full of resentment, envy and self-pity, which discourages them. Her response is that this is not failure but the practice beginning to work: you are finally seeing what was there all along; the task now is not to hate it but to keep looking with curiosity and kindness.

How it changes your view. You notice how cruel the inner judge sounds. Switch its tone to the one you'd use with a good friend, and the same mistake stops being evidence that you're no good and becomes a wound you can tend.

4. The eight worldly dharmas: four pairs that yank you around

What it is. This is an old Buddhist teaching to which the book gives a chapter. The mind is pulled back and forth by four pairs of opposites: wanting pleasure and fearing pain, wanting praise and fearing blame, wanting fame and fearing disgrace, wanting gain and fearing loss. "Dharma" here simply means a phenomenon or condition; "worldly" marks these as the concerns that keep us caught.

ChaseAvoidWhat it looks like day to day
PleasurePainA flicker of discomfort and you reach for something to feel better
PraiseBlameOne compliment lifts the whole day; one criticism ruins the week
FameDisgraceCaring how you look to others, fearing embarrassment more than being wrong
GainLossClutching what you have and feeling diminished when it goes

The eight worldly dharmas: four pairs of chasing and avoiding (the right-hand column is our summary of how Chödrön describes them in daily life).

The mechanism. Her point is not that you should stop wanting happiness or stop caring what people think, but that you see how automatic the whole machine is: most of our emotional ups and downs are one of these eight winds blowing, and we take it for "me" feeling things. The eight in themselves aren't the problem; the problem is taking them as the truth, believing praise proves our worth and loss proves our failure. Once you see them as weather, you don't have to be knocked over every time.

How it changes your view. The next time a remark knocks you off balance, you can name it: "praise and blame, blowing again." Naming doesn't make the feeling vanish, but it moves you back from "I've been rejected" to "I can see a wind pushing me." That step back is the shared aim of everything she teaches.

5. Hopelessness: the most counterintuitive lesson

What it is. The book's most misunderstood and most powerful chapter is called "Hopelessness and Death." Chödrön argues that hope and fear are two sides of the same coin: as long as you hope things will turn out a certain way, you are also afraid they won't. Hope keeps us living in "it'll be better later," never fully present now.

The Tibetan phrase. She borrows a Tibetan expression Trungpa used, ye tang che (roughly, "totally, completely tired out"), for a thorough giving up after every exit has been tried: not collapsing in despair, but finally being sick of grasping and fleeing over and over, too tired to keep twisting reality into the shape you want (paraphrased). That kind of hopelessness, she says, is where things really begin.

An analogy. Picture waiting for a call that probably isn't coming: you check the phone every few minutes, suspended in midair. Real relief doesn't come when the call finally arrives but when you admit "it may not come" and return to the life in front of you. There is pain in that moment, but you land back in your own body. That is what she means by giving up hope: not giving up on life, but giving up the expectation that keeps you from ever being here.

The link to death. The same chapter pairs giving up hope with facing death: our craving for certainty is, at bottom, a refusal of impermanence and of dying. Buddhist practitioners are taught to keep death in mind; her reading is that this is not about gloom but about no longer wasting life on "someday."

How it changes your view. You begin to notice how many "once this happens, then..." thoughts fill your head: once I change jobs, once he changes, once I'm well. Each one pushes you out of the present. Letting go of them doesn't mean you stop working to change things; it means you stop staking your peace of mind on the outcome.

6. Tonglen: breathe the pain in, breathe relief out

What it is. Tonglen is Tibetan: tong means "sending out," len means "taking in." It comes from the Tibetan mind-training (lojong) tradition: as you breathe in, you take in others' suffering; as you breathe out, you send them relief, warmth and happiness. This runs exactly against instinct, which keeps the good for ourselves and pushes the bad away; tonglen asks you to reverse the direction.

The steps. The version she teaches has roughly four stages (our summary of her instructions):

StageWhat you do
1. A flash of opennessPause for a second or two and let the mind open and go quiet, like the sky
2. Working with textureBreathe in a sense of hot, heavy and dark; breathe out cool, light and bright, syncing them with the breath
3. One specific personDo it for someone who is suffering: breathe in their pain, breathe out the relief you wish for them
4. Widening the circleExtend it to everyone in the same situation, then to all beings, including people you dislike

The four stages of tonglen (a schematic summary of her teaching, not a verbatim quotation).

Starting with your own pain. Her most distinctive move is to hook tonglen to your own suffering. When you are hurting, from jealousy, rejection or heartbreak, you breathe that pain in while recognizing that countless people are feeling exactly the same thing right now, and you breathe out relief for yourself and for them together. The pain that cut you off from the world becomes the thing that connects you to everyone who suffers. This is what "using poison as medicine" means.

Bodhichitta and the soft spot. One more term is needed here: bodhichitta, Sanskrit for "awakened heart" or "mind of awakening," which in Mahayana Buddhism means the aspiration to wake up for the sake of all beings. Following Trungpa, Chödrön makes it concrete: everyone has a "soft spot," a place that is touched by others' pain and aches for them, and that is bodhichitta in seed form. We usually keep it armored; tonglen slowly removes the armor so the soft spot is exposed and can grow.

The usual worry. Many people's first reaction is: won't breathing in other people's pain harm me? Her answer (paraphrased) is that tonglen is not literally inhaling someone's illness but training an attitude, and what it works against is precisely the habit of protecting ourselves; people who practice it often find they feel lighter, because what exhausted them all along was the clench of self-protection. She also says that when you can't do it for others, start with your own pain, and don't force it.

How it changes your view. When you see someone in pain on the street, the usual response is to look away. Tonglen offers another option: in the space of one breath, acknowledge their pain and send a little goodwill. It doesn't replace practical help, but it changes your relationship to suffering from avoidance to approach.

7. The three poisons and "cool loneliness": afflictions as raw material

The three poisons. Buddhism names three root afflictions of the mind: passion (grasping and craving), aggression (rejecting and attacking) and ignorance (not wanting to look, pretending nothing is there). They correspond to three ways of meeting experience: pulling it in, pushing it away, looking past it. Her approach isn't to wipe them out but to recognize them as they arise and use them as material for tonglen: the anger you feel right now is the same anger every angry person in the world shares.

Six kinds of loneliness. A chapter on the six kinds of loneliness distinguishes two kinds. "Hot loneliness" is restless, desperate to fill the gap with someone or something at once; "cool loneliness" is a calm solitude that no longer grabs outward. She lists six kinds of cool loneliness (our summary of her list): less desire, contentment, avoiding unnecessary activity, complete discipline (not looking for an escape from the present), not wandering in the world of desire, and not seeking security from our own chatter of thoughts. Her point is that loneliness need not be eliminated; it can become a form of clarity.

How it changes your view. In a sudden empty moment late at night, you can try not reaching for your phone and simply feel the emptiness. The emptiness itself, she says, isn't what's frightening; our resistance to it is.

The Skeleton of the Argument

Compressed into one line: the world is impermanent and there was never any ground (groundlessness) → our suffering comes from refusing this and grasping and fleeing (the eight worldly dharmas, the three poisons) → the way out is to stay with discomfort without running, which only works if you are friendly enough toward yourself (staying, maitri) → giving up the hope that it will all turn out well lets you be fully present (hopelessness) → once your pain stops being only yours, it becomes a channel to every suffering being (tonglen, bodhichitta).

The book doesn't argue; it rests on two things: more than two thousand years of Buddhist teaching on impermanence, suffering and compassion, and the practice experience of Chödrön and her students. It persuades not with evidence but with an invitation: try it yourself; next time it hurts, don't run, and see what happens. What it establishes is less a proposition than a possibility: that a person can stop fighting themselves even when nothing is going well.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: "staying with pain" means self-punishment or endurance. She means looking with friendliness, not gritting your teeth; if staying turns into self-attack, it violates maitri. Nor is she saying don't change your situation, only don't flee your own feelings before you do.

Misreading two: "giving up hope" means despair. Despair is still tied to hope; it's what happens when the hope fails. The hopelessness she means drops even the demand that things must improve, and is therefore lighter.

Misreading three: tonglen is a mystical energy exchange. In her teaching it is training in a change of attitude, not a claim that you can suck away someone's illness. Read as mind-magic, it leaves people either disappointed or blaming themselves.

Criticism one: experiential, and untestable. Claims such as "groundlessness is how reality is" or "pain transforms when you relax into it" rest on Buddhist tradition and personal experience; the book offers no research and the claims rarely take a testable form. All a reader can do is try, and when it "doesn't work," the tradition tends to blame insufficient practice, which makes it hard to refute.

Criticism two: risk for trauma survivors. Staying with discomfort may help with ordinary anxiety and loss, but for people with serious trauma, sustained attention to painful bodily sensations can trigger flashbacks and a stronger sense of losing control. The American psychotherapist David Treleaven, in Trauma-Sensitive Mindfulness (2018), lays out how mindfulness practice can worsen trauma symptoms and argues that people should be free to pause, open their eyes and shift attention. Chödrön's book is written for general readers; it is not a treatment plan.

Criticism three: spiritual bypassing and silence about injustice. The psychotherapist John Welwood coined "spiritual bypassing" for using spiritual ideas to sidestep psychological problems and real conflicts that need facing. Language like "everything is a teacher" or "accept the present," used one-sidedly, can keep someone enduring a relationship they should leave or doing only inner work in the face of structural injustice. Chödrön herself stresses compassion and not causing harm, but this book does say little about when to say no or resist.

Criticism four: Buddhist premises slip in quietly. Many of the book's claims presuppose a Buddhist worldview: karma, rebirth, sentient beings, awakening. Because she writes so plainly, readers may take away only the psychological techniques without noticing they've accepted a whole metaphysics. That isn't wrong in itself, but readers should know which parts are psychological observation and which are religious belief.

Criticism five: the shadow of the lineage. Her teacher Trungpa was one of the most influential figures in bringing Tibetan Buddhism to the West, and also widely controversial for heavy drinking and sexual relationships with students. His designated successor (the "regent"), Ösel Tendzin, was revealed in the late 1980s to have had unprotected sex with students while knowing he was HIV positive. Beginning in 2018, Trungpa's son Sakyong Mipham, head of Shambhala, faced allegations of sexual misconduct from multiple members and stepped back from leadership. Chödrön herself was not implicated in any of this misconduct; in early 2020 she resigned her role as a senior Shambhala teacher, saying she could not support how the community was handling it (paraphrased). None of this refutes the teachings in the book, but it is a reminder that teachings about compassion and awareness do not guarantee the organizations that carry them, and that absolute trust in a guru deserves particular caution.

Pema Chödrön Beyond This Book

This is her best-known book, but her later work develops a core idea this one doesn't, and without it you might think she teaches only "stay put": shenpa. Shenpa is Tibetan, usually translated "attachment," and in her later books and talks (for example Taking the Leap, 2009) she makes it very concrete: it is the instant you get hooked, a tightening in the body that comes before any thought, as when someone touches a sore spot and your chest contracts before you've worked anything out, followed only afterward by justifying, blaming or escaping. Her analogy is scratching an itch (paraphrased): scratching feels good, but it makes the itch worse and deepens the wound; shenpa is the urge to scratch. Her practice is to recognize the moment of getting hooked, pause, breathe, and not follow the urge. It is really an upgraded version of this book's "staying": moving the practice up to the very second an affliction first stirs.

Another major book, The Places That Scare You (2001), lays out the four "limitless qualities" of loving-kindness, compassion, joy and equanimity, along with their "near enemies": each good quality has a convincing impostor, such as pity as the near enemy of compassion (looking down on someone you feel sorry for) and attachment as the near enemy of loving-kindness (being kind because the other person gratifies you). She also often uses Trungpa's term "idiot compassion" for giving people whatever they want to avoid conflict or to make yourself feel better, such as repeatedly cleaning up after a family member who drinks; it looks kind but enables harm. This answers the silence-about-injustice criticism above: her compassion does not mean having no boundaries.

Crossovers

  • Psychology · Exposure therapy: Edna Foa and Michael Kozak's emotional processing theory (1986) holds that fear fades only when a person meets it without escaping, so the brain can take in the news that it isn't so dangerous. Alike in holding that avoidance keeps fear alive; unlike in that exposure is graded, measured and aimed at a specific fear, while Chödrön's "staying" is a stance toward all discomfort.
  • Neuroscience · Empathic distress vs. compassion: In training studies by Tania Singer and Olga Klimecki, practicing empathy alone (feeling another's pain) increased negative emotion, while adding compassion training raised positive emotion and shifted activity toward brain regions linked to affiliation and reward. That echoes tonglen's answer to "won't taking in pain crush me?": the key is not feeling the pain but feeling it with goodwill. Unlike, in that they trained a traditional loving-kindness style of meditation and did not test tonglen itself.
  • Psychology · Self-compassion: Psychologist Kristin Neff defines self-compassion as three parts, self-kindness, a sense of common humanity and mindful awareness of pain, and built a scale to study it empirically. Alike: it is nearly maitri plus tonglen's "others hurt too"; unlike: it is a measurable trait that assumes no awakening.
  • Western philosophy · Kierkegaard on anxiety: In The Concept of Anxiety, Kierkegaard calls anxiety "the dizziness of freedom" and says that whoever has learned to be anxious in the right way has learned the ultimate (paraphrased). Alike in treating anxiety as a teacher rather than a fault; unlike in that he points toward faith in God, she toward groundlessness with no final support.
  • Western philosophy · Camus on hope: In The Myth of Sisyphus, Camus treats hope in an afterlife or an ultimate meaning as a form of evasion and argues for living lucidly without it. Alike in seeing hope as what pulls us out of the present; unlike in that Camus's keynote is revolt against the absurd, hers is tenderness and compassion.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. There was never solid ground under your feet; the collapse just lets you see it.

2. Things never really get solved; they come together and fall apart, and healing comes from making room for all of it (paraphrased).

3. Most of our suffering comes from trying to nail down what is moving.

4. When discomfort comes, don't run, don't fill it, don't explain it; stay a while and see what it really is.

5. What lets you stay is not willpower but unconditional friendliness toward yourself (maitri).

6. Pleasure and pain, praise and blame, fame and disgrace, gain and loss: these eight winds move you, but they are not you.

7. Hope and fear are two sides of one coin; give up "it has to turn out well" and you can be fully here.

8. Tonglen reverses instinct: breathe in pain, breathe out relief, and your own hurt becomes a channel to everyone who hurts.

9. Everyone has a soft spot that aches for others; that is where compassion starts, and practice means taking off the armor around it.

10. But this is experience, not evidence: be careful with trauma, don't meet injustice with inner work alone, and stay clear-eyed about any lineage.
