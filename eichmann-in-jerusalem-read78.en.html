Eichmann in Jerusalem

A Report on the Banality of Evil · Hannah Arendt · 1963

中文 →

In one sentence

A middle-aged civil servant who kept office hours, deferred to his superiors, was rated a model family man, and tried to recite Kant from the dock, sent millions of people to the gas chambers using timetables, freight cars and memoranda — and half a dozen psychiatrists certified him normal. Having sat through his trial in Jerusalem, Arendt reached a conclusion that is still uncomfortable: the largest organized murder in human history did not require demons. It required people who had stopped using their own heads to ask what it was they were doing. Evil need not have a deep root waiting to be dug up; it can spread like mould across the surface and still cover the world.

Where it sits

Hannah Arendt (1906–1975), German-Jewish political thinker, student of Heidegger and Jaspers, out of Germany in 1933 and into the United States in 1941, had already made herself one of the poles of twentieth-century political thought with The Origins of Totalitarianism and The Human Condition. In 1961 she asked The New Yorker to send her to Jerusalem to cover the trial of Adolf Eichmann; the articles became this book in 1963. The genre matters: this is not a philosophical treatise but a trial report — which, thanks to five words in its subtitle, became one of the most attacked and most misquoted books of the century.

The core claims

  • At this scale, the people who carry out evil are mostly not fanatics but conscientious ordinary men. Calling them beasts feels like a verdict; it is actually an evasion, because it places them outside the human race and so spares us the question of what we would have been in that chair.
  • What turned an ordinary man into an organizer of deportations was not hatred, not perversion, not even mainly fear or obedience, but thoughtlessness — the loss of two very plain capacities: putting yourself in someone else's position, and talking things over with yourself when you get home.
  • Which means the last line of defence is not conscience but judgment. Conscience gets most of its volume as an echo from the surrounding consensus, and flips when society flips. What holds is the ability to say, of one concrete thing, with no rule to appeal to and everyone walking the other way: not this, not by me.

The key ideas, one at a time

The banality of evil: a phrase misquoted for sixty years

Start with a counterintuitive fact: the phrase occurs exactly twice in the book — once in the subtitle, once on the last page. It is not a doctrine; Arendt insisted afterwards that it was simply "something factual" (paraphrased). She had gone to Jerusalem prepared for a Richard III, and what sat in the glass booth was a man of medium height with a nervous tic in his neck and a cold, anxious to correct the court about whether he had been a lieutenant colonel or was being confused with a full colonel. Six psychiatrists pronounced him normal. His attitude to his wife, children and parents was described as exemplary.

So "banal" describes the man, not the crime. What you cannot find in him is depth: no well of hatred, no nihilist creed, no interior of any kind commensurate with what he did. We are used to imagining evil as a poisoned tree with a root, so that dealing with it means digging the root out. Arendt says not this time. In a later letter to the scholar Gershom Scholem she put it harder (paraphrased): evil is never radical, only extreme; it has no depth and no demonic dimension, and precisely because it has no root it can spread like a fungus over the surface and lay the whole world waste. No root also means it can grow anywhere.

Why this matters: it changes what you have to watch. If evil requires demons, prevention means identifying demons. If it can be done by a punctual clerk and model husband, then what needs watching is structures, and the state of mind of people inside them. And it changes how you read atrocity: you can no longer end your thinking about one with "the man was an animal" — the most moral-sounding sentence available, and the laziest.

One boundary, immediately: banal does not mean innocent. Arendt argues throughout for hanging him, and writes her own version of the sentence at the end. Dismantling the demon explanation is not a way of lightening responsibility; it is a way of making responsibility land on one specific man rather than on "the age", "the system" or "human nature" — abstractions that never have to climb the scaffold.

Thoughtlessness (Gedankenlosigkeit): not the same thing as stupidity

Her diagnostic word is thoughtlessness, and her formulation runs roughly: he was not stupid; it was sheer thoughtlessness — something by no means identical with stupidity — that predisposed him to become one of the greatest criminals of that period. This has to be spelled out, or the whole book collapses into "bad people are dim".

Eichmann was shrewd, capable, and an outstanding organizer. In 1944 he ran the Hungarian operation in person, moving some 437,000 people in under two months. That is pure administrative competence. What he lacked was not intelligence but two specific things.

First, thinking from another position. Kant called it the enlarged mentality: before you judge, walk mentally through the standpoint of everyone concerned and see whether your judgment still stands. Eichmann never once did this. The book gives an almost clinical example: he visited a killing installation once and came away physically shaken — but what he retold ever afterwards, what he actually retained, was that he himself had nearly fainted. His attention stayed on his own sensations and never travelled, for one second, to the people. That is not cold-bloodedness; cold-bloodedness at least requires seeing the other person first. This is not seeing at all.

Second, talking with yourself. Arendt later defined thinking as the two-in-one: the soundless dialogue of me with myself, in which a person alone splits into two, one speaking and one answering. She takes Socrates' point that it is better to be at odds with the whole world than with yourself — you can get away from the world, but not from the person you have to share a room with tonight. Someone who never talks with himself has nobody at home to contradict him, and that contradicting voice is exactly where conscience lives.

Thoughtlessness has an observable symptom: cliché. Arendt records again and again that Eichmann could hardly produce a sentence that was not a stock phrase; he admitted that officialese was the only language he had (paraphrased). Her explanation is the sharpest thing in the book: clichés are not poor expression, they are armour. Reality makes a claim on your attention every moment — a person screaming, a packed cattle car — and a ready-made phrase deflects the claim entirely. He did not fail to see; he simply had a board to lay over it each time.

This is the most immediately useful thing in the book: any sentence you can produce whole, without thinking — "it's what's best for the company", "my hands are tied", "that's the process" — is worth stopping at, and lifting up to see what is underneath.

Language rules (Sprachregelung): the anaesthetic that comes with bureaucracy

The Nazi apparatus ran an explicit set of language rules: never "killing" or "extermination", always "final solution" (Endlösung), "special treatment" (Sonderbehandlung), "resettlement" (Umsiedlung). Eichmann had them by heart in court.

Treat these as mere euphemism and you miss Arendt's actual discovery. Their function was not to keep people ignorant, but to make it impossible to connect what one was doing with everything one already knew and felt about murder. Participants knew perfectly well that the people on the trains would die; that was not a secret. But "special treatment" did not, in the mind, complete the old circuit to "murder". What the vocabulary severs is not the information but the wire running from the information to the conscience.

An analogy: it is like relabelling every dial on a machine that is still running. The readings stay accurate, the operation stays correct — and no reading will ever make you reach for the stop button.

Honesty requires saying that this is not a Nazi patent, or the section would be mere history. Today's versions are "collateral damage", "rightsizing", "managing out", "efficiency measures". The test is simple: when a word's main job is to let you say it without discomfort, it is a language rule. Watch, in meetings, for the terms that say nothing and that nobody argues with — they are usually standing directly in front of something no one wants to look at.

The cog theory and the desk murderer: how a division of labour dilutes murder to zero

Eichmann's central defence was that he had been one small cog in a vast machine. He never killed anyone with his hands; he produced timetables, rolling-stock allocations, lists, memoranda and minutes. German has a word for the type: Schreibtischtäter, the perpetrator behind the desk.

Why is the defence strong? Because bureaucratized murder cuts the deed into a hundred operations: the man who drafts does not sign, the man who signs does not board the train, the driver does not shoot, and the shooter has orders. Every man in the chain can say, truthfully, "I didn't do it" — and those "I didn't do it"s add up to six million. The arithmetic is worth naming: responsibility was divided by the number of participants; the deaths were not.

Arendt's answer is the most portable thing in the book: the cog theory is right in sociology and void in a courtroom. When you describe a system, people genuinely can be treated as interchangeable functional parts — that is a legitimate abstraction. But a trial is not asking how the machine ran; it is asking what you, this man in the dock, did. Her formulation runs roughly: the moment they enter a courtroom, all the cogs, however small, are turned back into men. That translation, from the language of systems back into the language of persons, is what a court is for.

A second cut lands on "obedience". Arendt later argued the word out of existence for adults: in politics there is no obedience, only support. A grown man carrying out a policy is voting for that policy with his body. "I was only obeying" is a way of saying: I supported it, but I would rather not be credited with the support.

Conscience inverted: why "I followed my conscience" is not enough

The coldest pages are on the Wannsee Conference of January 1942. Eichmann was there as note-taker, and watched the cultivated senior civil servants of every ministry, most of them with doctorates, not merely fail to object to the extermination programme but compete in eagerness. At that moment, he said, his guilt fell away (paraphrased): he felt something of a Pontius Pilate, washing his hands — who was he to judge? (Pilate is the Roman governor who sentenced Jesus and, in the gospel account, washed his hands before the crowd — the original gesture of "I carried it out, but it isn't mine".)

Name the mechanism or this stays an anecdote. Everyday morality rarely rests on solitary conscience; it rests on a tacit assumption — "if I did that, every decent person around me would condemn me." The strength of the signal is largely an echo returning from the consensus. So when a whole society inverts — when killing becomes law, becomes the Führer's will, becomes a respectable line on a colleague's performance record — the echo inverts with it: conscience stops obstructing and starts urging you to do the job well.

Hence a counterintuitive but crucial judgment: in the Third Reich the direction of temptation was reversed. The usual moral test is not to yield to the temptation to kill. There, the test was not to yield to the temptation not to kill — because not killing was the illegal, negligent, disloyal option that made trouble for your family. Grasp the inversion and you see why "everyone was doing it" is not an excuse but a machine.

One detail cannot be skipped. Eichmann suddenly declared in court that he had lived his life by Kant's categorical imperative, and produced a roughly correct statement of it — act only on a maxim that could at the same time serve as a universal law for everyone — to the astonishment of the judges. Arendt points out what he had done: a household version of Kant for the little man. Kant's whole point is autonomy, that the universal law must be legislated by your own reason; Eichmann had swapped "universal law" for "the will of the Führer". The Nazi jurist Hans Frank put the substitution baldly (paraphrased): act in such a way that the Führer, if he knew of your action, would approve of it. See how frightening the swap is: the form is identical, the sense of duty and discipline and self-demand all remain — only "judge for yourself" has been replaced by "will they be pleased upstairs". The moral shell keeps running, and runs harder.

Judgment: what the ones who refused were going on

If conscience inverts with society, what did the refusers have? Arendt's answer is judgment — the capacity to say of one concrete thing "not this, not by me", with no ready-made rule to apply. Its criterion is usually not a lofty principle but something very plain: if I did that, I could not go on living with myself.

Denmark is the one chapter where you can breathe. Danish officials told the Germans openly that there was no Jewish question in Denmark and that any measure against Danish citizens would bring the government's resignation; they refused to distinguish Danish Jews from refugees, and refused to introduce the yellow star. (A correction while we are here: the story that the king wore one himself has no historical basis; what actually happened is that the authorities refused to impose it.) The astonishing part is not the Danes but that the German officials stationed in Denmark changed: they stalled, leaked, and enforced as little as possible, until some seven thousand Jews were carried to neutral Sweden by fishing boat in a matter of days. Arendt says the episode should be required reading in political science, because it shows where the force of non-violent resistance actually lies: occupiers are people too, and a man marinated long enough in a society that says no is eroded by the no.

The other case is Sergeant Anton Schmidt, a Wehrmacht NCO who helped Jewish partisans with trucks and papers and was executed for it in 1942. When a witness told the story, the courtroom fell silent for two minutes. Arendt wrote, in substance: how utterly different everything would be today if only more such stories could be told. But her point is not sentiment; it is a hard political finding. Stories like this prove that "there was no choice" is a lie. Someone chose, and it cost him his life — but it existed as an option. And the record shows that Germans who refused to carry out killing orders were, in most cases, transferred or passed over for promotion rather than shot.

Alongside it is one of her most quoted lines (paraphrased): the holes of oblivion do not exist. Nothing human is that perfect; one man always survives to tell the story. The idea that a clean job is the same as no job is itself a mistake.

The darkest chapter: the Jewish Councils and the grey zone

This is where the book detonated. Arendt called the role played by the Jewish leadership in the destruction of its own people the darkest chapter of the whole dark story (paraphrased). Under German instructions, the Jewish Councils drew up lists, allotted the handful of exemptions, kept order, and had a Jewish police force carry out the round-ups. She went further: had the Jews been wholly unorganized and leaderless, there would have been chaos and misery but the final death toll would have been far lower.

Why put this among the concepts rather than in the controversy pile? Because it is the mirror image of the banality of evil: the same administrative rationality — save whoever can be saved, concede the smallest demand first, keep order — can walk people on the victims' side, step by step, into cooperation. That insight far outlasts the shouting it caused.

Two things must be marked just as honestly. First, this is the part of the book most attacked and most likely overwritten: her tone is widely felt to be lofty about people in an impossible position, and the quantitative claim about the death toll has never been established historically. Second, the more careful successor concept is Primo Levi's — the Auschwitz survivor's grey zone, in The Drowned and the Saved (1986): the camps opened a blurred continuum between victim and perpetrator, its extreme case being the Sonderkommando, prisoners forced to handle the corpses of their own people in exchange for a few more months. Levi's position is that the zone must be understood, and that nobody sitting in a warm room is qualified to judge it. He said explicitly that Arendt had judged the councils too harshly.

The argument in outline

On the surface this is a trial narrative — the man and his career, the "geography" of the deportations country by country, the prosecution, the judgment, the hanging, a postscript. Underneath it is one chain of reasoning:

  • A new crime: not a crime of passion, not a tyrant's slaughter, but administrative massacre — a state using lawful procedure, professional specialization and paperwork to turn the extermination of a people into ordinary work.
  • A new criminal: hence a man who commits crimes "under circumstances that make it well-nigh impossible for him to know or to feel that he is doing wrong" (paraphrased). The old moral vocabulary — motive, malice, cruelty, greed — comes up empty against him.
  • A philosophical finding: evil need not have depth. It can proceed with no motive, no conviction, nothing demonic, only thoughtlessness. She publicly revised her own earlier term, the "radical evil" of The Origins of Totalitarianism: evil is never radical, only extreme.
  • A political conclusion: the defence is not conscience (it inverts), not education (Wannsee was a table of doctorates), and not law (the law had been made an accomplice). What is left is each person's capacity to judge a particular case.
  • A legal answer: responsibility still cannot be absorbed by "the system". She writes the sentence she thought the court should have pronounced, in substance: because you carried out a policy of not wanting to share the earth with the Jewish people, no member of the human race can be expected to want to share the earth with you — that is the reason, and the only reason, you must hang. Note that the reasoning bypasses both "you are a monster" and deterrence.

Misreadings & serious objections

  • Misreading 1: "banality of evil" means evil is ordinary and therefore forgivable. The book argues from beginning to end for the death penalty. "Banal" describes the man, not the crime.
  • Misreading 2: "anyone could have been Eichmann", i.e. collective guilt. Arendt rejected collective guilt vehemently; her line runs, in substance, where all are guilty, no one is — spreading guilt over everybody is the most efficient exculpation there is. She wanted the opposite: each person nailed back to the particular thing he did.
  • Misreading 3: it is Milgram's obedience study in book form. Different claim entirely, and Arendt specifically rejected the obedience frame. Since those experiments are constantly cited as backup, state their current standing: in Milgram (1961–63) the famous "about 65% went to the maximum shock" is the result of one condition, and the variants differ enormously; later archival work (Gina Perry) showed the experimenter prodding well beyond the script and a substantial share of subjects doubting the shocks were real. The Stanford Prison Experiment (1971) has been largely discredited — the archives show the guards were coached. The better-supported modern account is engaged followership: people are typically not blindly obedient but identify with a cause and then work creatively to advance it — which, awkwardly, fits the Eichmann of the next objection better than Arendt's.
  • Objection 1, the strongest, and you should know it: Bettina Stangneth's Eichmann Before Jerusalem (2011). Working through the mass of tapes and notes from his years in Argentina — the Sassen interviews, conducted by a former Nazi journalist — she found an Eichmann who is neither humble nor thoughtless: a committed ideologue who boasted about his part in the extermination and regretted not finishing the job. The conclusion is unsparing: the mediocre little functionary in the Jerusalem dock was very likely a performance, and Arendt — who attended only part of the trial and worked from transcripts for the rest — was taken in by it. This is the gravest blow to the book's factual basis.
  • Objection 2: the Jewish Councils chapter, on both facts and tone. Jacob Robinson and others went through her errors line by line; Gershom Scholem, the great scholar of Jewish mysticism and her friend of many years, accused her in a famous open letter of lacking love for the Jewish people and of a heartless, almost sneering tone. Her reply is equally famous (paraphrased): I have never in my life loved any people or collective… I love only my friends. It is a classic head-on collision between loyalty to thought and loyalty to a community, and neither side is being frivolous.
  • Objection 3: two opposed historical lines. Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996) argues that ordinary Germans held an eliminationist antisemitism and were willing executioners — the direct contrary of Arendt. Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men (1992) studied Reserve Police Battalion 101, middle-aged Hamburg policemen, mostly not party members, who shot tens of thousands of people in Poland: the commander explicitly offered to excuse anyone who did not want to take part, those who stepped out were in fact not seriously punished, and only a minority stepped out; the main reason most men fired was neither fanaticism nor fear but not wanting to look weak in front of comrades, or to push the dirty work onto them. Read together, the honest conclusion is that the concept may outlive the specimen it was drawn from: Eichmann may not be a good instance of the banality of evil, but Browning's policemen are.
  • An objection about form. She wrote a trial report and threaded irony through it — she admitted the irony herself. Treating extermination in a satirical register was unbearable to many survivors. That is not their failure to understand; it is the price the register carries.

Arendt beyond this book

Read only this one and you get a lopsided Arendt: as if her whole claim were that terrible things are done by ordinary people.

The Origins of Totalitarianism (1951): totalitarianism is not tyranny intensified. Tyranny wants you silent; totalitarianism wants you filled. Two pillars. Ideology: a single law of history or nature that explains everything, so that once you accept the premise, everything else is deduced and reality becomes superfluous. Terror: whose point is not that it targets opponents but that it is arbitrary — precisely because anything may get you arrested, there is nowhere to stand. Its soil is loneliness and an atomized mass, and she separates the words with care: solitude is being with yourself; loneliness is having lost even that company — which dovetails exactly with Eichmann's thoughtlessness. Her most-quoted verdict there runs, in substance: the ideal subject of totalitarian rule is not the convinced fanatic but the person for whom the distinction between true and false no longer exists.

The Human Condition (1958): human activity in three layers. Labour sustains the biological cycle; its product is consumed and leaves no trace. Work fabricates durable things and builds a "world" a person can inhabit. Action is speaking and acting among others, and it discloses who you are. Action presupposes plurality — we live in the plural and each of us is different — which is why its consequences are unpredictable and irreversible. Her remedies for those two defects are unusual: forgiving, which undoes what has been done, and promising, which drives a stake into an unpredictable future. This positive doctrine is what shows up Eichmann's emptiness: he never acted; he completed tasks.

The Life of the Mind (1978, unfinished): produced directly by the trial. Jerusalem, she said, forced a question on her — what is the relation between thoughtlessness and evil-doing? — and she set out to redo the ground in three volumes: Thinking, Willing, Judging. She died having written only the title of the third; the surviving lectures show she meant to borrow from Kant's aesthetic judgment a capacity for judging a particular case when no universal rule is available to cover it. The last work of her life was the theory this report needed.

Ten sentences

1. She went expecting a monster and found a man with a cold and a facial tic who mainly wanted his rank corrected — six psychiatrists called him normal. That is all "the banality of evil" means: banal describes the man, not the crime.

2. "He was not stupid. It was sheer thoughtlessness — something by no means identical with stupidity — that predisposed him to become one of the greatest criminals of that period." (paraphrased)

3. Thoughtlessness is the absence of two capacities: standing in another person's position, and talking things over with yourself. A man who never talks with himself has nobody at home to contradict him — and that contradicting voice is conscience.

4. He could barely utter a sentence that was not a cliché. Clichés are not poor expression; they are armour — reality claims your attention every moment, and a ready-made phrase deflects the claim whole.

5. "Final solution", "special treatment": language rules do not cut the information, they cut the wire from the information to the conscience — like relabelling every dial on a running machine, so the readings stay accurate and none of them ever makes you reach for the stop button.

6. The cog theory holds in sociology and is void in court: the moment they enter a courtroom, all the cogs, however small, are turned back into men. And among adults there is no obedience in politics, only support — to carry out a policy is to vote for it with your body.

7. At Wannsee he watched a table of doctorates compete in eagerness and felt he had washed his hands like Pilate. Conscience is largely an echo of the surrounding consensus, and when the society inverts, the echo inverts with it.

8. So the moral test there ran backwards: what had to be resisted was not the temptation to kill but the temptation not to. He had rewritten Kant's imperative as "act so the Führer would approve" — form intact, core swapped, the sense of duty running harder than ever.

9. The Danes refused the yellow star until even the German officials posted there were eroded into foot-dragging. The point of such stories is not to move you but to prove that "there was no choice" is a lie. And the holes of oblivion do not exist — someone always survives to tell it.

10. Her sentence, in substance: because you carried out a policy of not wanting to share the earth with the Jewish people, no one can be expected to want to share the earth with you — that is the only reason you must hang. And in 2011 Stangneth showed, from the Argentine tapes, that the mediocre functionary was probably a performance. The honest reading: the concept outlives the specimen — Eichmann may be a poor instance, but Browning's policemen, who did not want to look weak in front of their comrades, are a perfect one.
