Beyond Good and Evil

Jenseits von Gut und Böse · Friedrich Nietzsche · 1886

中文 →

In One Sentence

You take morality to be a set of rules that applies to everyone, and truth to be a thing worth wanting unconditionally. This book turns both of them over to look at the underside: every morality is one type of person's survival conditions rewritten as universal law, and every philosophy is its author's constitution and appetites doing his arguing for him. What it asks of you is not that you stop evaluating, but that you learn a question nobody asks: who needed these values, and under what conditions?

Coordinates

Having finished Thus Spoke Zarathustra and discovered that almost nobody could read a book written in aphoristic verse, Nietzsche restated the same thoughts in 1886 in the plainest and sharpest polemical prose he ever wrote, and subtitled it "Prelude to a Philosophy of the Future." If Zarathustra was the sermon, this is the teardown: everything the earlier book wrapped in metaphor is here disassembled into claims, evidence, and attacks. Nine chapters, 296 numbered sections and a closing poem, running from epistemology through morality, religion, scholars, nations, and finally "what is noble." It is the best door into Nietzsche — and one of the books he published at his own expense, to near-total silence.

The Central Claims

  • Philosophy is not disinterested inquiry; it is unconscious autobiography. The first question to put to a doctrine is not "is it true" but "out of what condition of life did it grow, and what is it defending?"
  • There is not one morality but at least two, and they are enemies. Master morality, generated by the strong out of themselves, works in the coordinates good / bad. Slave morality, generated by the weak out of a negation of the enemy, works in the coordinates good / evil. What passes for morality as such in modern Europe is the victory of the second.
  • What runs underneath all life is will to power — not survival, not pleasure, but growth, the overcoming of resistance, the imposition of one's own form on chaos. Even the will to truth is one of its shapes.

The Core Concepts, One by One

"Supposing truth is a woman": the opening slap

The first line of the preface is one of the most provocative openings in philosophy: "Supposing truth is a woman — what then?" All dogmatic philosophers, he goes on — dogmatists being those who believe they can prove the final structure of the world once and for all, from Plato to Hegel — have been comically clumsy in their courtship of truth, because they "understood nothing of women": solemn, armored in a system, convinced that seriousness and force would do it.

The image is not flirtation; it makes a precise point. Truth is not handed over because you are upright, earnest and logically airtight. It asks for tact, patience, indirection — and for the admission that you have preferences, that you are somewhere, that you are not standing outside the world. The line that follows is the harder one, and the one later philosophy never stopped quoting: "Christianity is Platonism for the people." Plato puts the real world in the realm of Forms and makes the senses a shadow; Christianity puts it in heaven and makes this life an examination. Elite version and popular version of a single structure. Two thousand years of European thought, Nietzsche says, are variations on one gesture: shifting the center of gravity out of here and into somewhere else.

How it changes your seeing: from now on you will read any grand system with one extra question — where does it store "what really matters"? If the answer is always "not in the place you are standing," be alert: that may not be a discovery but a technique for handling discomfort.

Every great philosophy is its author's involuntary memoir

Section 6 of the first chapter is the book's methodological manifesto (paraphrased): "It has become clear to me what every great philosophy so far has been: namely, the personal confession of its author, and a kind of involuntary and unconscious memoir." The philosopher's moral intentions, he adds, are the root the plant actually grew from; the cold arguments are what got fetched afterwards.

The mechanism matters, or this collapses into "attack the man, not the argument." Nietzsche's model: a person is a set of competing drives (Triebe) — combative, security-seeking, order-loving, domineering — and every one of them "would like to philosophize," to have the whole world interpreted in its own image. Whichever drive is on top conscripts the intellect to build it a cosmology. Spinoza's system, pressing everything into geometrical proof, is the fortress a fragile hermit builds for himself; Kant's critique, which leaves a back door open for morality, is a pious Königsberg man declining to give up God.

Crucially, this is not "therefore they are wrong." It is "therefore the wrong question is being asked." Asking whether a philosophy is true is like asking whether a man's body temperature is true; the question is what constitution it belongs to, and whether it makes that life stronger or weaker. This is the seed of what Nietzsche would soon call genealogy: not the justification of values but their ancestry.

How it changes your seeing: the next time you hear someone insist hotly that "people are basically selfish, so they need firm control," or that "systems don't matter, only self-discipline does," you will add a question automatically: where is the person saying this standing? If this claim holds, who benefits, and who gets put in their place? That is not an ad hominem dodge; it is reducing a position to the survival strategy it serves. Ideology critique, the sociology of knowledge, and Foucault's power/knowledge all begin here.

Interrogating the will to truth: why truth rather than untruth?

Section 1 goes straight for philosophy's heart (paraphrased): "What in us really wants truth? … We asked after the value of this will. Granted we want truth: why not rather untruth?" For two millennia the pursuit of truth needed no defense. Nietzsche is the first to put the premise itself in the dock.

Section 4 then states the most misread and most important claim in the book (paraphrased): "The falseness of a judgment is to us not necessarily an objection to it … the question is how far it is life-advancing, life-preserving, species-preserving, perhaps even species-breeding." His examples are the founding fictions of logic and mathematics — "identical things," substance, causality, number. There are no two identical leaves; equality is a simplification we impose. And it is exactly that simplification that lets a creature calculate, predict, and survive. The most useful beliefs may well be the least accurate ones.

Hence perspectivism: there is no eye that sees the world from nowhere; all seeing is from a position, with an interest. Do not read this as "anything goes" — Nietzsche means the opposite. Precisely because there is no view from above, the number and quality of perspectives becomes the only available rigor. As he puts it in the Genealogy (paraphrased): the more eyes, the more different eyes we can use on the same thing, the more complete our "objectivity" about it will be.

How it changes your seeing: it converts the wish to "be objective" into an executable move — not emptying yourself of a standpoint (impossible), but deliberately collecting the standpoints whose interests run against your own.

"A thought comes": the subject is a superstition left behind by grammar

Section 17 is the tightest demolition Nietzsche ever wrote (paraphrased): "A thought comes when 'it' wishes, and not when 'I' wish; so that it is a falsification of the facts to say that the subject 'I' is the condition of the predicate 'think.'" Try to order yourself to produce a new idea in the next three seconds. You cannot. Thoughts arrive on their own; you sign for them afterwards and call it "I thought of it."

He charges Descartes's cogito with smuggling in a whole cargo: that there is an "I," that this I causes the thinking, that thinking is an activity, that activities require an agent. None of that is observed. It is inferred from the subject-predicate structure of a sentence. Hence the heaviest line in the chapter (paraphrased): "I am afraid we are not rid of God because we still believe in grammar." Grammar obliges you to attach a subject to every event, so you come to believe there is a "lightning-doer" behind the lightning, a doer behind the deed — which is where moral responsibility gets stored.

The same knife goes into causa sui, the notion of being one's own cause. Section 21 calls it the best self-contradiction yet conceived (paraphrased), invented for one purpose: to make a person fully answerable for his acts so that punishment can look justified. He proposes replacing the pseudo-problem of free versus unfree will with a real one: strong will and weak will. Not whether you had a choice, but how far your will can carry itself through resistance.

How it changes your seeing: once you notice that the "subject" was installed by grammar, familiar arguments change shape. "I just wasn't disciplined enough" assumes one unified I who ought to have been giving orders. The likelier picture: a crowd of drives fighting over the steering wheel, and what you call a decision is the dispatch written by whichever one won.

Will to power: from psychological hypothesis to the world's inner face

Zarathustra sings about will to power; this book gives it its one near-complete argument, in section 36 (paraphrased): suppose nothing is "given" as real to us except our world of desires and passions; suppose thinking is only a relation among these drives; then, on grounds of methodological parsimony (do not multiply kinds of principle needlessly — Ockham's razor), we are obliged to try explaining the so-called material world with the one kind of reality we actually have. The conclusion is the sentence everyone quotes on its own: "This world viewed from inside … would be 'will to power' and nothing else."

First, what it is not: will to power is not "wanting to boss people around." It is what any living thing does against resistance — the vine cracking the pavement, the infant learning to walk, the scholar breaking a hard problem. Domination of others is its poorest form; its strongest form is command over oneself. Section 51 supplies the elegant case: why do the powerful kneel before the saint? Because in a man ascetic to that degree they smell a power they lack — anyone who can be that hard on himself must be guarding something formidable.

And now the honest part. Section 259 pushes the logic to its least comfortable conclusion (paraphrased): "Life is essentially appropriation, injury, overpowering of the strange and weaker, suppression, hardness … exploitation." Not, he insists, because society is corrupt or unfinished, but because life simply is will to power. This is the hardest passage in the book and the last one that should be skipped: what is frightening in Nietzsche is not that he celebrates cruelty, but that he refuses to file it under exceptions.

How it changes your seeing: it gives you a different ruler for motives. Someone who keeps demanding fairness may be seeking growth; so may someone clinging to safety (his strength only reaches far enough to hold what he has). The question shifts from "is he good or bad" to "how much force does he have, and in which direction is he spending it."

Master and slave morality: two coordinate systems, not two kinds of people

Section 260 is where the book detonates. In every higher civilization he has examined, Nietzsche says, morality falls into two basic types — and they are usually mixed inside one society, often inside one person.

Master morality is generated outward from the self: the healthy, self-possessed person feels his own state to be good and names what resembles him good (gut) — noble, brave, generous, truthful, able to keep his word — and then, as an afterthought, names the opposite bad (schlecht): timid, petty, calculating, unable to meet your eye. Note that "bad" here carries no hatred. It is closer to "second-rate." It looks down; it does not fear.

Slave morality runs the other way, outward from a negation: the suffering, unfree person's first move is to label his oppressor evil (böse), and only then to derive, by reversal, that whatever is unlike him is good. The qualities promoted to virtues are the ones the weak live by: pity, patience, humility, harmlessness. The creative act of this morality is the No. Nietzsche later gave the state it grows from a French name: ressentiment — not a flash of anger but a chronic toxin, resentment so blocked from revenge that it takes its revenge in value judgments instead.

The easiest way to misread this is as "so go be a master and trample people." Nietzsche himself was sickly, solitary, and living on a pension; by his own taxonomy he was nowhere near the master type. What he wants is for you to see that your moral vocabulary has an ancestry: when you call something evil, you are reaching for coordinates whose origin is the negation of an enemy; when you call something second-rate, you are reaching for the other set. Two different sentences, two entirely different states of the person saying them.

How it changes your seeing: you start noticing the grammar of your own anger. "This is unfair" and "this is beneath contempt" are not the same utterance — the first requires a judge to compensate you, the second requires nobody's agreement at all. Nietzsche's question: how many of your value judgments need somebody else to be in the wrong before they can stand up?

Herd morality: how "being good" becomes a danger

Chapter Five, "On the Natural History of Morals," argues that what circulates today as morality is herd-animal morality — morality whose entire content is "harmless to the group." The mechanism is blunt: a group survives only by suppressing whatever threatens its order — appetites that run too hot, self-regard that stands too tall, solitude, dangerous curiosity. Those traits get renamed evil; their opposites are canonized. Nietzsche's summary is exact (paraphrased): "the timidity of the herd is now called morality."

He targets two things in particular. First pity: he does not deny it reduces suffering; he questions what follows from enthroning it as the highest virtue. If the elimination of pain becomes the supreme goal, everything that only grows out of pain — craft, depth, the capacity to carry weight — is eliminated with it. Section 225 (paraphrased): "The discipline of suffering, of great suffering — do you not know that only this discipline has created every enhancement of man so far?" Second, equality: his target is not equality before the law but the promotion of equality from an institutional rule into a metaphysical truth — that all human beings are fundamentally the same. Once that holds, anyone unlike the rest becomes a problem to be leveled.

This is the chapter most easily conscripted for harm. Its insight is real: a society that reduces morality to "do no damage" does lose the capacity to grow exceptions. Its blind spot is equally real: he says almost nothing about whose drudgery paid for the nobility he admires.

Where "beyond" actually is: not no standard, but a higher one

The title is routinely misread as "anything goes." Nietzsche wrote a note in the Genealogy specifically to block this (paraphrased): "Beyond good and evil — at least this does not mean beyond good and bad." What he wants dismantled is the good/evil coordinate system, whose defining move is to cast the different as a moral enemy. The good/bad evaluation stays — and gets stricter.

Where does the new standard come from? Section 211 (paraphrased): "Genuine philosophers are commanders and legislators: they say 'thus it shall be!' … Their knowing is creating, their creating is a legislating, their will to truth is — will to power." The most ambitious and most contested sentence in the book: values are not discovered, they are enacted.

But legislating is not licence, and this is the layer least often quoted. Section 188 (paraphrased): everything free, subtle, bold and masterfully certain — in thought, in rule, in art — developed only under the long compulsion of some "arbitrary law"; the condition of everything valuable on earth is "long obedience in a single direction." His example is metrical verse: it is the shackles of rhyme and meter that force out the freest thing in the language. "Beyond" is not escape from all constraint; it is trading the constraint imposed from outside for a stricter one you impose on yourself.

How it changes your seeing: it swaps the engine inside the word "discipline." Most people mean restraining appetite; Nietzsche means organizing scattered appetites into something that can point one way for a long time — what he elsewhere calls "giving style to one's character." Subtraction and endurance versus composition and shaping.

Masks, the abyss, and what is noble

The line from Chapter Four that has been quoted to death has a hard context (section 146): "He who fights with monsters should see to it that he does not become a monster. And when you gaze long into an abyss, the abyss also gazes into you." The mechanism is specific: spend all your force opposing something and you are forced to organize yourself in its shape — your attention, your vocabulary, your map of friend and enemy are all set by your opponent. This is the psychological aftermath of slave morality's No.

Its companion is section 40: "Every profound spirit needs a mask." Not a lesson in hypocrisy — his reason is that anything deep is deformed in shallow retelling. And he adds a colder observation: the mask grows by itself, because the people around you keep misunderstanding you, and those misunderstandings are the mask.

The final chapter, "What Is Noble," lands the book on its most counterintuitive claim: nobility is not defined by conduct. Section 287 (paraphrased): what makes a person noble is not his actions — actions can be imitated — but his relation to himself: the noble soul has reverence for itself. Paired with it is the pathos of distance, which is not the arrogance of standing apart from others but the height differential a person maintains inside himself — the gap between the higher you and the current you, which is the power source of every ascent.

The Argument in Outline

Compressed into a single line, the 296 sections move like this:

  • Step one (dismantle epistemology): philosophy is not disinterested; behind every system a drive is defending itself. The will to truth has never had its own value questioned. A judgment's falsity is no objection to it. There is no view from above, only views.
  • Step two (dismantle the subject): the cogito is grammar, not observation. Thoughts arrive by themselves; causa sui is a self-contradiction invented to justify punishment. The real question is not whether the will is free but whether it is strong.
  • Step three (supply a replacement principle): if desires and passions are the only reality actually given, parsimony obliges us to try explaining everything with them — the world seen from inside is will to power: not survival but growth and overcoming.
  • Step four (re-read morality with it): morality is not a body of truth but two coordinate systems generated by two conditions of life — good/bad out of self-affirmation, good/evil out of ressentiment. What now passes for morality is the victory of the second: the herd's self-preservation restated as the universal good.
  • Step five (set the task): hence the need for "philosophers of the future" — not scholars who interpret the world but creators willing to legislate; and the conditions of legislating are not licence but long obedience in one direction and reverence for oneself.

In a sentence: if no objective moral fact is waiting to be discovered, then the only serious question left is who has the strength to legislate for himself — and what he legislates.

Common Misreadings & Real Criticism

Misreading one: "beyond good and evil" means there is no right and wrong, so do as you like. The opposite. Nietzsche said explicitly that this is not "beyond good and bad." He abolishes the coordinate system that moralizes the enemy into evil; evaluation itself survives and tightens. And the conditions he sets for creators — long obedience, self-reverence, being harder on yourself than on anyone else — are far more demanding than conventional morality. The book is not a permit. It is a higher bar.

Misreading two: master morality is the good one, so let's go back to being masters. Nietzsche is doing genealogical description, not writing a prescription; he never proposes restoring an ancient aristocracy, and he insists the two moralities are tangled together in modern people. Reading it as a call to trample the weak is exactly what the twentieth century's most notorious appropriation did.

On the Nazi appropriation, two things have to be separated. The falsification is real: after Nietzsche's collapse in 1889, his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche — a committed antisemite who with her husband had tried to found a "pure German" colony in Paraguay — took control of the papers, assembled scraps of notebook into the book published as The Will to Power, and marketed her brother as a prophet of the German nation. Nietzsche himself, in Chapter Eight of this very book, mocks German nationalism, calls himself a "good European," and is savage about antisemites. But it cannot all be blamed on the forgery: passages like section 259, which describes exploitation as the essence of life and speaks of breeding a higher type, are in his own hand and did supply usable material. The honest formulation: the Nazi reading was a distortion, but not a distortion out of nothing.

On the passages about women, there is no defense, and none should be attempted. Chapter Seven contains a stretch of writing that is indefensible by any current standard. One widely circulated misquotation is worth correcting, though: "You are going to women? Do not forget the whip" comes from Zarathustra, spoken there by an old woman, not asserted by Nietzsche in his own voice. That changes nothing about Chapter Seven; it only keeps the books straight.

The hardest philosophical objection: self-reference. If all knowing is perspectival and every claim an expression of will to power, what about that claim? Either it exempts itself (contradiction) or it concedes it is also just an interpretation (in which case why should it beat its rivals?). Nietzsche saw this coming and answered almost with a shrug at the end of section 22 (paraphrased): "Supposing that this too is only interpretation — and you will be eager enough to raise that objection? — well, so much the better." Critics from Habermas onward reply that this costs him any normative ground to stand on: his critique of Christian morality then reduces to an aesthetic preference with no argumentative force.

The historical objection. The master/slave narrative — especially the etymological argument in the Genealogy, that words for good and bad originally denoted social rank — has struck many classicists as loose and selective in its evidence. Taken as rigorous moral history it does not hold. Taken as a hypothesis that illuminates a phenomenon, it is worth everything.

Is will to power a metaphysics? The scholarship remains split. Heidegger read it as the last step of Western metaphysics, with will to power installed where Plato had put Being. Walter Kaufmann, the translator who rebuilt Nietzsche's reputation after the war, pointed out that section 36 is written entirely in questions and conditionals — a methodological hypothesis, not an ontology. The text supports both, which suggests either that Nietzsche deliberately left the door ajar, or that he had not settled it himself.

Ten Sentences

1. "Supposing truth is a woman — what then?" The dogmatists failed not from insufficient seriousness but from too much of it: they thought a system and some force would take truth, and never admitted they had preferences, a position, a stake.

2. "Every great philosophy so far has been the personal confession of its author, a kind of involuntary and unconscious memoir." So do not only ask whether a doctrine is true; ask what condition of life it grew from and what it is defending.

3. "The falseness of a judgment is not necessarily an objection to it." The question is how far it advances life. Identity, substance, causality are fictions — and those fictions are what let a creature calculate and survive. The most useful beliefs are often the least accurate.

4. There is no eye that sees from outside the world; there are only perspectives. This does not license "anything goes" — it implies the reverse: with no view from above, the only rigor left is deliberately collecting the perspectives whose interests oppose yours.

5. "A thought comes when 'it' wishes, and not when 'I' wish." The cogito is not an observation but a sentence structure — which is why he adds: I fear we are not rid of God, because we still believe in grammar.

6. "This world viewed from inside would be will to power and nothing else." It does not mean wanting to rule others — domination is its poorest form; its strongest is command over oneself. Which is why the powerful kneel before the saint.

7. Morality runs on two coordinate systems: good/bad begins in self-affirmation, good/evil begins in the negation of an enemy. The first looks down without hatred; the second's creative act is the No — and whoever says that No is thereafter defined by what he opposes.

8. "He who fights with monsters should see to it that he does not become a monster; and when you gaze long into an abyss, the abyss gazes into you." Not decorative: it is the psychological consequence of the previous line. Spend all your force against something and you must take its shape to fight it.

9. "Beyond" is not escape from constraint. "The condition of everything valuable on earth is long obedience in a single direction." Meter shackles the poet's hands, and that is what forces out the freest thing in the language. Freedom is not lawlessness; it is a stricter law you write yourself.

10. Nobility is not defined by conduct — conduct can be imitated. What makes a person noble is reverence for himself, and the height differential he keeps inside: the gap between the higher self and the current one. Without that gap there is no direction called up.
