The Theory of Moral Sentiments

The Theory of Moral Sentiments · Adam Smith (1723–1790) · first edition 1759, heavily revised sixth edition 1790

中文 →

In One Sentence

Morality isn't a rulebook handed down from heaven, nor a ledger of self-interest; it grows out of a mirror. We use imagination to stand in someone else's shoes and feel what they feel (sympathy), then gradually internalize "how would others see me?" into an unbiased judge inside our own heads (the impartial spectator). Conscience, justice, self-command, even the "invisible hand" are all products of that mirror.

Where It Sits

This was the first book by Smith, then professor of moral philosophy at Glasgow, seventeen years before The Wealth of Nations (see READ 5a). He revised it six times and was still overhauling it in the year he died. It's the high point of Scottish Enlightenment sentimentalism (the view that moral judgment is rooted in feeling rather than pure reason), carrying forward Hume and Francis Hutcheson, and its chief opponent is Bernard Mandeville's cynical slogan "private vices, public benefits."

Core Arguments

One: we are built to care about others, and that care doesn't reduce to self-interest. The very first sentence lays it out: "How selfish soever man may be supposed, there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others, and render their happiness necessary to him, though he derives nothing from it except the pleasure of seeing it." That line is aimed squarely at Hobbes and Mandeville, who said every kindness is self-love in disguise. Smith says no: concern for others is part of our original equipment.

Two: moral judgment works by spectating. We don't learn rules first and apply them afterward. In a concrete situation we imagine ourselves as an onlooker and ask whether we can "go along with" a feeling or an action, whether it is fitting. Rules come later, distilled from thousands of such judgments. Morality is a kind of perception first and a code second.

Three: conscience is a social product, yet it can rise above society. We first learn to judge ourselves through other people's eyes. But the impartial spectator inside a mature person can overrule everyone's applause or jeers, because what it cares about is not being praised but being praiseworthy.

Key Concepts, Fully Unpacked

1. Sympathy: not pity, but imagined exchange of places

What it is. Smith's "sympathy" isn't modern pity, and it isn't quite compassion either. Pity and compassion, he says, are fellow-feeling with sorrow; sympathy can mean fellow-feeling with any passion at all: joy, anger and gratitude included. The mechanism is imagination. We can never feel another person's sensations directly. Our senses, as he puts it (paraphrased), never carry us beyond our own person. All we can do is imagine ourselves into their situation and generate a similar feeling of our own.

Examples. Smith keeps them physical. When we see a blow about to land on someone else's arm or leg, we flinch and pull back our own. A crowd watching a dancer on a slack rope sways and twists along with him. Stranger still, we sympathize with people who feel nothing. A man who has lost his reason and laughs and sings is in no pain himself, yet onlookers are wrung with anguish, because they imagine being in his condition while keeping their present judgment. We even sympathize with the dead, grieving that they lie in cold earth, forgotten, though they feel none of it. The point: sympathy springs from the situation plus the spectator's imagination, not from the other person's feeling as such.

Why it matters. Change the definition and the starting point of ethics changes with it. The Hobbesian story says you help people because you fear ending up in their place. Smith replies that sympathy isn't worry for yourself: a man can sympathize with a woman in childbirth though he can never give birth. Sympathy is a cognitive-plus-emotional simulation. It turns another person into a model that can run inside your own mind.

How it changes your view. Notice that when we assess someone's emotions, we almost never reason about whether they ought to feel that way. We simulate: "in their place, would I?" If a grown man sobs over a lost pen, we don't deduce that he's being irrational. We simply can't simulate that much grief. We can't keep up, so we disapprove.

2. Propriety, and the negotiation of pitch

What it is. For Smith, to approve of someone's feeling is to find that you can keep up with it. If you can, it's "proper"; if you can't, it's too much or too little. The catch is that a spectator's sympathy is always weaker than the original passion: however hard I try, I feel only a fraction of your bereavement. So social life becomes a two-way tuning. The person concerned lowers the pitch of their passion to something an onlooker can follow; the onlooker works to raise theirs by entering more fully into the situation. Where they meet, Smith finds "the pleasure of mutual sympathy." Being understood is pleasant in itself, even when what's understood is pain.

Examples. At a funeral the restrained mourner moves us most; the one who wails and demands that everyone hurt as much as he does pushes people away. A lottery winner who tones down his glee out of regard for friends finds them far happier for him. From here Smith draws a sharp everyday observation: people sympathize more readily with our joy than our sorrow, which is why we parade our riches and hide our poverty. The pain of being poor is hard for others to follow, and showing it mostly earns avoidance.

Two families of virtue grow from this. The spectator's effort to raise the pitch becomes the amiable virtues: sensitivity, tact, indulgent humanity. The sufferer's effort to lower it becomes the respectable (Smith also says "awful") virtues: self-command, fortitude, dignity. An entire moral system emerges from the small business of two people trying to understand each other, with no divine legislator and no utility calculus required.

How it changes your view. Decorum, tact and good breeding stop looking like hypocritical etiquette. They become technologies of emotional cooperation: each person lowering the cost of being understood. Aristotle's "mean" gets a new grounding as well. The right measure is the point a spectator can just keep up with.

3. The impartial spectator: the mirror inside

What it is. The first two ideas explain how we judge others. This one explains how we judge ourselves, and it's the book's finest move. Imagine, says Smith, a person raised alone on a desert island. He could no more think about whether his character is good or bad than whether his face is handsome: he has no mirror. Society is the mirror. We first notice that others are judging us, and begin to imagine how we look to them. To judge ourselves we split in two: the agent being examined, and an examiner who takes a spectator's seat to look at the agent.

Real spectators, though, are neither well-informed nor fair. They don't know our motives and are easily swayed by bias. So over time we imagine a third party who is fully informed and has no stake. Smith calls him "the man within the breast," "the great judge and arbiter of our conduct." Conscience is that judge's voice.

The famous example: an earthquake in China. Smith imagines a humane European who hears that a great earthquake has swallowed up the vast empire of China and all its millions. He will express deep sorrow, muse on the precariousness of life, and then sleep soundly. But if he were to lose his little finger tomorrow, he would lie awake all night. By the measure of raw feeling, one little finger outweighs millions of lives. Yet ask whether he would let those millions die to save his finger, and he recoils in horror. What stops him? Not humanity's soft feelings, Smith says, since that feeble spark can't beat self-love. It is "reason, principle, conscience, the inhabitant of the breast," the spectator who shows us that we are but one of the multitude, in no respect better than any other.

Praise versus praiseworthiness. Here Smith makes his deepest distinction. We want not only to be loved but to be lovely; not only to be praised but to be praiseworthy. The evidence: praise you know you don't deserve gives little real satisfaction, while doing right and being misunderstood by everyone still leaves you on firm ground. If morality were only about pleasing the people around us, neither experience would make sense. The impartial spectator is born from society's gaze and grows into something that can stand against it. It's Smith's strongest answer to the charge that morality is mere conformity.

How it changes your view. Next time you're unsure whether to do something, try Smith's method. Don't ask what people will say. Ask what someone who knew all the facts and had nothing riding on the outcome would think. That stays closer to the situation than asking for a rule, and gives you less room to excuse yourself than asking your own gut.

4. Self-deceit and general rules: why we still need principles

What it is. If we have the man within, why do people still behave badly? Smith is clear-eyed. Before and during the act, passion runs too hot for us to see straight; afterward, we'd rather not look. When we review our own misdeeds we instinctively turn away, and would sooner rekindle the old passions to justify ourselves. "This self-deceit, this fatal weakness of mankind, is the source of half the disorders of human life."

The remedy is general rules. Watching countless actions by others and thinking each time "that's despicable," we gradually build principles: don't betray a trust, don't take advantage of the weak. Their virtue is that they're formed when we're cool and can be used when we're hot. In the grip of passion we may no longer manage impartiality, but we can still remember "this is something I've always held one must not do." Smith calls that regard for rules a sense of duty, and thinks it's what actually keeps most ordinary people decent. Most of us will never be saints, but most of us can keep to rules.

How it changes your view. It's an elegant settlement of the rules-versus-judgment debate. Rules aren't innate commands; they're crystallized judgment. Once formed, though, they protect judgment from being hijacked by passion. "Never send email when angry" and "never negotiate over dinner" are general rules in exactly Smith's sense.

5. Justice and beneficence: pillar and ornament

What it is. Smith starts from our sense of merit and demerit. We approve someone's gratitude because we can sympathize with it, and approve resentment because we sympathize with the victim's anger. That yields two kinds of virtue. Beneficence, meaning generosity and helpfulness, deserves praise but can't be compelled: nobody can sue you for not being generous enough. Justice, meaning not harming another's person, property or reputation, can and should be enforced, because violating it rouses general resentment in spectators.

Examples and lines. Justice is a negative virtue: "We may often fulfil all the rules of justice by sitting still and doing nothing." Smith's analogy is that the rules of justice are like the rules of grammar, precise enough to follow, while the rules of the other virtues are like the standards critics give for elegant writing, vague and grasped only by feel. Hence society can survive without beneficence, as among merchants who feel no affection for each other yet get by on contracts. But without justice it falls apart.

The shadow of luck. Smith noticed something unsettling: we hand out praise and blame by outcome, not intention. Toss a stone carelessly over a wall; whether it hits someone or not makes an enormous difference to the onlooker's anger, though the thrower's state of mind is identical. He calls this the influence of fortune on our sentiments, and thinks the irregularity has a use: it means we answer for deeds, not for mere thoughts. Two centuries later philosophers named this "moral luck," and Smith was one of the first to put it on the table.

How it changes your view. You see why The Wealth of Nations can talk about self-interest alone: it stands on the foundation of justice laid here. A market doesn't need buyer and seller to love each other, only not to steal, cheat or break contracts. "No benevolence needed" doesn't mean "no morality needed." The market needs the hardest piece of morality there is.

6. The deception of ambition, and the first "invisible hand"

What it is. Why do people chase wealth and status so hard? Smith's answer is surprising: not for comfort, but to be seen. "To be observed, to be attended to, to be taken notice of with sympathy, complacency, and approbation, are all the advantages which we can propose to derive from it. It is the vanity, not the ease, or the pleasure, which interests us." The rich man glories in his riches because they draw the world's eyes to him; the poor man is overlooked, as if he weren't there.

The parable of the poor man's son. In Part IV Smith describes a poor man's son "whom heaven in its anger has visited with ambition." He admires the mansions and carriages of the rich, and so spends his life toiling, flattering people he despises and sacrificing real tranquillity, only to find in old age that wealth and greatness are "mere trinkets of frivolous utility" that can't buy peace of body or mind. Then the twist: it is well that nature deceives us this way. That very deception is what drives mankind to cultivate the land, build cities, invent the arts and sciences, and change the face of the earth.

The invisible hand makes its first appearance here, before The Wealth of Nations. The landlord's eyes are bigger than his belly: "The capacity of his stomach bears no proportion to the immensity of his desires." What he grows must end up feeding those who serve him. So the rich "are led by an invisible hand" to distribute the necessities of life almost as if the earth had been divided into equal portions among all its inhabitants, without intending it or knowing it. In this book the invisible hand isn't a hymn to market efficiency. It's an irony about how vanity ends up feeding everyone by accident.

The corruption of the moral sentiments. In a chapter added to the sixth edition Smith puts it more bluntly: the disposition to admire, almost to worship, the rich and powerful, and to despise or at least neglect the poor, is "the great and most universal cause of the corruption of our moral sentiments." We give wealth and rank the respect that belongs to wisdom and virtue. For people of middling station the road to fortune and the road to virtue mostly coincide; higher up they often part ways.

How it changes your view. This shows a side of the "father of the market" few people know. He understood the productive force of ambition and saw through its emptiness. He welcomed the prosperity it brings and refused to call it happiness or virtue. You can hold both "desire drives civilization" and "desire won't satisfy you." For Smith the two ran side by side without contradiction.

The Essential Skeleton

The book establishes one thing: without theology, and without reducing everything to self-interest, human beings can grow a reliable moral order out of natural feeling. The argument is a chain that runs from near to far, from outside to in:

  • Starting point: we can sympathize, entering others' situations through imagination. This is observation, not assumption (the flinch, the tightrope, mourning the dead).
  • Step one: approval means my sympathy can keep up with your feeling. Out of that come propriety and the two families of virtue, from lowering and from raising the pitch.
  • Step two: judging merit and demerit means sympathizing with the gratitude of those helped and the resentment of those harmed. That separates enforceable justice from unenforceable beneficence.
  • Step three: turning judgment on ourselves needs a mirror. Society provides it, imagination refines it into the impartial spectator, and the desire to be praiseworthy lets conscience stand apart from public opinion.
  • Step four: self-deceit makes judgment fail, so experience distills general rules and a sense of duty as guardrails for ordinary people.
  • Step five: the same "wanting to be seen" machinery has side effects, ambition and worship of the rich, which both power prosperity and corrupt judgment.
  • Close: the final part surveys earlier moral systems (Plato, Aristotle, the Stoics, Epicurus, Hutcheson, Mandeville) and argues that each grabbed one link of this chain and mistook it for the whole.

So Smith's answer is neither "morality is derived by reason" nor "morality is computed from interest" but "morality grows out of people watching each other." At the far end of the chain is The Wealth of Nations: once justice is in place and sympathy and self-command keep self-interest in check, the order of exchange can safely be left to the invisible hand.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: "sympathy" means being soft-hearted. As shown above, Smith's sympathy is imaginative fellow-feeling with any passion, anger included. Sympathy with a victim's resentment is what gives punishment its moral footing. The concept is far colder than "soft-hearted."

Misreading two: "Das Adam Smith Problem," the idea that the two books contradict each other. Nineteenth-century German scholars argued that Moral Sentiments is about altruism, The Wealth of Nations about selfishness, and that Smith switched sides. Most scholars now see this as a misreading. First, Smith kept revising Moral Sentiments until 1790, well after Wealth came out, and clearly saw no conflict. Second, Moral Sentiments never denies self-interest; it lists prudence, sensible care for one's own interests, as a virtue. Third, Wealth takes for granted the framework of justice laid down here. The two books are two faces of one model of human nature, not two different human natures.

Misreading three: the invisible hand is a celebration of markets. In this book it turns up in a passage mocking vanity, refers to how goods end up distributed, and appears exactly once (as it does in Wealth). Treating it as the slogan of Smith's whole system is a later inflation.

Criticism one: the hole Hume pointed out. The year the book appeared, Hume, Smith's close friend, wrote to ask: if all sympathy is pleasant, why should sympathizing with someone's pain be pleasant? That isn't how it feels. In the second edition Smith added a footnote distinguishing two feelings: the sympathetic emotion itself, which takes its colour from its object and can be painful, and the feeling of approval at discovering that our feelings match, which is always pleasant. Whether that fully works is still debated, and it shows how many jobs the single word "sympathy" is doing.

Criticism two: is the impartial spectator really impartial? If conscience is internalized from the social mirror around us, it may just be a refined version of local prejudice. Smith himself concedes the power of custom; in Part V he condemns the ancient Greek tolerance of infanticide as custom corrupting judgment. Whether his theory can explain from the inside how the spectator gets beyond custom is the most common challenge to the book. Amartya Sen, in The Idea of Justice, turns this into a defence: Smith's "open impartiality," which deliberately invites the views of distant outsiders, corrects parochial bias better than the "closed impartiality" of Rawls's social contract, where only members of one society make the deal.

Criticism three: rich in observation, thin on justification. Kantian critics argue that Smith describes how people do make moral judgments without showing why those judgments are right; you can't get "worthy of approval" from "we tend to approve." Smith would reply that praiseworthiness is his normative answer. But the book offers no workable procedure for what an ideally impartial spectator would actually approve.

Criticism four: the blind spots of its age. Generalizations such as women being more humane but less generous, and a defence of social rank as good for stability (he thought our sympathy with the great helps keep order), read today as plainly eighteenth century. Intriguingly, they sit in a tension Smith never fully resolved with his own sixth-edition attack on the worship of wealth and power.

Crossovers

  • Neuroscience · empathy versus compassion circuits: Tania Singer and colleagues found that feeling another's pain recruits "shared pain" regions like the anterior insula, while compassion training shifts activity toward circuits tied to warmth and reward, and burns people out less. Like Smith, this separates hurting along with someone from being moved for them; unlike Smith, it doesn't start from imagining a situation.
  • Social psychology · Mead's "generalized other": George Herbert Mead held that the self forms by taking the roles of others, finally internalizing the attitude of the whole group. Both trace the self to a social mirror; but Mead's generalized other simply is the group, whereas Smith's impartial spectator must be able to overrule it.
  • Evolutionary biology · reputation and indirect reciprocity: Nowak and Sigmund (1998) showed that if people track who has helped others (an "image score"), cooperation among strangers can evolve stably. That is the evolutionary ledger behind Smith's "wanting to be seen": our sensitivity to others' eyes was plausibly selected for in small cooperative groups. The gap: evolution rewards being praised, while Smith insists a mature conscience seeks praiseworthiness, something the models give no payoff for.
  • Buddhism · exchanging self and other: in chapter eight of The Way of the Bodhisattva, Śāntideva teaches meditators to imagine trading places with others to break the grip of self-cherishing. Both use imagined exchange to correct the weight of self-love; but Smith exchanges places to calibrate and to build a self-commanding "I," while the Buddhist practice aims to see through the "I" altogether.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① However selfish we may be, some principles in our nature make us care about others' fortunes, and that can't be reduced to self-love.

② Sympathy isn't pity; it's entering another's situation through imagination, and it works for any feeling.

③ To approve of a feeling is to find you can keep up with it; if you can't, it's too much or too little.

④ The sufferer lowers the pitch and the spectator raises it; society meets in the middle. One grows self-command, the other tact.

⑤ People sympathize more easily with joy than sorrow, which is why we parade our riches and hide our poverty.

⑥ Someone raised alone on an island could no more know whether he is good than whether he is handsome. Society is the mirror.

⑦ In feeling, a little finger outweighs millions of lives; in conscience, no one would trade millions of lives for a finger.

⑧ We want not just to be praised but to be praiseworthy. Conscience is born of opinion and can stand against it.

⑨ Justice is the pillar, beneficence the ornament: society can survive without kindness, never without justice.

⑩ Ambition is nature's con. It promises happiness and never pays, yet feeds everyone by accident. The invisible hand began life as irony.
