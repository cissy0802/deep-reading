Mindset

Mindset: The New Psychology of Success · Carol S. Dweck · 2006 (updated edition 2016)

中文 →

In one sentence

You hold a default answer to the question of whether ability can grow, you have probably never said it out loud, and it is already making decisions for you: whether to attempt the harder problem, whether a failure is a verdict on you or an event that happened to you, whether a colleague's success registers as intelligence or as threat. Dweck spent thirty years showing that this silent assumption grows into two complete and mutually exclusive systems of behaviour, each of which manufactures its own evidence. How much leverage it gives you is another matter — the book claims considerably more than the large replication studies since have supported, and that gap is itself the most useful thing to learn from reading it now.

Where it sits

Carol Dweck (b. 1946) is a professor of psychology at Stanford, formerly at Columbia. Her research began with learned helplessness in children — the giving-up state that follows repeated failure — and with a puzzle inside it: given the same unsolvable problem, why do some children collapse while others become visibly energized? Through the 1970s and 80s she located the answer in a single belief, which the literature calls an implicit theory of intelligence: you privately assume either that intelligence is a fixed quantity (entity theory) or that it is something that grows (incremental theory). Mindset (2006) is the popular version of that thirty-year line of work; "fixed" and "growth" mindset are the academic terms in better clothes. The book went on to become the most widely adopted concept in global education — and consequently absorbed the heaviest replication scrutiny that educational psychology has run in two decades.

The central claims

  • What decides outcomes is not ability but what you do when ability runs out — and that step is governed by your default assumption about what ability is. The same bad test result reads to one person as "which part do I need to drill" and to another as "so this is my ceiling."
  • Each assumption grows a coherent system of behaviour, and each system verifies itself. People who take ability as fixed avoid challenge and hide weakness, so they really do learn more slowly, which proves the point. People who take it as growable move toward difficulty and really do grow. The belief is not predicting the fact; it is producing it.
  • Mindset is not a personality type but a state that context can trigger and that can be changed. This is the book's most hopeful claim and its most fragile one. Nearly every later controversy attaches to its second half: changed by how much, and worth how much.

The core concepts, one at a time

The two mindsets: a belief about ability, not about yourself

Clear away the usual confusion first. A fixed mindset is not low self-esteem and a growth mindset is not confidence. They answer a different question: is ability a quantity you carry, or a thing that grows? A highly confident person can be thoroughly fixed — confident precisely because he believes he was born with it, which is why the confidence cannot survive one real failure. Someone in a growth frame may rate his current level as poor and be entirely untroubled, because in his ledger the current level is an opening reading.

Why does the difference matter so much? Because the assumption determines what "difficulty" means. If ability is fixed, a hard task is a measurement — it is telling you how much you have. If ability grows, a hard task is training — it is telling you where to push. Same event; in one case a medical exam, in the other a workout. Nobody behaves the same way in both.

Goals: why the best students fear hard problems most

Two goal orientations follow immediately: performance goals (be seen to be good) and learning goals (become good). On easy tasks they are indistinguishable. The moment a task gets hard they diverge.

Dweck's most counterintuitive finding is that the fixed mindset does more damage to high achievers. The mechanism is clean: a child who has long been labelled "the smart one" holds credentials that require constant renewal, and hard problems are the only thing that can invalidate them. So he makes a choice that is entirely rational inside his own ledger — he picks the problems he is sure he can get right. What observers see is reliability. What is actually happening is that he has locked himself inside his current boundary, and ability only grows at the boundary. It explains a familiar life trajectory: the child for whom everything came easily breaks hardest at the first opponent he genuinely cannot beat.

The effort paradox: why not trying is the optimal fixed-mindset strategy

The finest piece of mechanism in the book. In the fixed ledger, needing to work hard is itself evidence of insufficient talent — real ability shouldn't require it. Visible effort therefore becomes humiliating, and that produces a strategy that looks absurd and is in fact airtight: self-handicapping, manufacturing an excuse for failure in advance in order to protect your sense of worth.

Gaming all night before the exam, writing the paper in the last available hour, skipping training the week before the match — these look self-destructive and are actually insurance. As long as you never went all out, the result is never a verdict on your ability. Lose, and it was the preparation. Win, and the talent must be extraordinary. The premium is simply that you never find out what you could have done. In the growth frame effort is the only road, so there is no policy to buy.

Failure as identity or as event: the children who talked to themselves

What turned this distinction from reasoning into data was a set of studies Dweck ran with Diane Diener in 1978–1980. Schoolchildren worked through a series of problems, solvable at first and then deliberately pitched above their level, while saying aloud whatever they were thinking.

The two response patterns differ so sharply they look like two species. The "helpless" children turned immediately to self-assessment once the problems hardened — my memory is bad, I was never good at this — and some began talking about entirely unrelated things (a pet at home, last week's talent show). Their strategies visibly degraded, in some cases regressing to methods far below the level they had already demonstrated. The "mastery-oriented" children barely mentioned themselves at all; they talked about the problem. One rubbed his hands and said he loved a challenge; another muttered that he should try a different approach; one, stuck, offered that mistakes are our friends (paraphrased). The crucial detail: the two groups had shown no difference in ability beforehand. The difference was not skill. It was which column the failure got filed under — "me" or "this problem."

The praise experiment: telling a child he's smart announces that he is being measured

The most widely circulated part of the book, and the part most worth getting right. Claudia Mueller and Dweck ran six studies in 1998 with several hundred fifth-graders. Every child first did a set of manageable problems and was told they had done well; then each was randomly given one of two follow-up sentences: praise for ability ("you must be smart at these") or praise for process ("you must have worked hard at these"). From that one sentence:

  • Offered a choice of next task, the ability-praised children more often took the one they could perform well on; the effort-praised more often took the one they could learn from.
  • After a deliberately hard set that frustrated everybody, the ability-praised children reported less enjoyment and performed worse; the effort-praised held steady or improved.
  • In the last study, children wrote their scores on a sheet supposedly going to students at another school — and a substantial proportion of the ability-praised children misreported them.

The mechanism deserves spelling out. "You're so smart" is not encouragement; it is an announcement of the grading criterion. The implied premise is that there exists a fixed quantity called smartness and that adults are currently measuring it. The child rationally switches into managing that measurement — take the safe problems, retreat when frustrated, adjust the number if necessary. The lesson is not "don't praise children." It is that every evaluative remark simultaneously transmits a rule about what is being taken seriously, and children absorb rules far more efficiently than they absorb encouragement. (In fairness: replications of these praise manipulations have been mixed. What has held up better is longitudinal observation — Gunderson and colleagues (2013) found that parents' use of process praise when children were one to three predicted more growth-oriented beliefs at seven or eight. Correlational, but pointing the same way.)

Two years of middle school: the one place the divergence was actually watched

Cross-sectional experiments can only show that reactions differ in the moment. To show that the difference accumulates into a gap you need longitudinal data, and that is the role of the 2007 study by Lisa Blackwell, Kali Trzesniewski and Dweck — the strongest single piece of evidence the book has on its own side.

The researchers measured mindset in roughly 370 New York students entering seventh grade and then tracked two years of math grades. Middle-school math is a natural stress test: the difficulty steps up sharply and everyone eventually meets a problem they cannot do. The result: students who entered at comparable levels diverged over the following two years — the incremental theorists' grade curve rose, the entity theorists' stayed flat. Mindset still predicted the slope after controlling statistically for incoming achievement. Interviews gave the same account the lab had: one group read a bad grade as "time to change method," the other as "this subject isn't my thing," and behaviour forked from there.

The same paper included an intervention: eight sessions for a smaller group of about ninety students, teaching that the brain grows new connections with practice, like a muscle — note that this is not a pep talk but a concrete physiological mechanism offered for belief. That group's declining grade trend stopped and reversed while the control group's continued down, and teachers blind to the assignment named disproportionately many of the intervention students when asked who had recently changed in motivation. In fairness: an intervention sample of ninety-odd is exactly the kind of study whose effect sizes later meta-analyses pulled down — but the mechanism it demonstrates has not been overturned.

"Not yet": giving failure a time axis

The smallest and most portable device in the book. Dweck describes a Chicago high school where students who did not pass a course received, instead of a failing grade, the grade "Not Yet." Changing one word changes the grammar of the whole sentence: "fail" is a verdict on a state of affairs; "not yet" puts the same fact back on a timeline, and the sentence now contains a future.

It is more than a phrasing trick because it targets the fixed mindset's actual sore point. The pain there was never "I can't do this now"; it was that "I can't do this now" got read as "this is what I am." "Not yet" does not deny the failure. It refuses to let the failure take the perfect tense. Its limits should be stated too: this is an intervention at the level of wording, changing the interpretation and not the ability, and treating it as a promise that redeems itself is precisely the disease described next.

False growth mindset: the correction Dweck issued herself

After the book took off, Dweck published corrections around 2015 describing three ways her concept was being misused. This is one of the most valuable parts of the material today, and among the least quoted.

  • Treating growth mindset as "praise effort." She calls this the biggest misuse: effort without strategy is not a growth mindset, it is flailing. A person who has practised the wrong method for three years should not be congratulated on working hard; he needs a different method, better resources, someone to ask. The full content is effort plus strategy plus help-seeking, and keeping only the first turns it into a placebo that encourages people to keep walking into the same wall.
  • Treating it as a slogan on the wall. Posters go up and assemblies declare that every child can grow, while the grading scheme, the tracking system and the actual classroom response to a wrong answer stay exactly as they were. Children read the system, not the poster.
  • Treating it as a personality label. She notes that "I'm a growth-mindset person" is itself a fixed-mindset sentence. Everyone is a mixture of both, and everyone has triggers: some flip to fixed the moment they are criticized publicly, others the moment they meet a peer who is plainly better. The usable practice is not to label yourself but to learn your own triggers and recognize, while it is happening, that this is the reaction and not the fact.

Schematic: both mindsets confirm themselves, so from the inside neither feels like a belief. Both feel like experience.

Organizations and relationships: real insight, thinnest evidence

The back half of the book ports the framework into companies, couples and sport. Its sharpest cut is the diagnosis of the genius-worship culture: when a firm defines itself as hiring only the smartest people, it installs a fixed mindset organization-wide. Every employee's first job becomes proving continued membership in that group, so bad news goes unreported, ignorance goes unadmitted, and failing projects go uncancelled. Dweck uses Enron — a company that marketed itself on the talent myth and ended up destroyed partly because nobody could afford to be wrong. The relationships chapter lands a similar blow: people who believe the right partner should feel effortless read the first patch requiring work as evidence that this is the wrong partner.

But this has to be labelled honestly: these chapters are narrative, not data. The corporate cases are stories selected after the fact (Enron's collapse has a long list of accounting and governance causes; culture is at most one line item), and the sports cases — McEnroe cast as fixed, Jordan, who as a sophomore did not make the varsity squad, cast as growth — are retrospective labelling, since winners can always be narrated as growth-minded. Reading the first half (controlled studies) and the second half (famous stories retold through the frame) with different levels of credence is the single most important move when reading this book.

The argument, stripped to its spine

The whole book is one tightly linked causal chain: a default assumption about whether ability can change → it fixes the meaning of difficulty (measurement or training) → which fixes goal orientation (perform or learn) → which fixes how you read effort, failure, criticism and others' success → which fixes behaviour → which produces real results → which confirm the original assumption.

The chain's force lies in its closure. Both mindsets verify themselves, so from inside neither one feels like a belief; both feel like experience. A fixed-mindset person genuinely does hold a pile of evidence that he isn't good at things — evidence his own avoidance produced. That is why Dweck locates the intervention point at the belief rather than the behaviour: telling someone to take on more challenge does not work, because within his ledger taking on challenge runs at a loss.

And the same chain marks where the book is oversold. Every link has experimental support; how much force the whole chain transmits is a separate question — between "belief shapes interpretation" and "changing the belief measurably changes grades two years later" sit family, money, teaching quality, peers and curriculum, all of them far heavier variables than belief. The book treats that distance optimistically. Most of the last fifteen years of research has been an attempt to measure it.

Misreadings, and the serious objections

Misreading one: growth mindset means effort is enough. Dweck has publicly corrected this: effort without strategy does not count. The full version is effort, strategy and help-seeking.

Misreading two: it means unlimited potential, anyone can become anything. She has explicitly denied this. The claim is that ability can be developed, not that starting points are identical or that anyone can be Einstein.

Misreading three: it means positivity, so don't say negative things. The opposite: the framework requires taking criticism in as data. Ducking honest feedback is a signature fixed-mindset move.

Misreading four: it is a personality type you can label people with. See false growth mindset above.

The core controversy: how big is the effect, and does it replicate? This has to be laid out properly; it is unavoidable when reading the book today.

  • The correlation is far weaker than advertised. Sisk and colleagues' 2018 meta-analysis in Psychological Science (a meta-analysis pools effect sizes across many studies of the same question) found an average correlation between mindset and academic achievement of about r = 0.10 — meaning mindset accounts for something on the order of one per cent of the variation in results. Average intervention effects were similarly small.
  • It has failed to replicate across cultures. Yue Li and Timothy Bates were unable to reproduce either the mindset–achievement link or the praise-manipulation effects in samples of Chinese children.
  • The most credible test: real, but small and conditional. The National Study of Learning Mindsets (Yeager et al., Nature, 2019) is the methodologically hardest work in the field: around 12,000 US ninth-graders, randomly assigned, pre-registered (hypotheses and analysis plan filed publicly before the study, which blocks after-the-fact cherry-picking), delivered by an independent organization. The answer is worth learning from: two online sessions of about an hour in total had a very small average effect on GPA overall; among students who had been lower-achieving it raised GPA by roughly 0.1 points; and the effect appeared only in schools whose peer norms supported taking on challenge.
  • The meta-analyses are still fighting. Macnamara and Burgoyne's 2023 systematic review in Psychological Bulletin concluded that once publication bias (the systematic overestimate caused by positive results being likelier to get published) and low-quality studies are discounted, support for general effectiveness is weak; other researchers' meta-analyses report stable effects in targeted populations under high-fidelity delivery. Dweck's side answers largely on fidelity: most school-brand "growth mindset" is one of the three corrupted versions above.

And a heavier objection: it is so usable that it can substitute for the real problem. When a school is short of money, losing teachers and feeding hungry children, "build growth mindsets" is an extremely cheap programme — it translates a structural problem into a psychological one and transfers responsibility for fixing it to the party with the fewest resources. That is not Dweck's argument, but it is the most common real-world use of her concept.

So how should it be read now? An honest landing point: what wears best is the phenomenology — the behavioural loop of the fixed mindset, which nearly everyone can recognize in at least one corner of their own life, and recognizing it is itself worth something. What wears worst is the size of the lever it promises. As a pair of glasses for spotting the situations in which you switch into defensive mode, it is very good. As an educational programme for raising achievement, the evidence does not support the claim.

Ten sentences

1. What decides outcomes is not ability but what you do when ability runs out — and that is governed by an assumption you have never said out loud: can ability grow?

2. The assumption fixes the meaning of difficulty: if ability is fixed, a hard task is a medical exam; if it grows, a hard task is a workout. Nobody behaves the same way in both.

3. Which is why the fixed mindset hurts high achievers most: the "smart one" credential needs constant renewal, and hard problems are the only thing that can invalidate it.

4. In the fixed ledger, needing effort equals lacking talent. So gaming all night before the exam isn't self-destruction, it's insurance: as long as you never went all out, the score is never a verdict.

5. Stuck on a problem, one kind of child starts talking about himself ("my memory is bad"), the other about the problem ("let me try another way"). The two groups had shown equal ability; the difference was only which column the failure was filed under.

6. "You're so smart" is not encouragement, it is an announcement of the grading criterion — there's a fixed quantity here and adults are measuring it. The child switches to managing the measurement: safe problems, early retreat, and if necessary an adjusted number.

7. Replace "fail" with "not yet." It doesn't deny the failure; it refuses to let the failure take the perfect tense.

8. Dweck's own correction is the part to remember: effort without strategy isn't a growth mindset, it's flailing — and the sentence "I'm a growth-mindset person" is itself fixed-mindset grammar.

9. Both mindsets confirm themselves, so from inside neither feels like a belief; the fixed-mindset person's pile of evidence that he can't do things was manufactured by his own avoidance.

10. The honest bottom line: the best large randomized trial (Nature, 2019) found an hour of intervention raised GPA by about 0.1 points, only for previously lower-achieving students, and only where school norms cooperated. As glasses for spotting your own defensive mode, excellent; as a programme for raising grades, unsupported.
