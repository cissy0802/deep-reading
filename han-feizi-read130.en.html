Han Feizi

Han Feizi · Han Fei · mid-3rd century BCE

中文 →

In One Sentence

The Confucians bet the peace of the world on people becoming good. Han Fei says the arithmetic doesn't work: sages appear once in a thousand years, good people are one in ten, and government has to handle millions of ordinary ones. So don't count on people doing right by you; make sure they have no room to do wrong — build, out of public and inescapable law, techniques for handling ministers, and a position of power that is never shared, a machine of rule that even a mediocre king can drive.

Where It Sits

Han Fei (c. 280–233 BCE) was a prince of the small state of Han in the final decades of the Warring States period — a stammerer and a superb writer who studied, alongside Li Si, under the Confucian Xunzi. He fused three earlier strands of Legalist thought into one system, which makes him the great synthesizer of Legalism (fajia). The king of Qin, the future First Emperor, read his essays and said he could die content if he could only spend time with this man. When Han Fei was later sent to Qin as an envoy, Li Si had him thrown in prison and sent him poison. His ideas went on to become the operating system with which Qin unified China — and shared the blame when Qin collapsed after only fifteen years.

The Core Argument

  • Behavior is driven by interest, not virtue. This is not a moral accusation but a design premise: don't ask "is he a good person?", ask "what does he gain by doing this?"
  • Government must work for the many, not the few — for average people, not saints. "The sage does not rely on people doing good for him; he relies on their being unable to do wrong" (from the chapter "Eminence in Learning"). An arrow that grows straight by itself, a piece of wood that grows round by itself, is not found once in a hundred generations; good craftsmen rely on their straightening tools, not on miracles.
  • When the times change, the methods must change. "As the age differs, affairs differ; as affairs differ, preparations must change" ("The Five Vermin"). Governing today's chaos with the benevolence of the ancient kings is like waiting by a tree stump for another rabbit.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. "Loving profit, hating harm": human nature as a constraint, not a project

Han Fei's teacher Xunzi had said that "human nature is bad," but drew the conclusion that ritual and learning could transform it — people could be taught to be good. Han Fei kept the first half and threw out the second: people don't need to be made good, only to be calculated correctly. His view of human nature reads like an economist's assumption: people pursue benefit and avoid harm, and under any given set of incentives they will do what is best for themselves.

His examples are chilling. In "Guarding Against the Inner Court," he notes that a doctor will suck the pus from a patient's wound not because they are kin but because there is profit in it; the carriage-maker hopes everyone grows rich, while the coffin-maker hopes everyone dies young. "It is not that the carriage-maker is kind and the coffin-maker cruel" — if people don't get rich, no one buys carriages; if people don't die, no one buys coffins. Put the same person in a different trade and his wishes change with it. He even takes the scalpel to the family: in "The Six Contradictions" he observes that parents of his day congratulated each other on the birth of a son but sometimes killed a newborn daughter. Both came from the same parents' arms; the difference was a calculation about who would provide for them later. Even between parent and child, there is "reckoning of long-term advantage."

What makes this view so useful is that it turns "bad people" from a moral problem into an engineering problem. A Confucian sees a corrupt official and thinks: his virtue is lacking, he needs moral cultivation. Han Fei sees the same official and asks: what rules made corruption pay for him? Think of a bridge engineer. She never assumes the wind will be gentle or the trucks will stay under the weight limit; she assumes the worst load and designs a bridge that stands anyway. Han Fei is that engineer, and his bridge does not rest on human goodness.

His sharpest application of the idea comes in "The Five Vermin." In high antiquity there were few people and plenty of things: "men did not plow, for the fruits of plants and trees were enough to eat; women did not weave, for the skins of animals were enough to wear," so nobody fought. Now "a man with five sons is not thought to have many, and each son has five sons, so that before the grandfather dies there are twenty-five grandsons" — more people, less wealth, and so struggle. "The ancients made light of goods not because they were kind, but because goods were plentiful; people today fight over them not because they are base, but because goods are scarce." Then he dismantles the most sacred Confucian story of all: the legendary sage-kings Yao and Shun, who handed the throne to the worthiest man rather than to their sons. When Yao was ruler, Han Fei says, he lived in a thatched hut, ate coarse grain and wore deerskin — a gatekeeper lived no worse. When Yu ruled, he dug flood channels with his own hands until the hair wore off his shins. Giving away a job like that was just quitting a miserable post; what is there to praise? Today, when a county magistrate dies, his descendants ride in carriages for generations, so everyone prizes the office. The same act of "yielding" rests on different structures of interest, not different levels of virtue. Once you learn to see this way you can't unsee it: behind every noble-looking act you start asking who is paying for it.

2. Law (fa): public, uniform, inescapable rules

The strand of Law comes mainly from Shang Yang, who reformed the state of Qin in the 4th century BCE. In "Settling the Laws," Han Fei defines it like this: "Laws are decrees displayed in the government offices; punishments are certain in the minds of the people; rewards go to those who carefully obey the law, and penalties fall on those who violate orders." Three requirements are packed in here. It must be public — written down and posted so everyone knows what is allowed. It must be uniform — "The law does not bend for the noble, as the plumb line does not bend for crooked wood… punishment for wrongdoing does not spare high ministers, reward for good does not pass over commoners" ("Having Standards"). And it must be certain — penalties always land, rewards always arrive, so that people are sure of it.

Why do these three matter more than whether the law is harsh or mild? Han Fei keeps returning to one contrast: "A strict household has no unruly servants, but a loving mother has spoiled sons." The point is not cruelty but predictability. Once rules can be bargained over or bypassed through connections, people's energy shifts from doing the work well to cultivating the right people. The automated traffic camera is a modern version of fa: it doesn't know who you are and won't hear your excuses, so nobody bothers trying to sweet-talk it — they just stop running red lights.

His Law also comes with a rider that makes modern readers wince: "Make the law the only teaching, and officials the only teachers" ("The Five Vermin"). The state needs no books from the rival schools; the statutes are the curriculum. This is the thought-control side of Legalism, and when Li Si proposed burning the books in 213 BCE, he used almost exactly this language.

Han Fei also defends heavy punishment. Quoting Shang Yang, he argues roughly that if you punish light offenses severely, light offenses won't happen and serious ones will never arrive (paraphrased). In his calculus, harsh penalties aren't cruelty but "using punishment to end punishment": push the expected cost of wrongdoing so high that no one tries, and in the end the penalties are never needed. The logic is clean. In practice it became one of Legalism's biggest holes (see the criticisms below).

3. Technique (shu): the art of handling ministers

The strand of Technique comes mainly from Shen Buhai, chief minister of Han in the same era. If Law is public and aimed at the people, Technique is kept hidden in the ruler's mind and aimed at the bureaucracy. "Settling the Laws" defines it: "Technique means granting office according to ability, holding names accountable to realities, wielding the power of life and death, and examining the competence of the ministers — this is what the ruler holds."

At the heart of Technique is "holding names accountable to realities" — known as xingming, matching "forms and names". A minister first declares what he will do (the name); the ruler assigns the task on that basis; and at the end the ruler checks whether the result (the form) matches the claim exactly. Han Fei takes this to an extreme: claim big and deliver small, you are punished; claim small and deliver big, you are punished too. Why punish overachievement? Because "it is not that the ruler dislikes great results, but that a mismatch between name and reality does more harm than a great result does good." Today he delivers more than he promised; tomorrow he delivers less, and he has learned that he can explain himself however he likes.

The companion story is the famous one of the Keeper of the Cap ("The Two Handles"). Marquis Zhao of Han fell asleep drunk; the official in charge of his hat saw that he was cold and laid a robe over him. On waking, the marquis was pleased and asked who had covered him. Told it was the Keeper of the Cap, he punished both the Keeper of the Robes and the Keeper of the Cap — the first for neglecting his duty, the second for exceeding his. Han Fei's comment: "It was not that he didn't mind the cold, but that the harm of officials trespassing on each other's posts was greater than the cold." A chill passes; once overstepping your role is rewarded, the whole division of labor starts to rot.

The idiom "a fake piper in the band" (lanyu chongshu) is also a story about Technique, from the "Inner Collection of Sayings": King Xuan of Qi liked to hear the reed pipe played by three hundred musicians at once, and a certain Mr. Nanguo, who couldn't play at all, slipped in and drew a salary. When King Xuan died, his successor preferred to hear the players one by one, and Mr. Nanguo fled in the night. Ensemble playing dissolves individual responsibility; hearing them one by one pins it on a specific person. Management theory calls this accountability. Han Fei called it shu.

The other half of Technique is the ruler's stillness and concealment of likes and dislikes. "The Two Handles" lists the evidence: King Goujian of Yue loved courage, so many of his people threw away their lives; King Ling of Chu loved slim waists, so his court was full of people starving themselves; Duke Huan of Qi was jealous and fond of women, so Shu Diao castrated himself to run the harem; the duke loved fine food, so Yi Ya steamed his own son's head and served it to him. Conclusion: "When the ruler shows what he hates, the ministers hide their true motives; when he shows what he likes, they fake abilities." The moment you reveal a preference, the people below you disguise themselves as what you prefer, and you never see the truth again. So the ruler must "discard likes and dislikes, and the ministers will show their bare selves." This changes how you see organizations: when a boss complains that "people only bring me good news," the problem is usually the boss, whose face has long since told everyone which news gets rewarded.

4. Positional power (shi): the force of the seat itself

The strand of positional power comes from Shen Dao, a scholar at the Jixia academy in Qi. His image: "The flying dragon rides the clouds, the soaring serpent glides on the mist; when the clouds disperse and the mist lifts, dragon and serpent are no different from earthworms and ants — they have lost what they rode on." The dragon flies because of the cloud. Likewise, the sage-king Yao as a commoner could not have governed three people, while the tyrant Jie, as Son of Heaven, could throw the whole world into chaos. Power doesn't live in the person; it lives in the position.

In "Critique of Positional Power," Han Fei answers the Confucian rebuttal: if position lets Yao bring order and Jie bring chaos, isn't it neutral — something that only a worthy man can use well? His answer is the single most important move in the book. Yao and Shun, Jie and Zhou are extremes who appear once in a thousand generations; almost every actual ruler is average — not as good as Yao and Shun, not as bad as Jie and Zhou. If you wait for a Yao, you get a thousand generations of chaos for one of order. But "hold to the law and occupy the position, and there is order; abandon the law and give up the position, and there is chaos." With law and position, an average ruler can govern well, which means a thousand generations of order for one of chaos. Think of it this way: you can't expect every captain to be a naval genius, but you can build a ship that an ordinary captain following procedure can sail safely.

Position has a second meaning: power cannot be lent. In "The Two Handles," Han Fei says the tiger dominates the dog because of its claws and fangs; if the tiger hands them to the dog, the tiger will be dominated by the dog. The two handles are punishment (killing) and favor (reward), and once a minister wields them on the ruler's behalf, the people will fear and love the minister instead of the ruler. His examples: in Qi, the minister Tian Chang asked for the power to grant favors and lent grain to the people with an oversized measure to win them over; Duke Jian of Qi was eventually murdered, and the Tian clan later replaced the ruling house outright. In Song, the minister Zihan told his lord, "Rewards are what the people love — you hand them out; punishments are what they hate — let me bear that burden." He took control of punishment and ended up holding his lord hostage. Whoever holds reward and punishment is the real master.

5. "The Difficulties of Persuasion": why advising the powerful is so dangerous

Han Fei stammered and was poor at face-to-face speech, yet he wrote the most penetrating essay on the psychology of persuasion in Chinese history, "The Difficulties of Persuasion" (Shuinan). He opens: the difficulty isn't whether I know enough or speak well; it lies in "knowing the mind of the person I'm persuading, so that what I say fits it."

He tells two stories. First: Duke Wu of Zheng wanted to attack the state of Hu, so he married his daughter to the ruler of Hu to gain his trust, then asked his ministers, "I want to go to war — whom should I attack?" The minister Guan Qisi said, "Hu can be attacked." The duke had him executed: "Hu is a brother state, and you tell me to attack it!" Hearing this, the ruler of Hu concluded that Zheng was a true friend and dropped his guard, and Zheng took Hu in a surprise attack. Second: a rich man in Song had a wall collapse in the rain. His son said, "If you don't rebuild it, thieves will get in," and an old neighbor said the same. That night they were robbed; the family praised the son's wisdom and suspected the neighbor. Han Fei's verdict: both men were right, yet one was killed and the other suspected. "It is not knowing that is hard; it is knowing what to do with what you know."

Then there is Mizi Xia, favorite of Duke Ling of Wey. While he was in favor, he once took the duke's carriage without permission to visit his sick mother, and the duke praised his devotion; once he bit into a sweet peach and offered the rest to the duke, who praised his love. When his looks faded and the favor cooled, the duke dredged it all up: "This man once stole my carriage, and once fed me his leftover peach." The same deeds were merits when he was loved and crimes when he was not; the deeds hadn't changed, only the listener's heart. Hence the essay's most famous image: a dragon is gentle enough to ride, but under its throat it has a foot-long scale that grows the wrong way. Touch it and the dragon will kill you. Rulers, too, have their reversed scale.

The tragedy is the author himself. The historian Sima Qian lamented: "I grieve only that Master Han wrote 'The Difficulties of Persuasion' and yet could not save himself." The man who understood persuasion best died of it. The lesson: the fate of advice depends not only on whether it is right, but on your relationship with the listener, his position, and his mood at that moment. It is the coldest and truest lesson of working life.

6. "The Five Vermin" and the spear and shield: a systematic case against Confucianism

"The Five Vermin" names five kinds of parasites in the state: scholars (the Confucians), speech-makers (itinerant diplomats who sold alliances), sword-bearers (knights-errant), those who dodge military service by attaching themselves to powerful families, and merchants and artisans. Its most famous line: "Confucians use learning to throw the law into confusion; knights-errant use force to break the prohibitions." And the ruler honors both — which means rewarding the people who break his rules. His test has one question: does this kind of person contribute to farming and fighting, the two sources of a state's strength?

The fable of "waiting by the stump" comes from this chapter. A farmer in Song saw a rabbit run into a tree stump and break its neck, so he put down his plow and waited by the stump for another. No rabbit came, and he became the laughingstock of the state. Han Fei continues: "Those who wish to govern the people of today with the policies of the ancient kings are all stump-watchers." His history moves in three stages: "In high antiquity men competed in virtue; in the middle ages they vied in cleverness; today they struggle by strength." Each age competes for something different. The Warring States compete in raw power, and anyone still preaching benevolence is waiting by the stump.

The word for "contradiction" in Chinese — maodun, literally "spear-shield" — is Han Fei's invention ("Critiques, Part One"). A man of Chu was selling shields and spears. He boasted that his shield was so strong nothing could pierce it, and that his spear was so sharp it could pierce anything. Someone asked, "What if I use your spear on your shield?" He had no answer. Han Fei aimed the story straight at the Confucians, who praised Yao as a perfect sage-ruler and praised Shun for going around the country correcting people's faults. If Yao was truly perfect, there would have been no faults to correct; if Shun had to correct them, Yao wasn't perfect. Praising both is selling the spear and the shield. The deeper point: transforming people one by one, the way a sage does (Shun fixed one problem a year), is far too slow. A single decree, backed by rewards and penalties, reaches the whole realm in ten days.

7. Explaining Laozi: the Daoist core beneath the cold machinery

The book contains two chapters, "Explaining Laozi" and "Illustrating Laozi," which are the oldest surviving commentary on the Daodejing. They are not decoration: Han Fei turned Laozi's Dao into the ruler's inner discipline. The Dao is formless and without desire, so the ruler too must be "empty and still" — reveal no intentions, let the ministers speak and act, and simply check names against results at the end. "The Way of the Ruler" says: "The ruler must not reveal what he wants; if he does, his ministers will carve and polish themselves to match it." Laozi's non-action (wuwei) becomes an intensely active kind of non-action: the ruler does nothing so that everyone else does everything for him without being able to deceive him.

That is why Sima Qian put Laozi and Han Fei in the same biographical chapter, writing that Han Fei "applied the carpenter's line to the facts of the matter and made right and wrong clear, carrying it to the point of harshness and little mercy — yet all of it is rooted in the meaning of the Dao and its power." Grasp this and you stop seeing Han Fei as a mere theorist of cruelty: he used Daoist metaphysics to install a selfless, egoless control center in a cold machine of power.

The Skeleton

Compress all fifty-five chapters into a single line and here is what the book sets out to prove: in a world of self-interested people, scarce resources and states swallowing one another, the only reliable order comes from institutions, not from human hearts.

The argument runs in four steps. First, the premise: people love profit and hate harm, and the times have changed — more people, less wealth, so struggle is normal, not decadence ("The Five Vermin"). Second, the refutation: the Confucians bet on sages, but sages appear once in a thousand generations; betting on rule by virtue means being out of control most of the time ("Eminence in Learning," "Critique of Positional Power"). Third, the construction: use law to fix the people's expectations, technique to control the bureaucracy in the middle, and position to keep the power of reward and punishment from leaking away. Drop any one and you get a specific failure — Shang Yang had law without technique, so Qin grew strong but the gains went to powerful ministers; Shen Buhai had technique without law, so old and new statutes coexisted in Han and crafty officials always picked whichever suited them ("Settling the Laws"). Fourth, the warning: the ruler's gravest danger comes from those closest to him. "The Eight Villainies" lists eight routes by which treacherous ministers gain power, beginning with "those who share the bed" (consorts), "those at his side" (attendants) and "fathers and brothers" (royal kin). "Guarding Against the Inner Court" warns that the queen and crown prince may wish him dead. "Signs of Ruin" lists dozens of symptoms of a dying state.

What is the evidence? Not metaphysical deduction but a vast stock of historical cases. The "Inner and Outer Collections of Sayings" and "Forest of Persuasions" are practically an ancient case library for management; the man of Zheng who trusted his measuring string over his own feet when buying shoes, the fake piper, the spear and shield, the stump-watcher all come from here. Like Machiavelli's The Prince, the book persuades through its merciless observation of how power actually works, not through its moral stance.

Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: Legalism means the rule of law. This one most needs dismantling. The core of the modern rule of law is that power itself is subject to law. Han Fei's law is an instrument in the ruler's hands; "the law does not bend for the noble" binds ministers and subjects, never the ruler. "Rule by law" is more accurate. Nothing in his system can restrain the ruler — the whole machine has exactly one unchecked component, and it is the most important one. That is why a single weak ruler can bring the whole thing down: the Second Emperor of Qin, manipulated by the eunuch Zhao Gao, was precisely a ruler captured by "those at his side."

Misreading 2: Han Fei simply teaches cruelty. He started from a real problem: two centuries of incessant war, hereditary aristocratic privilege, laws that changed overnight. "Punishment does not spare high ministers; reward does not pass over commoners" was a radical challenge to aristocratic privilege in its day, and granting rank for merit under published law gave commoners, for the first time, a way up that didn't depend on birth. Reduce him to a manual for tyrants and you miss his deep insight into why institutions are more reliable than people.

Criticism 1: Qin's rapid collapse. Qin unified China with Legalist methods in 221 BCE and fell fifteen years later. The early Han scholar Jia Yi, in "The Faults of Qin," diagnosed it this way: "It failed to practice benevolence and righteousness, not realizing that the power to attack and the power to hold are different." Harsh punishment and coercion can mobilize a country for war but cannot win its loyalty. Tradition has it that the first great rebellion broke out because a band of conscripts was delayed by rain and "for lateness, the law is death" (excavated Qin legal texts suggest the real penalty may have been far lighter, so the story may be embellished, but popular hatred of Qin law is not in doubt). It is the strongest empirical rebuttal of "heavy punishment ends punishment": when every offense is a capital crime, anyone who has offended has no reason not to rebel.

Criticism 2: the collapse of information and trust. Technique requires the ruler to hide his mind, ministers to watch each other, and suspicion everywhere — and the result is a system where no one tells the truth. Han Fei was himself a casualty of the machine. Li Si used his own logic against him: Han Fei is a prince of Han; in the end he will work for Han, not Qin — that's only human nature (paraphrased). If people act only on interest, a jealous former classmate needs only one sentence. A system that assumes everyone is scheming trains everyone to scheme.

Criticism 3: a one-dimensional human nature. The Confucians, and many critics since, point out that people care not only about gain but also about shame, loyalty, honor and meaning; converting everything into reward and punishment crowds out these free sources of order. Confucius had already said: "Lead them with regulations and keep them in line with punishments, and the people will stay out of trouble but have no sense of shame" (Analects 2.3). This is Han Fei's most fatal blind spot.

Criticism 4: anti-intellectualism and thought control. "Make the law the only teaching," and listing scholars among the vermin, led directly to Qin's burning of the books. A regime that seals off criticism loses its ability to correct itself.

The historical outcome: Confucian on the outside, Legalist on the inside. Whatever the criticisms, Legalism never really left the stage. Emperor Xuan of Han said, "The house of Han has its own system, which mixes the way of the hegemon with the way of the king" — Legalist force blended with Confucian virtue. For the next two thousand years Chinese government spoke the language of benevolence while running on performance reviews, legal codes and centralized power, a pattern often summed up as "Confucian outside, Legalist inside." In the 1970s, the political campaign to "praise Legalism and criticize Confucius" recast Han Fei as a progressive thinker; that was political appropriation, not scholarship, and should be read as such.

Crossovers

  • Distributed systems · Byzantine fault tolerance (Lamport et al., 1982): the Byzantine Generals Problem assumes that some nodes in a network will lie or act maliciously, and asks for a protocol that still reaches agreement. It doesn't count on every node being honest; it guarantees correctness as long as bad nodes stay below a certain fraction — exactly "relying not on people doing good, but on their being unable to do wrong." The difference: Byzantine fault tolerance deliberately has no single trusted center, since any node may be the bad one; Han Fei puts all trust in one ruler — what an engineer would call a single point of failure with no redundancy.
  • Economics · mechanism design (Hurwicz, Maskin, Myerson, Nobel 2007): mechanism design asks how to set rules so that when everyone acts in their own interest, the outcome is what the designer wants ("incentive compatibility"). The two handles and the matching of names to results are incentive compatibility two millennia early. The difference: mechanism design usually aims at social welfare; Han Fei's objective function is the security and strength of one ruler.
  • Psychology · motivation crowding-out (Gneezy & Rustichini, 2000): when some Israeli daycare centers began fining parents for late pickups, lateness went up — the fine turned guilt toward the teachers into a service one could buy, and lateness stayed high even after the fine was dropped. It is the most precise experimental rebuttal of "convert everything into reward and punishment."
  • Evolutionary biology · parent–offspring conflict (Trivers, 1974): Robert Trivers showed that because parents and children share only half their genes, they "want" different levels of parental investment — there is a built-in conflict of interest even within the family. Han Fei's warning about queens and heirs uses the same cold eye; the difference is that Trivers describes an evolved tug-of-war, not conscious scheming.
  • Management & statistics · Goodhart's law (Goodhart, 1975): when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure, because people start optimizing the measure itself. Matching names to results is management by metrics. By punishing overachievement, Han Fei partly guards against gaming, but he never saw that the stricter the audit, the stronger the pressure to fake the numbers.

Ten Sentences

1. Don't rely on people doing good for you; rely on their being unable to do wrong.

2. People love profit and hate harm: the carriage-maker wants you rich and the coffin-maker wants you dead — not kindness versus cruelty, just where the money is.

3. The ancients gave away the throne not because they were nobler, but because the job was miserable; people now fight over a county post not because they are baser, but because the post is rich.

4. As the age differs, affairs differ, and preparations must change: governing today's people with the policies of the ancient kings is waiting by the stump for a rabbit.

5. Law must be public, uniform and certain — the plumb line does not bend for crooked wood; punishment does not spare ministers, reward does not pass over commoners.

6. Match names to results: promise big and deliver small, and you are punished; promise small and deliver big, and you are punished too — a mismatch does more harm than a great result does good.

7. Show what you like and your ministers fake abilities; show what you hate and they hide their motives: discard likes and dislikes, and they will show their true selves.

8. The dragon rides the cloud; when the cloud is gone it is an earthworm: power lives in the position, not the person — a tiger that hands its claws to the dog will be ruled by the dog.

9. It is not knowing that is hard, but knowing what to do with what you know — rulers too have a reversed scale, and even the right words can get you killed.

10. Han Fei built a machine that runs without good people, but left one component with no check at all — the ruler himself — and Qin's rise and fall both flowed from it.
