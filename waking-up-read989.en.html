Waking Up

Waking Up: A Guide to Spirituality Without Religion · Sam Harris · 2014

中文 →

In One Sentence

A neuroscientist famous for attacking religion argues that most religious doctrine is indefensible, but that religion has been sitting on one real discovery: by training attention, you can see through the feeling that you are a thinker inside your head, and loosen a great deal of suffering in the process. None of this requires believing anything; it only requires looking for yourself. The most direct guides to that looking, he says, are Tibetan Dzogchen and Indian Advaita.

Where It Sits

Harris holds a PhD in cognitive neuroscience from UCLA, but he is better known as one of the "Four Horsemen" of New Atheism: his 2004 book The End of Faith treated religious belief as a dangerous mistake. Waking Up is his answer to the obvious follow-up question, "So is anything in religion worth keeping?" It is a book about meditation and selflessness written in an atheist's vocabulary.

The Core Argument

  • Spirituality can be cleanly separated from religion. Experiences of self-transcendence are real, trainable and good for people. They prove nothing about gods, afterlives or scripture, and they do not need any of that to stand on.
  • The "I" that seems to sit behind your eyes, thinking your thoughts, is an illusion that can be seen through. Neuroscience finds no such center in the brain; meditation lets you discover, in the first person, that it is not there.
  • Consciousness itself is not an illusion; it is the one thing we cannot doubt. Harris dismantles the self but leaves consciousness standing, and admits that science cannot yet explain why it exists at all.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. Spirituality without religion: separating the experience from the doctrine

What it is. Harris knows the word "spirituality" comes with baggage: crystals, horoscopes, greeting-card wisdom. He uses it anyway, because nothing else names the experiences he means: moments when the sense of self fades, the felt gap between you and your experience disappears, and what remains is peace, openness and an unforced concern for others. Christian mystics, Sufis, Indian yogis and Buddhist monks have all described such states, under different names.

The mechanism: same experience, different labels. His reasoning is simple. If mutually incompatible religions all report similar experiences, those experiences cannot vouch for any one religion's doctrines. A Catholic nun who feels boundless love in prayer will call it Christ; a Hindu will call it Brahman; Harris calls it a state the human mind is capable of. The experience is real; the metaphysical label stuck on top of it is the optional part. That is the method of the whole book: keep whatever anyone can test for themselves, drop whatever must be taken on faith.

Why it matters. For a secular reader this resolves a dilemma: either swallow a religion whole to get at its wisdom, or reject it whole and throw the wisdom out with it. Harris wants to show there is a third option. He does not treat all traditions equally. He holds that Buddhism, along with the Advaita strand of Hinduism, describes the mind far more precisely than the Abrahamic faiths, because its core teachings function more like an operational method of introspection than a creed to be accepted (paraphrased). That judgment later became one of the most criticized things about the book.

How it changes your view. You no longer have to ask whether the religion behind a meditation practice is true. You can ask a plainer question: if I look the way this method says, do I see what it describes? You don't need to trust Galileo's character to point a telescope at Jupiter and see its moons.

2. Consciousness: the one thing that cannot be an illusion

What it is. The book contains a much-quoted line to the effect that consciousness is the one thing in this universe that cannot be an illusion (paraphrased). The colors you see may be illusory, your memories may be false, the outside world may be a dream; but that something is being experienced cannot be doubted, because doubting is itself an experience. This has the same root as Descartes' "I think, therefore I am," but Harris takes less from it: Descartes derived an "I," Harris keeps only "experience is happening."

The hard problem. He then admits a scientific impasse, what the philosopher David Chalmers calls the "hard problem" of consciousness. We can explain, step by step, how the brain processes visual signals and produces behavior (the "easy problems"), but not why any of that processing is accompanied by how it feels: why red looks red rather than being information flowing in the dark. He borrows Thomas Nagel's formulation from "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?": a creature is conscious if there is something it is like to be that creature.

Why it matters. This is the foundation of the book. If consciousness could be fully reduced to information processing, meditation would be just another brain-tuning technique. Harris insists instead that consciousness is the first-hand, unavoidable fact, and meditation is a way of studying it directly. That makes him impatient with overreach on both sides: with materialists who claim the problem is solved, and with spiritual seekers who leap from the mystery of consciousness to the immortality of the soul. He devotes pages to the neurosurgeon Eben Alexander's bestseller Proof of Heaven, arguing that its near-death account cannot support its conclusions, medically or logically.

How it changes your view. You can rest attention on a very simple, constantly overlooked fact: experience is appearing right now. Not experience of anything in particular, just appearing itself. Every later exercise in selflessness takes place on this ground, which cannot give way.

3. The self as illusion: no thinker behind the thoughts

What it is. The "self" Harris wants to dismantle is narrow and precise. It is not your personality, memory or body; it is the feeling of being a subject located in your head, behind your eyes, thinking these thoughts. Thoughts arise, he argues, but there is no additional thinker producing them; the sense of a thinker is just one more thought or sensation arising.

The neuroscience side: split brains. One of his main pieces of evidence is split-brain research. To treat severe epilepsy, some patients had their corpus callosum, the bundle of fibers connecting the two hemispheres, surgically cut. Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga found that the two hemispheres could then take in information and respond to it independently, each unaware of the other. In Gazzaniga's best-known case, a patient's right hemisphere was shown a snow scene and the left a chicken claw. His left hand (controlled by the right hemisphere) picked a shovel; his right hand picked a chicken. Asked why, the verbal left hemisphere, which knew only about the claw, fluently explained that the claw went with the chicken and the shovel was for cleaning out the chicken shed. Gazzaniga called this automatic story-making the left-brain "interpreter." What Harris takes from it: the unified self we feel does not correspond to anything unified in the brain; it can be divided with a scalpel, and the reasons we give ourselves are often invented after the fact. He also presses the question: if the right hemisphere has experiences of its own, which one is "you"?

The philosophy side: Parfit's teleporter. He also discusses a thought experiment from the philosopher Derek Parfit. A machine scans you, destroys you on Earth and rebuilds you exactly on Mars from new atoms. Is the person on Mars you? And if the machine malfunctions and the original on Earth survives, who is the one on Mars? Parfit concluded that personal identity is not a deep all-or-nothing fact; what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness. Harris sees this as converging with what meditation reveals.

Why it matters. For Harris, most suffering is organized around this "I." The thought "he looks down on me" stings because a seemingly real self catches it, owns it and chews on it. Identification with thought, being lost in thinking without knowing you are thinking, is the human predicament he names again and again (paraphrased).

How it changes your view. The next time you are angry, notice: the anger is there, related thoughts keep arriving, but when you look for the person who is angry, you find only more sensations and thoughts. Harris's point is that anger, unless it is fed by fresh thoughts, tends to dissipate within moments (paraphrased).

4. Looking for the looker: how meditation exposes the self

Step one: mindfulness. Harris recommends starting with vipassana-style mindfulness. Sit, rest attention on the breath; when a thought comes, notice it and return. Then gradually treat sounds, sensations, emotions and thoughts as objects appearing in consciousness, observed without judging or following. He spent years in vipassana retreats in the United States and Asia. The point of this stage is to discover that you spend most of your day lost in thought, and that a thought, the moment it is seen, loses most of its grip.

The problem: gradual practice can reinforce the illusion. Harris's central objection to the gradual approach is that if you treat meditation as a project to be accomplished through effort, the structure "a self who practices, striving toward a future awakening" strengthens the very self you are trying to see through (paraphrased). It is like reminding yourself every day not to think about someone you want to forget.

Direct pointing: Dzogchen and Advaita. The teachings he found most direct are two "nondual" traditions. One is Dzogchen, from the Nyingma school of Tibetan Buddhism, in which a teacher "points out" the nature of mind so the student recognizes on the spot that open, lucid awareness does not need to be produced; it is already present. Harris describes studying with the Dzogchen master Tulku Urgyen Rinpoche in Nepal, where this pointing-out first happened for him (his own account). The other is Advaita Vedanta, especially Ramana Maharshi's self-inquiry, "Who am I?": turn attention around and look for the one who is looking. He also sought out the Advaita teacher H.W.L. Poonja in Lucknow, India.

What "looking for the looker" actually is. This is the book's most practical move. Ordinarily your attention points outward, at this page. Now turn it around: look for the one who is looking. Where is he? Behind your eyes? Harris borrows the British writer Douglas Harding's description in On Having No Head: from the first-person point of view, you cannot see your own head at all; where you assume an "I" to be, there is only the world itself appearing, this page, this room, the surrounding sounds. Harris stresses that failing to find anything is not a failure. Not finding is the answer: the looker is not hidden too deep to see; it was never there, and only seeing is happening. The glimpse may last a second or two before thought rushes back and the sense of self reassembles; the practice is to recognize it again and again until it becomes more natural.

How it changes your view. Meditation stops being a relaxation technique for "quieting the mind" and becomes an investigation: a first-person test of a hypothesis about the self. What you are looking for is not a special state but an ordinary fact you keep overlooking.

5. Gurus: awakening is no guarantee of character

What it is. If deep insight requires a good teacher, an awkward problem follows: many spiritual teachers behave badly. Harris is blunt about this in his discussion of gurus. His most prominent example is Chögyam Trungpa, who brought Tibetan Buddhism to the West: he drank heavily in public and slept with many of his students, and his chosen successor, Ösel Tendzin, kept having sex with students while knowing he was HIV-positive, infecting at least one.

Harris's position. His conclusion has two layers. First, a person can be profound about the nature of mind and an ethical disaster at the same time: contemplative attainment is not an across-the-board upgrade of character, and awakening does not guarantee morality. Second, this does not mean everything such people teach is false; he says plainly that some teachers with bad reputations can still point people toward something real (paraphrased). So judge them separately: use a teacher's instructions and test them, but don't worship the teacher, and never hand over your judgment. He also warns that the guru-system argument that "your doubt about the teacher is just your own ego" is the perfect cover for abuse.

How it changes your view. For anyone looking for a teacher, this section is a risk checklist: can you verify the method yourself? Does the teacher ask you to give up common sense or property? Are there things around him nobody is allowed to talk about?

6. Psychedelics: proof that the mind can be utterly different, but not the path itself

His experience. The book opens with a psychedelic story. As a young man, Harris took MDMA with a close friend and, mid-conversation, suddenly realized he loved this friend: not with any romantic feeling, but by simply wanting him to be happy, with no trace of self-concern in the wish. That night, he says, convinced him for the first time that the mind can be in a state radically different from the ordinary (paraphrased). In the later discussion of psychedelics he is candid about the other side too: some experiences were terrifying, closer to hell than to bliss (paraphrased).

His position. He is cautious without being evasive. Psychedelics can prove within a few hours that consciousness can be otherwise, which is valuable for anyone who has never doubted the ordinary sense of self; but they cannot reliably take you there, and they carry real psychological risks. An analogy: a helicopter drops you on the summit for a look and then flies you back down. Having seen it, you believe the summit exists, but walking up yourself, and staying there, still takes practice. He thinks adults have the right to such experiments under careful conditions, but that the drugs should not be treated as a shortcut or an endpoint.

How it changes your view. Psychedelics play a proof-of-existence role in the book: the everyday sense of self is not the only shape a mind can take. That supplies a very personal kind of evidence for the earlier arguments, along with a reminder that no single experience is the same thing as lasting understanding.

The Skeleton of the Book

Compressed to one line: first establish the one certain thing (consciousness exists), then show that our intuition about who owns it is wrong (there is no central thinker), then offer a method anyone can test (look for the looker), stripping away religious superstition, guru authority and pharmacological shortcuts along the way.

Three kinds of evidence hold it up: philosophical argument (consciousness cannot be doubted; Parfit shows identity is not a deep fact); neuroscience (split brains and the interpreter show the unified self is constructed); and first-person experience (the author's years of retreat, Dzogchen pointing-out, psychedelic states). The third is the most important and the hardest to check. Harris's final argument is not "believe me" but "look for yourself." That gives the book the structure of a lab manual: the opening chapters set out the hypothesis and the theory, the last one lists the pitfalls.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: "The self is an illusion" means you don't exist and your personality is fake. No. Harris dismantles only the feeling of a thinker in the head. He does not deny that people have bodies, memories, characters and responsibilities. To call something an illusion is to say it is not what it seems, not that it is nothing.

Misreading 2: This is Buddhism for beginners. He explicitly declines rebirth and karma as articles of faith and asks for no conversion. He borrows the methods, not the religion.

Criticism 1: What kind of illusion? Phenomenologists such as Dan Zahavi argue that experience always carries a minimal first-person character, its "for-me-ness," which he calls the minimal self, and that this structure does not vanish even in meditation. The philosopher and cognitive scientist Evan Thompson, in Why I Am Not a Buddhist (2020), argues that the self is better understood as a process that is continually constructed, and "constructed" does not mean "illusory": a melody is made of notes, but nobody calls the melody a hallucination. On this line of criticism, Harris may be running together "there is no little man sitting in the brain" (which almost everyone accepts) and "the self does not exist in any sense" (a much stronger, more doubtful claim).

Criticism 2: Cherry-picking traditions. He calls most religion absurd, then takes the parts of Buddhism and Hinduism he likes, strips away rebirth, ritual, ethical precepts and community, and calls the remainder rational. Scholars of religion call this demythologized, meditation-and-psychology version "Buddhist modernism": a particular reading that took shape from the nineteenth and twentieth centuries onward, not the way these traditions have understood themselves. Critics also note that he gives little attention to contemplative practice in Christian mysticism, Sufism and elsewhere, which makes his verdict that Buddhism is superior look less than fair.

Criticism 3: His path is hard to replicate. Harris's insight rests on cumulative years of silent retreat and on studying with leading teachers in India and Nepal, all of which takes time, money and freedom. The book says anyone can verify this for themselves, but most readers will have ten minutes of sitting a day and a book's description. For most people, "look for yourself" still works in practice as "first take my word that something is there."

Criticism 4: Tension with his neuroscience framing. He uses brain research to dismantle the self, yet says consciousness itself cannot be dismantled and concedes the hard problem is unsolved. Critics ask: if neuroscience cannot explain consciousness, on what authority does it rule that the feeling of "I" is false while the meditative experience of no-self is true? Not finding the looker might be a particular state of attention rather than a revelation of a deeper fact. From the other side, philosophers in the line of Daniel Dennett go further, arguing that the "what it is like" layer of consciousness may itself be a misunderstood representation, so Harris's claim that consciousness cannot be an illusion also needs an argument. Squeezed from both directions, his position looks like a house half demolished.

Criticism 5: The limits of the evidence. There is real research on the benefits of meditation, but many studies are small, weakly controlled, and their effect sizes are often inflated in popular accounts. And claims like "nondual awareness reveals the true nature of mind" ultimately rest on practitioners' own reports, which third-person methods cannot fully check. Harris demands rigorous evidence from religion while being more lenient toward interpretations of meditative experience, an asymmetry critics often point out.

Harris Beyond This Book

Free Will (2012) is the companion argument. It holds that your thoughts and choices arise from causes you cannot see: you do not first choose your next thought and then think it. Harris argues this is not only a conclusion from physical causation but something you can observe directly in meditation: thoughts simply appear, with no author. So the "no thinker" of Waking Up and the "no author of the will" of Free Will are two sides of one observation.

The Moral Landscape (2010) explains why he takes consciousness so seriously. There he argues that moral questions are ultimately questions about the well-being of conscious creatures, so they have right and wrong answers in principle and science can help answer them. That explains the motive behind Waking Up: if well-being just is a state of consciousness, then studying and improving consciousness itself, including through meditation, is serious work. Without these two books, a reader might take Waking Up for an atheist's odd detour; it is in fact one piece of his whole program. His later meditation app, also called Waking Up, carries on the book's teaching; we leave it there.

Crossovers

  • Western philosophy · Hume's bundle: In the Treatise, Hume reports that whenever he looks inward for a "self" he only ever stumbles on some particular perception, heat or cold, love or hatred, never a self apart from them. The likeness: both look inward for a subject and come up empty. The difference: Hume ends in skeptical puzzlement, not in treating the empty result as liberation.
  • Psychology · William James, "the thoughts themselves are the thinkers": In The Principles of Psychology (1890), James describes consciousness as a stream and writes that the thoughts themselves are the thinkers. The likeness: both drop any extra subject behind thought. The difference: James keeps a warm, intimate sense of "mine" as the core of the self.
  • Neuroscience · the default mode network and psilocybin: The default mode network, active when the brain is at rest, is linked to mind-wandering and self-referential thinking. Robin Carhart-Harris and colleagues reported in 2012 that psilocybin reduces activity and connectivity in its key hubs; Judson Brewer and colleagues reported in 2011 that experienced meditators show lower activity there too. The likeness: both tie the sense of self to a measurable brain network. The difference: correlation is not causation, and a quieter network is not the same as seeing through the self.
  • Neuroscience · Anil Seth's "controlled hallucination": In Being You (2021), Seth argues that the brain is constantly predicting the body and the world, and that the self is one such predictive construction. The likeness: both treat the self as constructed. The difference: Seth does not see seeing through it as liberation, but as an essential function that keeps the organism alive.
  • Distributed systems · clusters without a master: Many distributed systems have no fixed central node; nodes coordinate through a protocol, yet the whole presents itself as one service. The likeness: a "unified I" may be the outward interface of many cooperating modules. The difference: a cluster's unity is guaranteed by an explicit protocol, while how the brain binds its modules into a single experience remains an open question.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. Most religious doctrine does not hold up, but the experience of self-transcendence that religions describe is real, and it can be trained without faith.

2. The same experience turns up in incompatible traditions, so it cannot vouch for any one tradition's metaphysics.

3. Consciousness is the one thing in the universe that cannot be an illusion (paraphrased); why there is consciousness at all, science cannot yet say.

4. Only one "I" is being dismantled: the subject that seems to sit behind your eyes, thinking your thoughts.

5. Split-brain research shows the unified self can be cut in two, and the reasons we give ourselves are often made up after the fact.

6. Most suffering comes from identifying with thought: being lost in thinking without knowing you are thinking.

7. Turn attention around and look for the one who is looking; not finding it is the answer.

8. Treating meditation as a project aimed at a future result can strengthen the very self you want to see through; Dzogchen and Advaita point straight at awareness already present.

9. Awakening does not guarantee character: test the teacher's method, don't worship the teacher; psychedelics can prove the mind can be different, but cannot walk the path for you.

10. In the end all of this still rests on first-person experience, and what kind of illusion the self is remains disputed: treat it as an experiment worth running yourself, not a new creed.
