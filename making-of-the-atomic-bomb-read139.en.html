The Making of the Atomic Bomb

The Making of the Atomic Bomb · Richard Rhodes · 1986

中文 →

In One Sentence

Twelve years separate a Hungarian physicist waiting for a London traffic light in 1933, struck by the idea of a chain reaction, from the flash over Hiroshima in August 1945; Rhodes spends nine hundred pages showing how that road was travelled, and his conclusion is this: the bomb was not the invention of a villain or a lone genius but something that, once a law of nature had been glimpsed, was all but bound to be built — and what it brought was not merely a bigger bomb but a new human condition, in which total war between great powers became suicide, and that very danger forced humanity to think seriously, for the first time, about the limits of national sovereignty.

Where It Sits

Rhodes is a writer who came up through journalism, not a physicist. The book won the 1988 Pulitzer Prize for General Nonfiction, along with the National Book Award and the National Book Critics Circle Award, and is still regarded as the definitive narrative history of the Manhattan Project. It became the first volume of a four-book series (the sequel, Dark Sun, covers the hydrogen bomb).

The Core Argument

  • The bomb was a fact of physics before it was a political decision. Within months of the discovery of fission in late 1938, physicists everywhere understood that a bomb was possible in principle. Knowledge cannot be recalled; the real question was never whether to know, but what to do once you knew.
  • Turning a principle into a weapon took not an inspiration but an industrial mobilisation. The true bottleneck was separating isotopes and producing plutonium — in three years the United States built plants on the scale of the automobile industry. The bomb is the archetype of science fused with the state, and the birth of "big science."
  • The weapon was at once the greatest danger and an opportunity — what Bohr called complementarity. The book's intellectual spine comes from Niels Bohr: the same thing that could destroy civilisation also made total war between great powers pointless; the only way to avoid an arms race was "openness" — sharing the secret with rivals and building international control before the bomb was ever used in war. Churchill and Roosevelt turned the proposal down in 1944, and Rhodes writes it as the fork in the road that history did not take.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. The chain reaction: one neutron in, two out, and the world changes

In September 1933 British newspapers reported a speech by Ernest Rutherford, the father of nuclear physics, in which he said that anyone looking for a source of power in the transformation of atoms was talking "moonshine." Leo Szilard, a Hungarian physicist in exile in London, was irritated by it, and the next day, waiting for a light to change on Southampton Row, it came to him: if you could find an element that, struck by one neutron, released two, and those two each struck another atom, the energy would multiply exponentially and run away. That is the nuclear chain reaction, and Rhodes opens the whole book at that traffic light. (Szilard had read H. G. Wells's 1914 novel The World Set Free, which coined the phrase "atomic bombs.")

First, the neutron. An atomic nucleus is made of positively charged protons and uncharged neutrons. James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932, and precisely because it carries no charge, it is not repelled by the nucleus and can slip straight in. It is the crowbar that pries the nucleus open.

The key number in a chain reaction is the multiplication factor, usually written k: of the neutrons released by each fission, on average how many go on to cause another one. If k is below 1, the reaction dies out; at exactly 1 it sustains itself steadily — that is a reactor; well above 1, every generation doubles — that is a bomb. Think of compound interest, or an epidemic's basic reproduction number: double each generation and after eighty generations you have 280, and a single generation takes about a hundredth of a microsecond. The whole thing is over in roughly a millionth of a second, which is why it is not burning but exploding.

Why "critical mass"? Because neutrons escape through the surface. In a small lump of uranium, neutrons fly out before they hit another nucleus, and k stays below 1. The bigger the lump, the smaller its surface relative to its volume, the fewer neutrons escape, until at some size k crosses 1. That size is the critical mass. All the engineering of a bomb comes down to this: how to turn two pieces that are "not enough" into one piece that is "enough" in an instant, and split as many nuclei as possible before the explosion blows the material apart.

How it changes the way you see: whenever the output of one step becomes the input of the next, with a factor above 1, change stops being linear — rumours, bank runs and viruses all work this way.

2. Fission: a discovery that "shouldn't have happened"

From 1934 Enrico Fermi in Rome bombarded element after element with neutrons, uranium included, and believed he had made new elements heavier than uranium. The chemist Ida Noddack suggested in print that the nucleus might instead have broken into large fragments, but almost nobody took her seriously: physical intuition said a neutron could chip a crumb off a nucleus, never cleave it.

In December 1938 the Berlin chemists Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann found barium in bombarded uranium — an element roughly half uranium's weight. Hahn wrote to describe the puzzling result to his longtime collaborator Lise Meitner, a physicist of Jewish descent who had fled to Sweden. That Christmas, walking in the snow with her visiting nephew Otto Frisch, Meitner worked it out using Bohr's "liquid-drop model" (picture the nucleus as a charged droplet: surface tension holds it together while the repulsion of its charges pushes it apart). Uranium's nucleus is so big that the repulsion nearly cancels the surface tension; add one neutron and it stretches like a water drop and pinches in two. With Einstein's E=mc² (a tiny mass corresponds to an enormous energy) they found the two fragments weighed slightly less than the original, which matched a release of about 200 million electron volts per split — tens of millions of times more than a chemical reaction. Frisch borrowed the biologists' word for cell division and called it "fission."

In January 1939 Bohr brought the news to America, and within weeks laboratories everywhere had reproduced it. Bohr and John Wheeler then showed theoretically that the readily fissionable form was uranium-235, which makes up only 0.7% of natural uranium, not the 99-plus percent that is uranium-238. That single fact would set the shape of the entire Manhattan Project.

Why it matters: fission was a natural fact the whole world touched at once — published in open journals, its meaning grasped almost simultaneously by physicists in many countries. It is the foundation of the book's argument for inevitability: nobody could decide not to discover it.

How it changes the way you see: big discoveries are often not flashes of insight in places nobody looked, but cases where everyone saw the data and was stopped by a default intuition. The four years between Fermi and Noddack's suggestion and Hahn's barium were the years that intuition — "of course the nucleus can't be split" — held the door shut. You start looking for the "obviously impossible" in your own field.

3. From principle to factory: isotope separation and "big science"

Knowing U-235 could make a bomb left an apparently impossible engineering wall: U-235 and U-238 are chemically identical, no chemical method can tell them apart, and the only difference is that the former is about 1.3% lighter. You had to exploit that sliver of mass to sift tens of kilograms of U-235, atom by atom, out of tons of natural uranium.

In March 1940 two émigré physicists in Birmingham, Frisch and Rudolf Peierls, wrote a memo of a few pages that first estimated the critical mass of pure U-235 not at several tons, as had been assumed, but at something on the order of a kilogram (an underestimate — the real figure is much larger — but enough to move the bomb from "too heavy for an aircraft" to "deliverable"). The Frisch–Peierls memorandum led to Britain's MAUD Committee, whose 1941 report helped push America to commit. (Back in August 1939 Szilard had persuaded Einstein to write to Roosevelt, but that letter produced only a modestly funded uranium committee.)

In September 1942 General Leslie Groves took over the effort, run under the name of the Manhattan Engineer District, and Rhodes tells what followed as an industrial epic:

  • Oak Ridge, Tennessee: several separation routes at once. One was Ernest Lawrence's electromagnetic separator, the calutron, in which uranium ions curve through a magnetic field and the lighter ones curve slightly tighter, landing in a different collector; with copper scarce in wartime, the project borrowed some 14,700 tons of silver from the U.S. Treasury to wind the magnets. Another was gaseous diffusion: turn uranium into uranium hexafluoride gas and push it through thousands of porous barriers, the lighter molecules passing slightly faster, each stage enriching only a hair, so thousands of stages in series. The K-25 diffusion plant was among the largest buildings in the world.
  • Hanford, Washington: an entirely different road — skip U-235 and instead let U-238 absorb neutrons in a reactor and become a new element, plutonium, which, being a different element, can be separated chemically. This rested on Fermi's Chicago Pile-1, a stack of graphite and uranium under the stands of the University of Chicago's football field, which on 2 December 1942 achieved the first self-sustaining chain reaction (k just above 1). Arthur Compton phoned the news in code: "The Italian navigator has landed in the new world."
  • Los Alamos, New Mexico: the design laboratory under J. Robert Oppenheimer, turning material into a device that would actually explode.

At its peak the project employed well over a hundred thousand people and cost about two billion dollars of the day, and most of those people had no idea what they were building. Rhodes's judgement: the bomb was an industrial achievement first and a scientific one second — physicists knew the answer in 1939; it was the engineering that took six years and two billion dollars.

That also explains why Germany did not build one. In Rhodes's account it was not short of talent (Heisenberg and others were there) but of resources and priority, and it made a crucial technical mistake: in early 1941 the physicist Walther Bothe measured how strongly graphite absorbs neutrons, very likely with a contaminated sample, and concluded graphite could not serve as a moderator (the material that slows fast fission neutrons so U-235 captures them more easily); Germany turned to scarce heavy water instead. And Nazi racial laws had already driven Meitner, Szilard, Fermi (whose wife was Jewish), Bethe, Teller, Wigner, von Neumann and many others into the arms of its enemies. Hitler's greatest "contribution" was to hand the bomb's builders to America.

How it changes the way you see: in technology races the side that understands the principle first does not necessarily win; the winner is the side that can press the principle into supply chains, plants and organisations — true of chips, vaccines and AI compute alike.

4. Gun and implosion: plutonium's surprise, and the hardest bomb

Los Alamos began with a simple idea: fire one sub-critical piece of U-235, like a bullet down a gun barrel, into another, and together they go supercritical. This is the gun-type design. It was considered so reliable that it was never fully tested — Little Boy, dropped on Hiroshima, was of this kind and had never been detonated before.

The trouble was plutonium. In 1944 Emilio Segrè and colleagues found that reactor-made plutonium contained plutonium-240, which undergoes spontaneous fission (it splits on its own, without an incoming neutron, steadily giving off stray neutrons). That meant a gun was far too slow to bring two pieces of plutonium together: before they fully met, stray neutrons would start the chain reaction early and the bomb would blow itself apart having released only a fraction of its energy — a "fizzle." The plutonium Hanford had been built at vast cost to produce looked as if it could not make a bomb at all.

The way out was implosion, proposed by the physicist Seth Neddermeyer and at first given little chance: wrap a sub-critical sphere of plutonium in a shell of explosives, detonate it from all sides at once, and squeeze the sphere inward, sharply raising its density — higher density, fewer escaping neutrons, so the same plutonium becomes supercritical, and in microseconds, too fast for spontaneous fission to interfere. The difficulty was simultaneity and symmetry: the slightest asymmetry in the shock wave would squirt the plutonium out sideways. John von Neumann helped with the calculations, and the chemist George Kistiakowsky designed "explosive lenses" — shaped combinations of fast- and slow-burning explosives that, as an optical lens focuses light, reshaped dozens of separate detonations into a single, perfectly converging spherical wave. A bomb this complex had to be tested: before dawn on 16 July 1945, in the New Mexico desert, the Trinity test yielded the equivalent of something over twenty thousand tons of TNT. Fat Man, dropped on Nagasaki, was of this kind.

The two designs split over a physical accident — plutonium-240's spontaneous fission. Nearly every nuclear weapon since has followed the implosion path.

The seduction of the technical problem is part of this history too. At his 1954 security hearing Oppenheimer recalled that when you see something that is "technically sweet," you go ahead and do it and argue about what to do with it only after you have had your technical success — and that was how it had been with the atomic bomb. The difficulty and beauty of the work is itself a force that keeps people from asking why. After Germany's surrender almost nobody left Los Alamos; the best-known exception was Joseph Rotblat, who resigned at the end of 1944 on learning that Germany's bomb effort had come to nothing, and who received the Nobel Peace Prize fifty years later.

How it changes the way you see: when a problem is beautiful enough, "can it be solved?" quietly replaces "should it be?" Look at the project you are most absorbed in — how much of that absorption comes from the purpose, and how much from the sweetness of the problem itself?

5. How the line between civilian and combatant was erased, step by step

Rhodes does not present Hiroshima as a decision that fell from the sky. He traces at length the history of strategic bombing (attacking not armies at the front but an enemy's cities, factories and population, to destroy its capacity and will to fight): Guernica in 1937, the German Blitz on London, the 1943 Allied raids on Hamburg that raised a firestorm (fires so widespread that the rising hot air sucks in wind from all sides, forming a whirlwind of flame strong enough to pull people in), Dresden in 1945, and the American incendiary raid on Tokyo on the night of 9–10 March 1945, which killed an estimated eighty to a hundred thousand or more people in one night — more than died immediately at Hiroshima.

His argument: by the time the bomb was ready, the moral threshold of killing enemy civilians en masse had long been crossed. The bomb did not invent a new moral crime; it compressed an already routine practice into one plane and one bomb. That is why "whether to use it" was barely treated as a real question at the top; the discussion was mostly about where and how. The committee choosing targets deliberately picked cities not yet damaged by conventional bombing, so the new weapon's effects would be "clearly visible."

Some did try to brake. In June 1945 a group of scientists at Chicago's Metallurgical Laboratory wrote the Franck Report (named for the Nobel laureate James Franck), urging a public demonstration on an uninhabited area before the world, Japan included, rather than unannounced use on a city, and warning that uncontrolled use would trigger an arms race with the Soviet Union; Szilard also circulated a petition. These were shelved, or never reached the President. On 6 August Little Boy exploded over Hiroshima; on 9 August Fat Man fell on Nagasaki; in the same days the Soviet Union declared war on Japan. By the end of 1945 the combined death toll of the two cities is estimated at somewhere between well over a hundred thousand and around two hundred thousand. Rhodes closes with extensive survivor testimony, turning the white flash back into thousands of particular people.

How it changes the way you see: major transgressions are rarely made in one step. They are usually a chain of small moves, each taking the last as normal — by the final step it no longer looks like crossing a line, just the obvious next thing. The real question is not only "is this step right?" but "how did we get to where this step feels obvious?"

6. Bohr's complementarity and the "open world"

The intellectual hero Rhodes gives the book is not Oppenheimer but the Danish physicist Niels Bohr. In quantum mechanics Bohr had proposed the principle of complementarity: an electron can behave as a particle or as a wave; the two descriptions exclude each other and never appear together, yet both are needed for the full picture. After escaping occupied Denmark to Britain in 1943, and then visiting Los Alamos under an alias, Bohr soon applied the idea to the bomb: the weapon was the greatest threat civilisation had faced and, at the same time, an unprecedented opportunity — precisely because it was so terrible, total war between great powers would lose its point, and humanity would be forced to find new arrangements for security. (paraphrased)

From this came a concrete policy: since the science was public and the Soviets would build the bomb sooner or later, trying to keep a monopoly through secrecy would only buy an arms race; the one way out was to tell the Soviet Union of the bomb's existence before it was used in war and invite it to build international control together — in exchange for an "open world" in which nations opened themselves to verification. In May 1944 he met Churchill, and the meeting went badly; Churchill barely listened. In August he saw Roosevelt, who was cordial. But in September the two leaders signed an aide-memoire at Hyde Park rejecting any disclosure and calling for inquiries into Bohr to make sure he leaked nothing. Rhodes writes this as the book's road not taken.

At the end of the book Rhodes takes up Bohr's thread himself and makes a larger claim: nuclear weapons, for the first time, set a physical ceiling on national sovereignty — no state, however powerful, can protect its people in an all-out nuclear war, so two old ideas, unlimited national sovereignty and winnable total war, were ended together by a single physical fact. That is complementarity in politics: the greatest danger and the greatest opportunity are two faces of one thing.

How it changes the way you see: faced with a powerful technology that cannot be un-invented, secrecy and monopoly tend to buy time, not safety; the real choice is to build a framework of control before the monopoly ends. The idea keeps returning in debates about biotechnology and AI — people are arguing, almost word for word, over Bohr's memorandum.

The Spine of the Book

The book makes a three-part argument. First: knowledge is irreversible. From Rutherford's nucleus, Chadwick's neutron and Fermi's experiments to Hahn's barium and Meitner's walk in the snow, Rhodes shows fission as a fact touched simultaneously by physicists the world over in open journals; once touched, the bomb existed in principle, and who built it first was a matter of time and resources.

Second: turning principle into weapon was a merger of state and industry. Frisch and Peierls brought the critical mass down from tons to kilograms and the bomb went from fantasy to project; Groves turned it into three cities — Oak Ridge, Hanford, Los Alamos; plutonium-240's surprise forced implosion, and Trinity proved it. This part also explains why it was America rather than Germany: resources, priority, and the talent totalitarian politics had driven out.

Third: the decision to use it was taken on a line that had already been erased, and its consequences outran the imagination of those who took it. Strategic bombing had made the killing of civilians routine; the atomic bomb merely compressed it into one weapon. Bohr saw that it would end total war and also start an arms race, proposed openness, and was refused. The book's conclusion is a stark, almost paradoxical hope: humanity built something capable of destroying itself, and that very possibility made war between great powers unthinkable for the first time. Rhodes's evidence is an immense body of primary material — memoirs, interviews, minutes, experimental data — carried by a narrative that explains the physics until you actually understand it and never lets you forget the cost.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading one: "Einstein invented the atomic bomb." E=mc² explains why fission releases so much energy, but not how to split a nucleus or sustain a chain reaction. Einstein's role was to sign a letter Szilard drafted; he did not work on the Manhattan Project and was never cleared to.

Misreading two: "The bomb was Oppenheimer's solitary genius." Oppenheimer was a superb organiser and scientific leader, but Rhodes describes a project carried out by thousands of scientists, well over a hundred thousand workers and an entire industrial system; the real chokepoints were isotope separation and plutonium production — the domain of Lawrence, Fermi, DuPont's engineers and Groves.

Misreading three: "Rhodes defends the bombing" or "Rhodes condemns it." Both are too simple. He details the chain of decisions, the dissent and the survivors' testimony, and keeps his verdict restrained; his real position lies in the larger question — not whether those two bombs should have been dropped, but how a world with nuclear weapons should be organised.

The strongest criticisms and disputes:

  • Whether the bombing was necessary remains the core historiographical fight. "Revisionists," led by Gar Alperovitz's Atomic Diplomacy, argue Japan was close to surrender and the bombs were largely a show of strength aimed at the postwar Soviet Union; "orthodox" historians such as Richard Frank in Downfall argue from intercepted Japanese cables that the military had no intention of surrendering and that an invasion would have cost far more lives. Tsuyoshi Hasegawa's Racing the Enemy (2005) adds that the decisive shock may have been the Soviet declaration of war on 8 August rather than the bombs. Rhodes's book predates the peak of this research; read alone, it can lead you to underrate the dispute.
  • The danger of inevitability. Writing the bomb as the necessary product of natural law can obscure the specific decisions that could have gone otherwise — whether to demonstrate first, whether to tell the Soviets, whether to continue after Germany fell. Some critics argue such a narrative effectively lightens the decision-makers' responsibility. Rhodes does show those forks, but the book's sense of fate remains strong.
  • The "nuclear peace" argument is hard to falsify. Rhodes leans toward the view that nuclear weapons ended total war between great powers; but "no world war because of the bomb" cannot be tested against a control, and near-misses such as the Cuban missile crisis or the 1983 Soviet early-warning false alarm (the duty officer, Stanislav Petrov, judged it an error and did not pass it up the chain) suggest that "peace" may also have been a run of luck.
  • Reading the German program. Whether Heisenberg deliberately slowed German research was hotly contested (Thomas Powers's 1993 Heisenberg's War argued he quietly resisted); Rhodes leans toward "Germany failed through error and lack of resources." The Farm Hall transcripts released in 1992 — secret recordings of interned German physicists hearing the news of Hiroshima — on balance support the latter view, though the argument has not entirely ended.
  • A partial vantage point. The book centres on Western physicists; Japanese decision-making, the Korean labourers killed in both cities, and the indigenous communities near uranium mines and test sites receive relatively little space.

Crossovers

  • Neuroscience · Neuronal avalanches: in 2003 Beggs and Plenz found that activity in cortical slices spreads in "avalanches" of all sizes, each firing neuron triggering on average about one downstream — a branching ratio near 1, exactly a reactor's "critical" state. Unlike a bomb, the brain uses inhibitory neurons to hold itself near that point, where signals travel farthest without running away.
  • Complexity science · Self-organised criticality: Per Bak's sandpile showed that many systems evolve on their own toward a critical point, where one grain may do nothing or trigger a landslide. The chain reaction shows criticality as a threshold; Bak adds that many real systems walk to the edge by themselves, with no one pushing a button.
  • Distributed systems · Retry storms: when a service slows, every client times out and retries, one failed request becomes two or three, the extra load slows the service further, and retries multiply — a chain reaction with k above 1, known as cascading failure. The fixes are nuclear engineering in spirit: exponential backoff and circuit breakers act as control rods, pushing the multiplication factor back below 1.
  • Evolutionary biology · Arms races: Dawkins and Krebs (1979) described arms races between species — faster cheetahs, faster gazelles, both paying more and neither safer, the "Red Queen" dynamic. It is the same structure Bohr feared; the difference is that humans can foresee a race and sit down to negotiate, and natural selection cannot.
  • Social psychology · Moral disengagement: Albert Bandura showed that people do harm without self-reproach by fragmenting the act, diffusing responsibility and swapping moral language for technical language. A project in which over a hundred thousand people each knew only their small piece scales this up to an institution; unlike that, the core scientists knew what they were building, and what shielded them was the logic of beating Germany.

Ten Sentences to Keep

① The atomic bomb began at a traffic light: Szilard saw that if one neutron yields two, energy runs away exponentially.

② A multiplication factor below 1 dies out, at 1 makes a reactor, above 1 makes a bomb — one physics, three fates.

③ Fission went unrecognised for four years because everyone believed the nucleus "of course" could not be split.

④ Published knowledge cannot be recalled: within months of fission, physicists everywhere could see the bomb.

⑤ Physicists knew the answer in 1939; the engineering took six years and two billion dollars — the bomb was an industrial achievement first.

⑥ Hitler's racial laws handed the bomb's builders to his enemies.

⑦ A surprise in plutonium-240 forced implosion; a "technically sweet" problem makes people do it first and ask why later.

⑧ Strategic bombing had already crossed the threshold of killing civilians; the bomb only compressed it into one weapon.

⑨ Bohr's complementarity: the greatest danger and the greatest opportunity are one thing, and the way out was to choose openness before the monopoly ended.
