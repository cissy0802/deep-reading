The Masnavi

Masnavi-ye Ma'navi · Jalāl al-Dīn Rūmī (1207–1273) · Persian, dictated c. 1258–1273, six books

中文 →

In One Sentence

You are a reed flute cut from the reed bed, and every ache, craving and restlessness you feel is homesickness for where you came from; the way back doesn't run outward but through taking apart the self you're so sure of — letting love cook the raw you, so that where "you" die, the real you comes alive.

Where It Sits

Rumi was the great thirteenth-century Persian poet of Sufism (the mystical, inward-turning tradition within Islam). He began as a respectable jurist and preacher in Konya, in today's Turkey, until at thirty-seven he met a wandering dervish, Shams of Tabriz, and was turned inside out. The Masnavi is the work of his last fifteen-odd years: six books and some twenty-five thousand couplets, dictated to his disciple Ḥusām al-Dīn Chelebi. In the Persian-speaking world it is honored as "the Qur'an in Persian" (a line traditionally credited to the fifteenth-century poet Jami), and it is the single most important didactic poem of Islamic mysticism.

The Core Argument

  • The basic human condition is separation. The soul comes from God and descends into a world of forms, appetites and bodies, and so it carries a lack it can't name. We think we want money, a lover, security; underneath, it is the same homesickness in different costumes.
  • What blocks the way home isn't the world — it's the self (nafs). The grasping, vain, comparing, endlessly self-justifying lower self is the real wall. Outward discipline, learning, even piety can all be co-opted by it.
  • What gets you through that wall is not reasoning but love (ʿishq); the method is to "die before you die." The small self has to be burned, cooked, dissolved (fanāʾ) so that a person can stand up again inside a larger life (baqāʾ).

Strung together: every longing you have points home; the thing in your way is you; and only love can take you apart.

A word on form. This is not a treatise but stories nested inside stories — the king and the handmaiden, the merchant and the parrot, the lion and the hare, Moses and the shepherd, the elephant in the dark — and every few lines Rumi breaks off to gloss a verse of scripture, argue a point, start another tale, and wander back, sometimes simply calling a halt: "this has no end." In the Arabic prose preface to Book One he calls the work "the roots of the roots of the roots of religion." The key to the book is understanding why it has to be told in this roundabout way. The five ideas below are the answer.

The Key Ideas, One by One

1. The reed and separation: homesickness as a sense of direction

What it is. The opening eighteen couplets, known as the "Song of the Reed" (Ney-nāma), are the overture to the whole work. Roughly:

Listen to the reed, how it tells a tale, complaining of separations — ever since I was cut from the reed bed, men and women have wept at my cry… Whoever is left far from his source keeps seeking the time when he was one with it.

The ney is the signature instrument of Sufi ritual, still at the center of the ceremonies of the Mevlevi order (formally organized by Rumi's son Sultan Walad and famous for its whirling dervishes). In this one instrument Rumi saw the whole human situation: it once grew in the reed bed, joined to the whole; only after being cut, hollowed out and bored with holes did it find a voice. Its music is the pain of parting.

Three layers to the image. First, "cut" means the soul leaving its source to be born, which is why we carry a gap that no particular thing can fill. Second, "hollowed" means a flute must be empty inside to sing; stuff it full of self and all it can make is its own noise. Third, the player: the music isn't the flute's but the breath blown through it. Rumi adds at once that "this cry of the reed is fire, not wind" — the breath moving through it is the fire of love.

Why it matters. The move flips the meaning of our most common bad feelings — emptiness, dissatisfaction, nostalgia, a sadness with no object. Ordinarily they're malfunctions to be fixed. For Rumi they are a compass: that you hurt at all proves you remember somewhere more whole than here. Homesickness isn't the illness; it's the sense of direction. The person with no sense of lack at all is the one truly lost — he has mistaken the flute for the reed bed.

How it changes the way you see. Next time you get what you wanted — the promotion, the relationship, the thing — and a few days later the familiar hollowness is back, try a different question: not "what's the next thing I'm missing?" but "what was I actually looking for in all these things?" Rumi's answer is reunion; the particular things are stand-ins. It's no accident that the very first story in the book is about mistaking the stand-in for the real thing. A king falls for a slave girl; she falls ill; his doctors are helpless. A mysterious physician sees that she is lovesick for a goldsmith in Samarkand. The goldsmith is fetched, then slowly poisoned; as his looks waste away, so does her love. Rumi's verdict, roughly: loves that are for the sake of color and looks are not love; in the end they are only disgrace. (We'll come back to the story's violent ending among the criticisms.)

2. Form and meaning: the story is the measure, the meaning is the grain

What it is. The title means, literally, "rhyming couplets of meaning." A masnavi is a Persian verse form in which each couplet rhymes internally and the rhyme changes from couplet to couplet, which makes it ideal for long narrative; ma'navi means "of meaning, spiritual." The most basic pair of categories in Rumi's thought is form (ṣūrat) and meaning (maʿnā): form is the shell you can see, name and classify; meaning is what actually does the work behind it. Nearly all the world's quarrels, in his view, come from people who recognize only form and are blind to meaning.

Signature example: the elephant in the dark (Book Three). Someone brings an elephant to show in a pitch-black house. Nobody can see it, so people feel their way. The one who touches the trunk says it's like a water pipe; the one on the ear, a fan; the one on a leg, a pillar; the one on the back, a throne. Rumi's conclusion, roughly: if each of them had held a candle, the difference would have gone out of their words. Note how his version differs from the familiar "blind men and the elephant": the trouble isn't that people are blind but that the room is dark — what's missing is light, not hands. Everyone touches something real; the error is taking a local form for the whole meaning.

Signature example: Moses and the shepherd (Book Two). Moses overhears a shepherd praying: "God, where are you? I want to stitch your shoes, comb your hair, pick your lice, bring you milk, kiss your little hand…" Moses is outraged — who do you think you're talking to? God has no shoes and no hair! The shepherd, mortified, tears his clothes and runs off into the desert. Then revelation comes to Moses, rebuking him: "You were sent to join, not to sever." And, roughly: I have given each person his own way of acting and his own way of speaking; I look not at the tongue and the words, I look at the heart and the state. The shepherd's words were crude, even blasphemous, in form and pure love in meaning; Moses's theology was flawless in form and wounding in meaning.

Signature example: the grocer's parrot (Book One). A talking parrot knocks over a bottle of oil; the grocer whacks it on the head so hard it goes bald, and it stops speaking. Days later a bald dervish walks by and the parrot suddenly squawks: "How did you end up bald? Did you spill the oil too?" Everyone laughs. Rumi uses it to attack the abuse of analogy (qiyās): people measure saints by their own experience and infer sameness of essence from sameness of surface — look alike, therefore must be alike, the root of almost every misjudgment.

Why the whole book is told in stories. Rumi answers this himself (Book Two, roughly): brother, a story is like a measuring cup; its meaning is the grain inside. The wise man takes the grain and doesn't stare at the measure. A truth stated outright gets filed by the listener as one more form — stored, debated, defended. A story makes you crack the husk yourself. That's why tales so often break off mid-plot and jump elsewhere: Rumi is deliberately keeping you from getting absorbed in the measure.

How it changes the way you see. In any argument, ask first: are we fighting over form or over meaning? Many quarrels — religious, political, or in a team meeting — are the trunk-holder and the leg-holder going at it: both right, neither whole. Rumi's fix isn't the relativist "everyone's right in their own way." It's light a candle: raise the level at which you're looking until the whole elephant comes into view.

3. The self (nafs): the frozen dragon

What it is. In Arabic nafs originally means "self, breath, soul," but in Sufi usage it names the lower self: greed, vanity, anger, envy, self-pity, the need to be seen — the sum of every impulse that has "me" at the center. It isn't a bad habit; it's a system that protects itself, disguises itself and learns.

Signature image: the frozen dragon (Book Three). A snake-catcher in the snowy mountains finds a huge dragon lying motionless, frozen stiff. Taking it for dead, he proudly hauls it to Baghdad to put on show for money. The crowd swells, the sun climbs, the dragon thaws, wakes — and swallows the snake-catcher. Rumi spells it out, roughly: your self is a dragon; it is not dead, only frozen for lack of means. You think you aren't greedy because you've had no chance to be; you think you'd never abuse power because you've never had any. Bring it to "Baghdad" — money, power, fame, an audience — and it wakes up.

Signature image: the lion in the well (Book One). A lion in the forest takes one animal a day, and the animals negotiate: we'll send you one in turn if you stop hunting. First comes a long debate — the animals argue for trust in God (tawakkul) and against striving; the lion argues for effort. Through the lion, Rumi quotes a saying of the Prophet: "Trust in God, and tie your camel's knee" — reliance and effort don't conflict. When a small hare's turn comes, it arrives late on purpose and tells the lion another lion waylaid it on the road. Furious, the lion has the hare lead him there, and the hare brings him to a deep well. The lion looks down, sees a glaring lion below, and leaps in to fight it — his own reflection. Rumi, roughly: the injustice and arrogance you see in others is often your own nature reflected in them.

The lesser and the greater holy war. In the same book Rumi cites a widely circulated saying (attributed to the Prophet; its authenticity is disputed): "We have returned from the lesser jihad to the greater jihad." The lesser is against the outward enemy, the greater against the self. Then, roughly: a lion who breaks the enemy's ranks is a small matter; the true lion is the one who breaks himself.

Why it matters. It makes the difficulty of the inner life precise. The self's most dangerous trait isn't its wickedness but that it can turn anything into food for itself — humility ("look how humble I am"), knowledge ("I know more than you"), even spiritual practice ("my practice goes deeper than yours"). That's why Rumi is often harsher on the self-satisfied pious and on scholars than on sinners.

How it changes the way you see. When you say "I'm not that kind of person," Rumi asks: or have you just not been carried into the sun yet? It's a test of honesty with yourself, and a kind of generosity toward others: whatever you find most intolerable in someone else is worth checking for in your own well first.

4. Love and the wooden leg: why reasoning can't reach the end

What it is. Rumi distinguishes two kinds of intellect. Partial intellect (ʿaql-i juzʾī) is the calculating, arguing, planning cleverness that works for the self. Universal intellect (ʿaql-i kull) is a clarity attuned to divine wisdom, one that sees the truth directly. The first is useful but doesn't go far; what carries you from the first toward the second is love (ʿishq).

Two signature lines. One (Book One, roughly): "The leg of the reasoners is made of wood, and a wooden leg is very unsteady." Argument can help you along to a certain point, but it's a prosthesis, no good on the path along the cliff edge. The other (Book One): love is the astrolabe of God's mysteries. An astrolabe was the medieval astronomer's instrument for measuring the height of stars and working out time and position. Rumi's point: love isn't a mood but an instrument of measurement, letting you read heights the naked eye can't. He continues, roughly: whether love comes from this side or from that, in the end it leads us yonder. Love for a person, love for a craft — even "worldly" love — can be a bridge to a higher one.

This isn't anti-intellectualism. Rumi was trained in law, Qur'anic commentary and theology, and the Masnavi is thick with citation. What he opposes isn't reason but treating reason as the destination. Think of reason as a flashlight: it lights the path at your feet, but it can't tell you where to go or why — direction comes from the pull toward home. A flashlight without direction walks in circles; a pull without a flashlight falls in a ditch. Rumi wants both, with the order of priority clear.

"Seek thirst, not water." Book Three has a very short line, roughly: don't go looking for water — get thirst, and water will gush up from above and below. It's another way of saying love: what decides what you'll receive isn't how many answers (water) you've collected but how deep your longing (thirst) runs. To someone who isn't thirsty, a spring is just scenery.

How it changes the way you see. When you circle a big question — which way to take your life, how to understand another person, what to believe — arguing it over and over and getting nowhere, Rumi suggests the argument may already have carried you as far as it can; the rest of the way depends on your willingness to love, commit and be changed. Many things you don't figure out first and then love; you love first, and then you see.

5. Die before you die: the parrot, the chickpea and the iron in the fire

What it is. Two core terms of Sufi practice: annihilation (fanāʾ), the dissolving of the small self, like a drop falling into the sea and ceasing to exist as "a drop"; and subsistence (baqāʾ), living and acting again, after that dissolving, in a larger way. Sufism has a famous maxim, "die before you die" (mūtū qabla an tamūtū), often presented as a saying of the Prophet though scholars mostly consider its chain unreliable. The Masnavi brings it to life through a run of stories.

The merchant and the parrot (Book One). A merchant keeps a parrot in a cage. Setting off to trade in India, he asks the parrot for any message, and it asks him to tell the wild parrots of the Indian forest: you live free — have you forgotten me in my cage? In India the merchant passes it on, and one wild parrot trembles and drops dead. Back home he dutifully reports this, and the caged parrot trembles and drops "dead" too. Grieving, the merchant tosses it out of the cage — and it flies straight up into a tree and tells him: that Indian parrot was teaching me by example; it was my voice and my beauty that put me in the cage, and only by "dying" could I get out. The "death" here is letting go of the performing self that keeps you trapped.

The chickpea in the pot (Book Three). A chickpea in boiling water keeps leaping up, trying to escape the pot. The cook knocks it back down with a ladle and tells it, roughly: boil nicely, and don't run from the one who made the fire; I'm not boiling you because I hate you, but so you'll gain flavor, become nourishment and join a human life. Suffering is reimagined as cooking: a raw chickpea is hard, bitter and closed in on itself; only cooked can it be absorbed and become part of a larger life. A line widely attributed to Rumi, though not found in the Masnavi, sums it up: "I was raw, then I was cooked, then I was burned."

Iron in the fire (Book Two). Put a piece of iron in the fire until it glows red; its color vanishes into the fire's, and it boasts, "I am the fire." Rumi uses the image to explain the tenth-century Sufi al-Ḥallāj, executed after crying "I am the Truth" (anā al-ḥaqq): the iron hasn't become fire, but every quality it has right now comes from the fire. Annihilation isn't being destroyed; it's being filled.

The ladder of the soul (Book Three). The most quoted passage in the book, roughly:

I died as…and became…
mineralplant
plantanimal
animalhuman
humanangel (to soar with the angels)
angel"that which enters no imagination" — "to Him we shall return"

"When was I ever less by dying?" Each death is a step up; the ladder rewrites death from an ending into a door. (Arranged from the sense of the poem, not a literal translation.)

Why it matters. This cluster of images answers the book's most practical question: how do you get home? Not by gaining, but by losing — losing the hard shells of "I matter," "I must be recognized," "I must be right." It sounds passive; it's the opposite. Each time you die to an identity or an attachment, you become something larger.

How it changes the way you see. It gives you a new question to ask of pain: is this pot boiling me to mush, or cooking me? A lost job, a breakup, a failure — if all you want, like the chickpea, is to leap out, the pain is just pain; ask "what is this fire trying to make of me?" and the same heat gains a direction. Rumi means a spiritual death, of course, not a hunt for suffering — more on that below.

The Spine of the Argument

Strip six books and twenty-odd thousand couplets down to the bone and the Masnavi traces a single circle:

separation (the reed cut from its bed) → not seeing (recognizing only form, groping the elephant in the dark) → being blocked (the frozen dragon of the self, the reflection in the well) → being drawn (love's astrolabe, thirst rather than water) → being transformed (the parrot's death, the chickpea's cooking, the iron in the fire) → return ("to Him we shall return").

How does it make the case? Not by argument — Rumi himself says the reasoner's leg is wooden — but by three things. First, the authority of scripture: nearly every page reworks verses of the Qur'an and sayings of the Prophet, and Rumi presents the Sufi path as an unfolding of scripture's deepest sense, not a new departure. Second, your own experience: each story is a mirror, and the argument is complete the moment you recognize yourself in the parrot, the lion or the shepherd. Third, the form itself: tales inside tales, broken off mid-plot, yanking you out just as you get absorbed — a structure that keeps breaking the spell trains you not to linger over the measure but to reach for the grain. The book doesn't hand you the truth; it keeps pushing you to the spot where you can see it for yourself. It isn't even finished: Book Six stops partway through the story of three princes and a forbidden fortress, and Rumi died — which feels like the right ending.

Common Misreadings & Criticisms

Misreading 1: Rumi is a universal love poet "beyond religion." This is the dominant Rumi in English today, largely thanks to free versions by Coleman Barks and others. Barks doesn't read Persian; he reworked earlier scholars' literal translations, stripping out much of the Islamic vocabulary and scriptural allusion. In 2017 the journalist Rozina Ali laid out the critique in The New Yorker under the title "The Erasure of Islam from the Poetry of Rumi." The fact is that the Masnavi is an Islamic text saturated with the Qur'an and the Prophet's sayings, and Rumi's "love" is first of all love of God. Peel that away and what you're left holding is the measuring cup.

Misreading 2: Sufi means ignoring the law, anything goes. Rumi is explicit in the prose preface to Book Five, roughly: the religious law (sharīʿa) is like a candle that shows the way; without the candle you can't set out; once on the way, your traveling is the path (ṭarīqa); and arriving is the truth (ḥaqīqa). He goes deeper within the law, not around it. The shepherd story says "God looks at the heart," not "form doesn't matter."

Misreading 3: most "Rumi quotes" online are his. Many widely shared lines can't be traced to any original, or are very loose reworkings of his lyric poems, or are simply invented. "Out beyond ideas of wrongdoing and rightdoing, there is a field. I'll meet you there," for instance, comes from a Barks version whose original context is quite different. A little caution before quoting is warranted.

Misreading 4: "dying first" glorifies suffering or rejects life. Rumi means the death of the small self, not of the body, and certainly not self-punishment. The chickpea is boiled to become nourishment — the goal is a fuller life, not disappearance.

Criticism 1: violence and moral trouble in the stories. In the opening tale of the king and the handmaiden, the mysterious physician cures the girl by arranging for the goldsmith to be poisoned until he wastes away and dies. Rumi defends it as done at God's command, like Khiḍr killing the boy in the Qur'an — evil on the surface, wisdom beneath that mortals can't see. That's exactly what troubles many modern readers: once you accept that apparent evil hides a higher good, it becomes very hard to hold anyone to account for harm done in the name of mystical insight.

Criticism 2: gender and the "indecent" passages. Women often serve as symbols of the self, desire and worldliness, carrying medieval prejudice; yet Rumi also wrote that woman "is a ray of God, not that earthly beloved; she is creative — you might say she is not created" (Book One, roughly), so the picture isn't uniform. Separately, Book Five and elsewhere contain several frankly bawdy sexual tales, which the British scholar R. A. Nicholson, publishing his eight-volume English translation in 1925–1940, rendered into Latin to keep them from general readers. Today's reader needn't look away: Rumi held that the coarsest story can carry the cleanest meaning — but these passages aren't for everyone.

Criticism 3: loose, repetitive, unfinished. Even admirers concede that the Masnavi sprawls in places and repeats itself, and that cut off from Persian meter and Islamic culture, translations often read as long-winded. "The structure is the teaching," as argued above, is the sympathetic reading; the unsympathetic one is that an old man dictating at will over fifteen years was never going to edit tightly.

Criticism 4: the epistemic problem of mysticism. The elephant story quietly assumes someone holds a candle and has seen the whole animal. But who has the candle, and who decides? The Sufi answer is the master (pir), to whom the disciple owes complete obedience. At best that's a lineage of teaching; at worst it's personal authority that can't be checked. Truth that rests on inner experience is hard to demonstrate to outsiders, and hard to protect from abuse.

Beyond This Book: The Rest of Rumi

Read only the Masnavi and you'd take Rumi for a calm, patient teacher. He had another, utterly different face. At thirty-seven, in Konya, he met Shams of Tabriz, a sharp-tongued, utterly unconventional wandering dervish. The two became inseparable; Rumi abandoned his teaching and students, who seethed with jealousy. Shams left once, was brought back, and around 1247/48 vanished for good — his fate remains a mystery (there are stories of murder, but no proof). That loss made Rumi a poet. He wrote some three thousand lyric poems (ghazals), collected as the Dīvān-i Shams, often signing them with Shams's name instead of his own — casting himself as merely Shams's throat. That collection is ecstasy, intoxication, whirling, love erupting without plan; the Masnavi is what came after the fire cooled, a teacher patiently translating it into stories for those who came later. There is also a prose collection of talks, Fīhi mā fīhi ("In it is what is in it"), conversational and easygoing, often commenting on the politics and daily life of the day. Put the ecstatic Dīvān and the didactic Masnavi together and you have the whole Rumi: first burned, then lighting the way for others.

Crossovers

  • Buddhism · The blind men and the elephant: In the Pali Udāna (6.4) the Buddha uses blind men groping an elephant to mock dogmatic rival teachers. Both say "part mistaken for whole"; but the Buddha's point is to drop attachment to views, Rumi's is to light a candle and see a real elephant entire.
  • Psychology · Jung's "shadow": Jung held that the parts of ourselves we refuse to own get projected onto others — the same structure as the lion lunging at its reflection. The difference: Jung wants to integrate the shadow; Rumi wants the self broken.
  • Neuroscience · "Wanting" vs. "liking": Kent Berridge and colleagues found that the dopamine system mainly drives "wanting" (incentive salience) rather than "liking," and the two can come apart — we can crave what brings no pleasure. Rumi's "seek thirst, not water" likewise treats the craving itself as the engine of direction. Unlike it: neuroscience's wanting can drag you into addiction, while Rumi's thirst must first be purified and aimed only at the source.
  • Evolution · Darwin: "I died as mineral and became plant" is often hailed as "evolution in the thirteenth century." Both sketch a ladder from simple to complex life; but Rumi's ladder is a soul's purposeful ascent, while natural selection has no direction and no destination.
  • Western philosophy · Plato's Symposium: Aristophanes says humans were once whole, round creatures, split in two by Zeus, and love is the longing to be whole again. Both make love homesickness for a lost wholeness; but his other half is another person, while Rumi's reed bed is the source of everything.

Ten Sentences to Keep

1. Listen to the reed complain of separation: whoever is far from his source seeks the time of reunion (paraphrased).

2. Your emptiness and longing aren't malfunctions but a compass — you ache because you remember home.

3. A flute must be hollowed before it can sing; fill it with "me" and all it plays is its own noise.

4. The story is the measure, the meaning is the grain; the wise take the grain and don't stare at the measure.

5. In the dark house everyone touches a real elephant; the mistake is taking a part for the whole — what's missing is a candle, not hands.

6. God looks not at the tongue and the words but at the heart and the state; you were sent to join, not to sever.

7. The self is a frozen dragon: not dead, just not yet in the sun.

8. The reasoner's leg is made of wood; love is the astrolabe that reads the mysteries above.

9. Don't seek water — get thirst; the chickpea is boiled so that it can become part of a life.

10. Die before you die: when was I ever less by dying?
