Fault Lines

How Hidden Fractures Still Threaten the World Economy · Raghuram G. Rajan · 2010

中文 →

In One Sentence

The crisis of 2008 was not produced by a few hundred greedy bankers. It was produced by deep fault lines in politics and the world economy that had been grinding for decades, and it broke where they met — in the financial sector. The worst of those lines runs like this: when incomes stop rising for the bottom half of a country, fixing schools and skills is slow and thankless while extending credit is fast and painless — so the political system chose credit. "Let them eat credit": debt used to fill a gap that only income could actually fill. The gap did not close. It was simply moved onto a bank's balance sheet.

Coordinates

Raghuram Rajan is a professor of finance at Chicago Booth, was chief economist of the IMF from 2003 to 2006, and served as Governor of the Reserve Bank of India from 2013 to 2016. He is best remembered for one afternoon: at the 2005 Jackson Hole conference, in front of a departing Alan Greenspan and a room full of central bankers, he presented a paper arguing that financial innovation had not made the world safer, and that incentives inside the industry were quietly accumulating tail risk. The room turned on him; Larry Summers called the position "slightly Luddite." This 2010 book is that warning worked out at length, and it won the FT/Goldman Sachs Business Book of the Year award.

Central Claims

  • The causes of the crisis were not inside finance; they were external pressures that converged there. Earthquakes happen at fault lines, but the energy comes from the plates. Wall Street was the fault, not the plate. Prosecuting bankers alone is standing at the crack and asking why it cracked.
  • The American plate is inequality plus a threadbare safety net. Wage stagnation is an education-and-skills problem measured in decades; electoral cycles cannot wait that long. Credit expansion makes life feel better immediately. Both parties took the shortcut, and housing finance was the main channel.
  • The other plate is export-led growth. A set of countries built growth by suppressing domestic consumption and pushing exports — and surpluses require somebody, somewhere, to run the matching deficit. Capital poured into the United States and made the already dangerous credit channel far wider.

The Core Concepts, One by One

What the title is actually a metaphor for

Pin the metaphor down first, because the book's whole explanatory structure rests on it. In geology a fault is the seam where two plates of crust meet. The plates grind against each other at a few centimetres a year, storing elastic energy while friction keeps the seam locked. When stress exceeds friction, it releases all at once — the earthquake happens at the fault, but the energy is neither generated nor stored there. It comes from decades of plate motion.

That is Rajan's claim about finance. The financial sector is the seam; it genuinely did rupture, and its own distorted incentives made it more brittle. But explaining 2008 as "finance broke" is like explaining an earthquake by saying the rock was weak. The practical value of the metaphor is that it moves the question up a level: not who did wrong, but what force has been pushing for decades while everyone pushing felt entirely justified at every step. "Let them eat credit": why politics reaches for debt

This is the sharpest line in the book. It plays on "let them eat cake," but inverts the meaning: this is not aristocratic obliviousness. It is shrewd political arithmetic.

The mechanism runs as follows. Median American wages grew feebly for decades; Rajan's primary explanation is that the supply of skills failed to keep up with technology's demand for them — the education system, especially at the school level, stopped moving enough people into high-skill work, and the gap on either side of a college degree kept widening. Fixing that means fixing schools, community colleges and vocational training: a project with a twenty-year payback and no credit available to whoever starts it. But another road exists. Let people who cannot afford a house borrow enough to buy one. As prices rise they live better and look richer on paper. Nobody has to be taxed, and nobody has to be told out loud that their wages are never going up again.

The policy was concrete: raising the home-ownership rate became a bipartisan achievement to campaign on; the housing agencies Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were pushed toward ever more aggressive affordable-housing targets; regulators were remarkably relaxed about the expansion of subprime lending. Rajan's point is not that a villain conspired, but that a democracy facing a slow cure and a fast painkiller will predictably reach for the painkiller.

How it changes the way you read things: the next time you see household leverage climbing fast anywhere, do not read it as a financial statistic. An abnormal credit expansion is usually the substitute for a distributional problem nobody wants to name. Asking "what here should have been rising and wasn't?" will usually get you closer to the truth than studying the loan book.

Jobless recoveries, thin safety nets, and a central bank doing social policy

This is the amplifier on the previous fault line, and the most underrated section in the book. A jobless recovery is one where output has resumed growing but employment does not come back. Rajan points to a clear shift in the American pattern: in the early postwar recessions, jobs returned within months of the output trough, whereas after the 1991 and 2001 recessions it took roughly two and nearly three years respectively for employment to regain its pre-recession level.

Why does that matter so much? Because the American safety net is built on the assumption that unemployment is brief: benefits run out quickly and health insurance is attached to a job. Once joblessness lasts two or three years, the net fails to catch people and the political pain becomes unbearable. All of that pressure then lands on the one institution capable of reacting fast: the Federal Reserve.

The result is sustained ultra-low rates, and an expectation built on top of them — the Greenspan put. A put option pays you when an asset falls; the phrase means that investors came to believe the Fed would never deliberately deflate a bubble but would always flood the system after a crash, which amounts to free downside insurance. If the downside is socialised and the upside is yours, leverage is the rational choice. Notice how counter-intuitive the chain is: a country's unemployment insurance being too thin ends up expressing itself as its asset bubbles being too large.

Export-led growth: why one country's success requires another's debt

The second plate lies outside America. Germany, Japan, and later much of East Asia including China, all travelled the same road: hold down domestic consumption — through high savings, weak social insurance, managed exchange rates and restrained wages — and aim capacity at foreign markets. During a catch-up phase this works extremely well, concentrating resources in tradable sectors and routing around a small domestic market.

But it has an accounting consequence nobody can escape: current account balances must sum to zero across the world. If someone sells more than they buy, someone else must buy more than they sell. The Asian crisis of 1997–98 magnified this. Emerging markets that had borrowed abroad were brutally punished, and afterwards they collectively changed strategy: stop being net borrowers, accumulate foreign reserves as self-insurance instead. The result was a strange spectacle — poor countries exporting capital to rich ones.

That money needed somewhere to go. Much of it bought US Treasuries and dollar assets, pushing down American long rates — in the one country that, for domestic political reasons, badly wanted cheap credit to keep voters content. The two plates met the seam precisely: one side desperate to borrow and spend, the other desperate to lend. America became the consumer of last resort. Rajan's insistence is that this is neither unilateral American profligacy nor unilateral surplus-country dumping, but two domestic political predicaments feeding each other across an ocean.

Arm's-length versus relationship-based: what happens when the two short-circuit

The third fault line is the most technical and the easiest to skip, but it explains why the money, once it arrived, blew up the way it did.

An arm's-length financial system — the American and British model — has strangers transacting through public information, standardised contracts, price signals, courts and ratings. Capital can move quickly to new opportunities and abandon failures just as quickly. A relationship-based system — postwar Japan, Germany, most of East Asia — runs on long-term bonds between banks and firms, on inside information and implicit commitments (the Japanese main bank, the German Hausbank). It is very good at mobilising patient capital during catch-up growth, bad at admitting failure, and systematically suppresses both depositor returns and household consumption.

The trouble arrives at the junction. The enormous savings generated by relationship-based systems entered an arm's-length one carried by people without the capabilities that system assumes. They were used to someone watching on their behalf; this system's premise is that everyone watches for themselves. So a role quietly went vacant: foreign central banks and institutions bought American assets trusting the AAA label; the rating agencies' models rested on historical data in which house prices had never fallen nationwide at once; the investment banks structuring the securities needed only for them to sell. Every link in the chain assumed the risk was being watched by the link above or below. No link was watching.

Tail risk and pay: picking up nickels in front of a steamroller

This was the core of the 2005 Jackson Hole paper, and it is Rajan's mechanism for how clever people collectively walk into something stupid.

Tail risk means a very low probability of a catastrophic loss. The crucial property is this: before the tail event arrives, a strategy loaded with tail risk is indistinguishable from genuine skill. Hence the image of picking up nickels in front of a steamroller — you collect money every single day until the day you fail to step aside. The sharper analogy is writing earthquake insurance: you book the premiums as profit year after year, your returns look steady and enviable, and then in one year you pay back twenty years of profits with interest.

Layer the pay structure on top and it becomes lethal. Bonuses settle annually; tail events arrive once a decade or less. The bonus has been banked long before the loss lands on the firm — and on the taxpayer. So the individually optimal move is to take on as much currently invisible risk as possible. Citigroup's Chuck Prince in 2007, in the most quoted sentence of the era (paraphrased) — as long as the music is playing, you have to get up and dance — was not being arrogant. He was describing the incentive structure accurately: if you sit out, you underperform your peers and you are replaced before the music stops.

How it changes the way you read things: any unusually smooth, low-volatility, above-peer return deserves one question — is this alpha, or is it an insurance premium in disguise? And this is not only about finance. Any organisation that judges long-horizon risk by short-horizon metrics — hospitals, airlines, engineering firms, content platforms — is mass-producing the same error.

What he prescribes, and what he doesn't

The prescriptions follow the diagnosis: if the disease is not in finance, reforming finance alone cannot cure it. His order of priority is human capital first — early childhood education, community colleges, affordable retraining — because it is the only thing that genuinely raises wages. Then rebuild the safety net in forms that do not punish work: detach health coverage from the employer, prefer wage insurance to simply extending unemployment benefits. Internationally, push surplus countries toward domestic demand. Within finance, his emphasis falls not on more rules but on fixing incentives: deferred bonuses, clawbacks, and debt that converts to equity in a crisis, which dismantles the implicit insurance policy.

What he leaves out should be said just as plainly. The book pins wage stagnation mainly on an inadequate supply of skills, and gives comparatively little space to the alternatives: the collapse of labour's bargaining power, rising market concentration, and the geographically concentrated damage of trade shocks. That tilts the prescription to the supply side — the people who fell behind are asked to go back to school, rather than the rules of distribution being changed. It is the book's most durable controversy, and the next section takes it seriously.

The Argument in Outline

Compressed to a single line: pressure → political shortcut → capital inflow → distorted incentives → rupture.

Inside America, widening income gaps and a thin safety net produced a politically intolerable kind of pain. The real cure — education and skills — was too slow, so credit expansion was chosen as the painkiller, with housing finance as the delivery mechanism. Meanwhile a group of countries staking growth on exports needed someone to consume their output, and after the Asian crisis they turned collectively into net exporters of capital; that ocean of savings flowed into the United States, pushed rates down and widened the credit channel further. The money then passed through an arm's-length system it did not understand, whose internal pay structures were rewarding precisely those who hid tail risk, with an implicit government guarantee absorbing their downside. Three forces stacked on one seam. The moment house prices stopped rising, the seam opened.

So what the book establishes is a single proposition: a financial crisis is the financial symptom of a political-economic disease. Its method is to run three independently evidenced causal chains — inequality and politics, global imbalances, incentives and tail risk — into the same endpoint. That is the insight, and it is also the exposure: joining them elegantly does not make every segment equally strong.

Misreadings and Serious Criticism

Misreading 1: "It's a book attacking bankers." The opposite — it explicitly refuses the villain story. Blaming greed reduces a structural problem to a moral one, and the practical result is that you replace some people, leave the incentives untouched, and wait for the next time.

Misreading 2: "Fault lines means flaws in the financial system." That inverts the metaphor. The fault is where the break happens, not where the energy comes from, and the book's entire effort goes into arguing about the plates.

Misreading 3: "He predicted the subprime crisis." Be precise. His 2005 warning was that incentives inside finance were accumulating tail risk, and directionally it was uncannily right — but that is not the same as predicting when or how house prices would collapse. Calling it a prophecy both overstates the paper and undersells what it actually did, which was identify a structure rather than a date.

The criticism that lands:

  • The role of Fannie, Freddie and federal housing policy is genuinely unsettled — and it is a load-bearing part of the first fault line. The majority report of the Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission (2011) concluded that the Community Reinvestment Act and the agencies' affordable-housing goals were not primary causes; Commissioner Wallison's dissent argued the reverse, and Rajan sits nearer the dissent. The strongest evidence against him is that during 2004–2006, the worst vintage years, the agencies were losing large market share to private-label securitisation, and the very worst loans came disproportionately from independent mortgage companies never covered by the CRA at all. If that link fails, a large part of the "politicians bought votes with credit" chain goes slack. Paul Krugman and others attacked him publicly on exactly this point.
  • The inequality-causes-credit-boom mechanism has weak empirical support. Subsequent work has tested it directly: long-run cross-country data has not produced a robust relationship in which rising inequality reliably predicts credit booms, and US micro data has found that in areas where inequality rose fastest, low-income households did not in fact borrow more. That does not make the story wrong, but it means this fault line remains a persuasive narrative rather than a settled finding.
  • Besides a "savings glut" there was a "banking glut." Rajan's international fault line uses Bernanke's savings-glut frame, which looks at net capital flows between countries. Hyun Song Shin and others later argued that what really inflated US money markets was the gross flow of large European banks borrowing short and buying long in dollars — vast in scale but almost invisible in net terms because it cancelled out. To the extent that correction holds, Asia's share of the blame has to be marked down.
  • The prescriptions lean to the supply side. Attributing wage stagnation mainly to a skills deficit implies that the answer is sending workers back to school. Critics, particularly on the left, note that this framing has long been used to avoid the distributional question — and the evidence since has strengthened the other side: David Autor and co-authors' work on the "China shock" shows trade-related job losses that were geographically concentrated and remarkably persistent, far beyond what retraining could offset. Skills matter; they may be half the fracture.
  • Time has graded him both ways. To his credit, the decade after 2008 closely fitted his description: rates stayed low for years, asset prices decoupled further from wages, and populism returned. Against him, the reckoning with inflation and debt that he expected did not arrive in the 2010s in the form he imagined, and monetary easing ran far longer than he anticipated. His reading of the pressure has proved much more reliable than his reading of the timing — which is, in fairness, exactly what his own theory of fault lines predicts: you can know that stress is accumulating, and not know which day it breaks.
