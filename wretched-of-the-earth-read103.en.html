DEEP READ · 103

The Wretched of the Earth

Les Damnés de la Terre · Frantz Fanon · 1961

中文 →

In one sentence

A psychiatrist spent three years running a ward in a colony, saw enough gunshot wounds, torture sequelae and mute peasants, and then wrote down a diagnosis no clinician is supposed to write: these people are ill, but the pathogen is not in their heads — it is in the order they are standing on, and that order was put up by force, so it will move aside only for greater force. The first third of the book became the most incendiary political prose of the twentieth century: it claims that at the moment the colonized man strikes, the inferiority goes out of his body. But the last chapter of the same book is his own case file — the men who struck, including his own patients who had killed, came back to his consulting room one after another.

Where it sits

Fanon was born in 1925 in Martinique, a French colony in the Caribbean; he fought with the Free French in the Second World War, then studied medicine in Lyon and switched to psychiatry. In 1953 he became head of service at the Blida-Joinville psychiatric hospital in Algeria; the war broke out the following year, and in 1956 he resigned and joined the FLN (Front de Libération Nationale). Leukemia was diagnosed in 1960; he dictated this book in roughly ten weeks in 1961 and died in December of that year in the United States, aged 36, seven months before Algerian independence. Where Said (read 102) traced how colonialism became a body of knowledge, Fanon traced how it gets into a body — and what it would take to pull it out again. The book is simultaneously a programme for Third World revolution, a founding document of postcolonial thought, and a clinical report.

The core claims

1. Colonialism is not a harsher-than-usual form of government; it is violence in its ordinary working state. Decolonization therefore comes with violence — not because the colonized are savage, but because the house was built with guns from the foundations up, and will not be asked politely to leave.

2. Driving the settler out is only the first move; the real danger comes after independence. A "national bourgeoisie" grows up in the colony that has no industry, no inventors, no capital of its own: it simply takes over the machine the colonizer left and goes into business as a middleman for foreign capital. The flag changes, the machine does not, and single-party rule, the cult of the leader and the hunting-out of foreigners follow. That chapter was written in 1961, and the following decades largely performed it.

3. The end of liberation is not "our turn to be the masters." What Fanon rejects on the last page of the book is imitation: he wants a new human being that Europe has talked about endlessly and never managed to produce. The same man wrote that page and the violence chapter — and most readers remember only one of them.

The key ideas, one by one

1. A world cut in two: why a colony has no middle ground

The book opens with a description of space. The colonial world, Fanon says, is a world cut in two (un monde compartimenté — literally a world divided into compartments), and the line between the halves is not income. It is the barracks and the police station.

The settlers' townThe colonized town
What it looks likeStone and steel, paved streets, working lamps, refuse collectedMud and corrugated iron, crammed together, unlit, no spare room
BodiesFed, unhurried, no need to look behindHungry, crouching, always ready to run
What the other side isA policing problem, a population to be managedNot another class — another species
What settles thingsLaw, property, the name of civilizationNot complying means not existing

Schematic: Fanon's two towns are not two ends of an income distribution but two worlds that do not interpenetrate.

The important row in that table is the one that is missing. In the bourgeois societies of Europe, Fanon notes, a thick layer of padding sits between ruler and ruled: schools, churches, manners, the whole aestheticized respect for the status quo, which spares power from having to appear in person. The colony strips that padding away — what stands at the point of contact is the soldier and the policeman. Which is why the colonized experience "order" quite differently from a European worker: the worker knows his boss exploits him, but he also knows there are courts, unions and Sundays. What the colonized knows is simply that crossing the line gets you beaten.

Here Fanon gives Marx a push. The standard model has an economic base determining a superstructure of law, religion and ideas. In the colonies, he says, the analysis has to be stretched slightly: here the economic base is also the superstructure, because what decides which side of the line you stand on is skin, which is visible at a glance. He compresses this into one of the most quoted lines in the book (paraphrased): "You are rich because you are white; you are white because you are rich." Race is not decoration on top of the economics here. It is the economic boundary.

How it changes the way you see things: put a Fanonian question to any social conflict — how many layers of padding are left between those who rule and those who are ruled? With courts, press and a table to sit at, conflict shows up as bargaining. Strip the padding away until only guns and people facing guns remain, and conflict eventually shows up in another form, regardless of anyone's moral quality. This is also the only honest entrance to the violence argument: Fanon is not recommending violence; he is saying that this structure has left only one language in use.

2. Muscular tension: where the energy goes first

Before the violence argument comes a clinical observation, and that observation is the engine of everything else. The colonized, Fanon says, lives in permanent muscular tension (tension musculaire) — held down, watched, pre-tamed by the slap that could land at any moment, with a charge in the body and nowhere to send it.

So where does it go? His answer is uncomfortably sharp: not at the settler first, but at his own people. Homicide in the colony occurs overwhelmingly among the colonized — over a word, an animal, a strip of land, with feuds flaring again and again. To Fanon this is not a hot temperament but energy forbidden from reaching its real target, finding the one outlet that is permitted — and Europeans then use the statistics to prove that these people are savages, so the loop supplies the regime with a justification as well. The second outlet is religion and dance: the all-night drums, the spinning, the moment of possession. Anthropologists filed these rituals under folklore; Fanon reads them as a precision-built pressure valve — the community discharges its accumulated aggression on schedule, everyone is calm the next morning, and not a hair on the ruler's head is touched. The third outlet is sleep, and it produces his most beautiful line: the dreams of the colonized are muscular dreams — he dreams that he is jumping, swimming, running, climbing. (paraphrased) The body forbidden to move by day completes its motion at night.

How it changes the way you see things: when a group is visibly living badly and almost all of its anger lands on itself, resist the character label and go looking for the line that says "not upwards." Fanon gives lateral self-destruction a structural explanation rather than a moral one — a move borrowed later by everyone from Steve Biko's Black Consciousness movement to the Black Panthers.

3. Violence: what "detoxification" actually meant, and where it fails

Now the famous claim can be handled. The chain runs: the colonial order was established by violence and is maintained daily by violence → the colonized therefore carries that muscular charge → ordinarily it discharges onto his own people or into the dance, confirming his inferiority each time → only when it is aimed, once, at its real object does the man stand up out of the belief that he was born beneath. Fanon's own formulation (paraphrased): violence is a cleansing force; it frees the colonized from his inferiority complex, from despair and inaction, makes him fearless and restores his self-respect. The French is more medical than the English: une praxis désintoxicante, a detoxifying practice. He is not saying killing feels good. He is saying the poison is already in the body and has to come out.

One qualification is almost always dropped: the act has to be performed by the colonized themselves. Independence granted at a negotiating table leaves the inferiority exactly where it was, because the people never took part in the act that freed them — so after independence they go on waiting to be told. What he wants is not bloodshed; it is the fact of having done it oneself.

So where does it fail? First, this is a clinical proposition offered without clinical evidence — by Fanon's own professional standards, "violence cures inferiority" is a claim that needs case notes. Second, and more damaging: the one chapter in the book that does supply case notes (chapter five) supplies evidence against it — FLN fighters with insomnia, auditory hallucinations and tremors after a shooting; a man who could no longer touch his wife after killing. Chapter one says violence heals, chapter five says violence sickens, and both came from the same hand in the same year. This reads less like an oversight than a seam he did not live long enough to close — he was dictating the book while dying. The honest way to read it is to leave the seam visible rather than carry off one half.

4. Who makes the revolution: peasants, the lumpenproletariat, and his break with Marx

Orthodox Marxism puts its hopes in the industrial working class. What Fanon sees in a colony is the opposite picture: the colonial proletariat is the best-looked-after fraction of the colonized — tram drivers, miners, nurses, clerks, interpreters. They have wages, uniforms, and something to lose. In his reading they are therefore the people most interested in better treatment within the existing system, and least interested in overturning it.

He places his bets in two other places. First, the peasantry: the most numerous, the most completely dispossessed, and holding no position that can be bought. Second, the lumpenproletariat — Marx's word for the vagrants, bankrupts, thieves and prostitutes who have fallen out of productive relations altogether, and whom Marx treated as a reserve army for reaction. Fanon inverts this: in a colony, these people, driven off the land and heaped into shanty towns on the edge of the city, are the urban spearhead — hungriest, least attached, first to move.

And then the turn that quoters almost always cut: Fanon immediately warns that the same group is the easiest to buy. A little money, a uniform, a scrap of authority, and they become informers, militiamen and auxiliaries; the colonial bourgeoisie, he writes, finds considerable room for manoeuvre in the lumpenproletariat (paraphrased). His actual position is therefore not that the very poor are natural revolutionaries, but that this force has to be organized and educated, or it will turn up facing you.

How it changes the way you see things: Fanon is refusing to paste a class map drawn in Europe onto Africa unaltered. What decides which side someone takes is usually not their label but whether the current arrangement has given them a position they are afraid to lose. That test travels better than "worker or peasant," and it still works.

5. Spontaneity, strong and weak: why risings start by themselves and collapse by themselves

The second chapter is titled "Spontaneity: Its Strength and Weakness." Fanon grants that risings are often spontaneous — one arrest, one volley, nobody organizing, and the fire crosses the country. But his conclusion is that spontaneity runs out. The first wave runs on anger, and anger has a shelf life, while the colonial army learns this kind of war fast: sectors, blockades, bounties, sponsored moderates. The rising moves into attrition, and attrition needs exactly what spontaneity cannot supply — supply lines, discipline, long objectives, and knowing why you are still fighting. More dangerous still: once the population is merely a crowd that follows, the leadership floats off above their heads.

He prescribes one thing: political education — defined in a strikingly unbureaucratic way: "to educate politically means to open their minds, awaken them, and allow the birth of their intelligence." (paraphrased) It comes with decentralization: not everything should issue from the capital; every region needs its own assembly, its own judgement, and the capacity to say no. How it changes the way you see things: anything that starts on a surge of feeling — a movement, a company, a reform — reaches the point where the feeling is spent, and whether it survives depends on whether, while the feeling lasted, the "why" was turned into something each person can state themselves.

6. The pitfalls of national consciousness: a prediction written in 1961

The third chapter is the coolest and the most prophetic in the book; its title is better rendered as "The Misadventures of National Consciousness." At the high point of the independence wave, Fanon pours cold water: after independence the country will most likely fall to a stratum called the national bourgeoisie, and that stratum is barely anything at all.

The dissection is exact. Whatever else is to be said against the European bourgeoisie, it did things: it ran an industrial revolution, built factories, accumulated capital. The colonial "national bourgeoisie" has no industry, no technology and no accumulated capital; it is made of lawyers, doctors, small traders, contractors and former colonial officials, and its one competence is intermediation. Independence therefore brings not structural change but a transfer of title: the plantations, mines and import agencies formerly run by metropolitan companies are now run by locals, while what is produced, who buys it and who sets the price stay exactly as they were. Fanon's verdict (paraphrased): it is content to be the transmission line between the nation and a capitalism that is rampant though camouflaged.

What follows reads like a timetable. With no real economic programme, the ruling party can only hold people together with something else, so nationalism is turned up. Nationalism that is not deepened into social content degenerates into chauvinism and then into racism: minorities are found at home, and "foreigners" from the next country are driven out — his examples are the expulsions and assaults aimed at Senegalese and Dahomeyans in newly independent West Africa. Meanwhile the party stops being an instrument of mobilization and becomes a network for distributing offices, and the leader becomes — his phrase is poisonous — the general president of that company of profiteers (paraphrased). After that come the single party, the army, and the habit of calling any criticism an attack on unity.

His summary is one of the most cited sentences in the book (paraphrased): national consciousness, if it is not deepened into genuine social and humanist content, is only an empty shell, a crude and fragile travesty of what it might have been.

How it changes the way you see things: this chapter separates changing the people from changing the structure. To judge whether a transformation actually happened, don't look at who is sitting in the chair; look at whether the line marking who decides what has moved. Hold that ruler up to any change of regime, any restructuring, any reorganization, and a whole class of name-changes becomes visible at once. The sharpest case is Algeria itself, which went on to tick off this chapter almost item by item.

7. National culture: not dug up, but made in the doing

The fourth chapter takes on a question that sounds genteel and is not: a colonized nation is to "recover its culture" — recover what? Fanon offers a three-stage model, and what he is really writing is an intellectual's autobiography. He went through the first two himself.

StageWhat he is doingFanon's verdict
1. AssimilationProving he has absorbed the occupier's culture: their language, their forms, their standardsThe highest achievement available is being called almost one of us
2. Turning backHe goes looking for his own past, but he is already an outsider, so what he produces is nostalgia, exoticism, tradition done up as a specimenA mummified past — an affair with a corpse
3. FightingHe acts alongside the people and writes inside the action: an awakener rather than an observerThis is where a national culture is actually born

Not a periodization of literary history — three situations the same person can pass through.

The weight is in the third definition. A national culture, Fanon says, is not folklore and not an abstract national soul; it is "the whole body of efforts made by a people in the sphere of thought to describe, justify and praise the action through which that people has created itself and keeps itself in existence" (paraphrased). Unpacked: first there is something done together, and then there is culture; culture is that act echoing in thought, not a stock item that can be withdrawn in advance from a museum.

This is also why he criticizes Négritude in this chapter — the movement of Black cultural self-assertion led by Aimé Césaire and Léopold Senghor; Césaire had been Fanon's schoolteacher in Martinique. His objection is not temperamental: "Black culture" is a racial category, not a national one. It groups people from Harlem to Dakar to Kingston by skin, though the machinery ruling each of them differs; worse, it was composed as an answer to white racism, so the coordinate system is still the other side's — you spend your strength proving that black is beautiful, and the motion was tabled by someone else. His coldest observation on this: the Black man has never been so thoroughly a Black man as since he was dominated by whites. (paraphrased)

The chapter also carries his single most quoted line (paraphrased): each generation must, out of relative obscurity, discover its mission, and then fulfil it or betray it.

8. Colonial war and mental disorder: the evidence from the clinic

The last chapter is the other half of the book's weight and the least like a political document: a stack of case notes from Blida and from wartime practice, grouped and set down without ornament.

Several are unforgettable. An Algerian man becomes impotent after French soldiers rape his wife. Two Algerian boys of thirteen and fourteen kill a European playmate and, asked why, answer that nobody asks why when grown men kill our people. And the one most often retold: a French police inspector comes to Fanon as a patient — he tortures in the interrogation room and then beats his wife and children at home without being able to stop, and he asks Fanon to cure him so that he can go on interrogating in peace. Fanon records the request and adds nothing. It makes his case better than any argument: the machine damages the people at both ends of it.

The chapter also settles a scholarly account. French North African psychiatry was then dominated by the Algiers School around Antoine Porot, whose doctrine held that the North African's cerebral cortex was underdeveloped, that he was a born impulsive criminal, hence the homicide rates, hence the unsuitability for psychotherapy. This is racism in a white coat: it recasts damage produced by colonization as an innate property of the colonized, so that the damage then proves colonization necessary. Fanon takes it apart from inside the profession: the "symptoms" are unstable once the colonial situation is removed, and what was called nature turns out to be circumstance. How a discipline can serve power while feeling entirely within its competence — that is why this settling of accounts is still worth reading.

The argument in outline

There is only one line in the book, and the six chapters are its six paragraphs.

One, the diagnosis of the situation. Colonialism is not an administrative arrangement but a world cut in two, held by force, with no mediation; in it, economics and race are the same line. Two, the diagnosis of the person. That structure leaves specific damage: muscular tension, aggression turned sideways onto one's own, discharge through ritual, and a settled belief in one's own inferiority. Three, the contested inference. If the damage comes from a forbidden act, only that act can undo it, and the colonized must perform it themselves — that is the whole of "violence as detoxification," and the weakest block in the building.

Four, the cold water. Spontaneity runs out, so political education and decentralization are required; and the revolutionary subject is not the cosseted colonial worker but the peasantry and the lumpenproletariat — who can also be bought at any moment.

Five, the prediction. After independence a middleman bourgeoisie takes over without transforming anything; nationalism curdles into xenophobia; the party becomes a spoils network and the leader a managing director. National consciousness that is not deepened is an empty shell.

Six, the close. Culture is made, not excavated; and the aim is not to occupy Europe's position but to stop building countries in Europe's image. So the real shape of the book is this: the first third argues that one must act, and the remaining two thirds argue that acting is the easy part. Anyone who reads only the first third has read a different book.

Misreadings & criticisms

Misreading 1: "Fanon, apostle of violence." Half of that label belongs to Sartre. The 1961 edition carried a preface by Jean-Paul Sartre pitched considerably higher than anything Fanon wrote — the famous line about shooting a European killing two birds with one stone, destroying an oppressor and the man he oppresses, leaving a dead man and a free man (paraphrased), is Sartre's, not Fanon's. For decades readers met the preface first, and many met only the preface. Hannah Arendt made exactly this point in On Violence: Sartre's praise of violence is far less qualified than Fanon's. A coda worth knowing: after Sartre's statements on the 1967 war in the Middle East, Fanon's widow Josie Fanon had the preface removed from later editions.

Misreading 2: taking "violence heals" as an established psychological finding. There is no evidence for it, and the book's own fifth chapter supplies the counter-evidence. Circulating it as a citable clinical proposition is the most serious misuse this book suffers.

Criticism 1: Arendt's conceptual objection. In On Violence she separates power from violence: power is the human ability to act in concert; violence is instrumental. Her conclusion is that violence can destroy power but can never create it — a gun can silence people, it cannot make them agree. Founding a new political community on violence is, for her, a conceptual dead end.

Criticism 2: the empirical objection, supplied by Algeria. After 1962 came army dominance, a single party, oil revenue captured by a stratum that had taken over the colonial economy, then the riots of 1988 and the civil war of the 1990s. That is simultaneously a refutation of chapter one and a confirmation of chapter three: no new man was born in the violence, while the middleman class Fanon himself predicted arrived on schedule. It is also worth saying that the chapter is not about Algeria in particular — which is why it keeps getting applied somewhere new.

Criticism 3: the peasantry is romanticized. Historians of the Algerian war generally note that the FLN leadership was substantially urban and French-educated, and that rural mobilization was far from uniformly voluntary — villages were caught between the French army and the FLN, and both took men and grain. Fanon himself, after Blida, worked mainly on a newspaper in Tunis and as a representative in Accra; he writes about peasants with an outsider's idealization.

Criticism 4: gender is almost absent. The new man in this book is literally a man. His discussion of women and liberation sits mostly in "Algeria Unveiled" in A Dying Colonialism, and that essay has itself been criticized by feminist scholars: the veil becomes a tactical terrain, women's bodies become equipment for the struggle, and their own claims are deferred to after independence — where, mostly, the bill went unpaid.

Criticism 5: the binary is too clean. His world is Manichean — good and evil split absolutely, with no gradient — yet colonial societies were crowded with intermediaries: interpreters, chiefs, policemen, the educated évolués, informers. Fanon describes them, but never lets them disturb the two-part map.

Fanon beyond this book

Read only this one and you get the revolutionary Fanon, and miss the earlier, more interior one.

Black Skin, White Masks (Peau noire, masques blancs, 1952) was written at 27 and is about what colonialism does inside a single person. The central scene is one sentence long: a white child points at him in the street and calls out, "Look, a Negro!" At that moment, Fanon says, the corporeal schema — the unconscious sense of where one's body is, what it can do and where it ends — comes apart, and in its place arrives an epidermal one, pasted on from outside and assembled out of the other person's fears and stories. He is compressed from a man walking down a street into a Black man, and he cannot decline the compression, because it is happening in someone else's eyes. He coined a word for it: the epidermalization of inferiority — an inequality produced by social structure, contracted into the skin and reissued as a property of the person. The book also contains his reply to Octave Mannoni's "dependency complex," which held that Malagasy people carried an innate psychological need to be ruled and that colonization merely met it. Fanon's answer: dependency is not what the colonized brought into the relation; it is what the relation produced — the same move he would later make against the Algiers School.

And one personal grievance worth knowing. In Black Orpheus Sartre described Négritude as a minor moment in a dialectic, destined to be superseded by a more universal liberation. Fanon's reaction is a rare loss of composure: he needed, he writes (paraphrased), to lose himself in blackness once, and by informing him that this was merely a transitional stage Sartre had robbed him of his last chance. That defeat turns up later as the critique of Négritude in this book. Taken together: the early Fanon asks what I am in other people's eyes; the late Fanon asks what we should do together — and the passage between them is the few years he spent walking from the consulting room into a war.

Ten sentences

1. The colonial world is a world cut in two, and on the dividing line there is no school and no church — what stands there is the soldier and the policeman.

2. "You are rich because you are white; you are white because you are rich." Which is why, under colonialism, the economic base is also the superstructure.

3. The colonized man's muscles are permanently tensed, and the charge goes first not at the settler but at his own people — infighting is often a by-product of the rule against hitting upwards, not a defect of character.

4. His dreams are muscular dreams: he dreams he is jumping, running, climbing. The body forbidden to move by day finishes the movement at night.

5. "Decolonization is always a violent phenomenon" — not because the colonized are savage, but because the house was built with guns from the foundations up.

6. He calls violence a detoxifying practice that drives the inferiority out of the body; and the last chapter of the same book is a file of case notes on the men who struck and came back to his clinic. The seam is Fanon's own, and should not be smoothed over for him.

7. Spontaneity runs out, so there has to be political education — which is not propaganda but "opening their minds, awakening them, allowing the birth of their intelligence."

8. National consciousness that is not deepened into social content is an empty shell, a fragile travesty; the stratum that inherits it produces nothing and merely transmits between the nation and capital, while the leader becomes managing director of the company of profiteers.

9. National culture is not folklore excavated from the ground but a people's whole effort in thought to describe the act by which it made itself; "each generation must, out of relative obscurity, discover its mission, and then fulfil it or betray it."

10. What the final page rejects is imitation: "let us not pay tribute to Europe by creating states, institutions and societies which draw their inspiration from her" — for Europe, for ourselves and for humanity, we must turn over a new leaf, work out new concepts, and try to set afoot a new man. The man who wrote that did not live the seven months to his country's independence.
